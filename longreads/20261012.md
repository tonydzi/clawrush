## What went out today

### My synthetic co-founder told me who to sell AI consulting to

You remember that I want to get a job somewhere, but I am still thinking that maybe it is worth being a classic startup guy and doing AI consulting. I succeeded in building my second brain, all my AIs can do a great deal of the work, and I even managed to create a digital co-founder.

So here is what my co-founder said when I asked him what one can make money on. He recommended SMB consulting.

He says the very first companies to sell AI consulting to are US accounting and tax firms with 8 to 50 people. Supposedly they have margins of 50 to 60%, and they will then pull their own SMB clients along with them. Second in line, HVAC: I have never heard that abbreviation, but it is plumbers and electricians. And after that, dentists. Pizzerias are not worth selling to, their margin is small, whereas those guys above have margins of 50% and up.

So: an audit, three grand over 5 days; then building some workflow for them over 10 to 20 days; and then 3 to 5 months of support. And owners of firms with $0 to 100 million in annual revenue close in 2 to 6 weeks.

I remember my experience warming up Chinese and Japanese B2B clients, where you warm them up for a year. In B2B the deal sizes were different, and here in America the deals are faster.

It is good that I have a crm, but of course I do not have leads like that: no plumbers, no accountants, no electricians. Then I want to hear your opinion, colleagues. Who has experience selling and automating for these audiences?

### Red Team, and the four parties that have to agree before an agent ships

I basically knew this term existed, but only yesterday did I actually listen to it. I thought there was Black Hat and White Hat: White Hat does pentests and breaks things kindly, Black Hat breaks them with bad intent. And it turns out there is also Red Team. Meaning I have been doing security, but never really knew what it is called.

Mycroft and I have been walking around GitHub repositories for a long time, analysing various agents. I am familiar with pentests because smart contract audits need it, and there is a lot of maths there. But with agents there is no maths at all. It is purely psychology and logic: a smart contract auditor thinks in numbers, and an agent thinks like a person who was handed an instruction.

So right now everyone is a pentester, and what comes next? Everyone has to quickly re-shoe themselves into being a security person for agents. Soon there will be an interview for a Red Team person, and I hope I do not fail it too badly, because the experience is there.

The interesting part: the existence of companies doing this lets the industry adopt agents faster. An airline can only take an agent on if its planes do not fall and its customers do not end up deceived; in America a company can be sued if an agent in support promised something. So the agent has to be audited, and the audit has to be performed by a company that builds a whole system of trust around the badge, negotiating with other parties so the badge is recognised and the airline's insurance does not lapse.

So there are several parties: the business that wants an agent and is afraid of it; the security company issuing the badge; the insurer, which does not mind the premium but needs a standard built collaboratively; and the integrator who resells agents and carries the badge. And possibly several insurers, one for the integrator and one for the airline that will not lose a pile of money if the agent made a decision about the glide path and the plane went the wrong way.

## How it connects

Two posts on this date, and they are the same business question answered from two different ends: who pays, and who removes the reason not to buy.

The first one is the other half of a question that has been running for a week.

Every recent post on this subject has been about getting hired: four doors into one company, which surface to apply through, what to say about work that is under NDA. This one puts the alternative on the table in the same voice, and keeps both options open rather than resolving them. The line "you remember that I want to get a job somewhere, but" is the whole structure of the week in eight words.

The part worth taking out of it is the ordering rule, and it is not about accountants. **The first segment to sell into is the one whose clients are your next clients.** Accounting and tax firms are not recommended here because they are the easiest sale or the highest margin on their own; they are recommended because every one of them sits on a book of small businesses and drags that book along behind it. That turns a first customer into a distribution channel, and it is the only property on the list that compounds.

Everything else in the post is a consequence of that rule. HVAC and dentists have margin but no book to pull. Pizzerias fail on margin. The offer ladder, audit then build then retainer, is the standard shape and is the least interesting part.

And the honest gap is named at the end rather than hidden: there is a CRM and there are no leads of this kind in it. A target list without a list of names is a hypothesis about a market, not a pipeline.

The second post describes a different kind of market shape, and the structural observation in it is the stronger of the two. An audit badge is usually read as a quality signal. It is not; **it is a mechanism for moving liability**. The business is not afraid the agent will be bad, it is afraid of being sued for what the agent says, which is a legal exposure rather than a technical one. That is why the chain needs four parties and not two: the badge only does work if an insurer accepts it as a condition of cover, and the insurer only accepts it if the standard behind it was built collaboratively rather than sold. A badge nobody underwrites is a logo.

Which makes the audit company's real product the recognition agreements, not the test. The test is the part engineers find interesting; the negotiation with insurers and integrators is the part that decides whether anyone pays for the test.

Worth keeping the craft distinction from the same post as well, because it travels: a smart contract audit is maths, and an agent audit has no maths in it. The target reasons in natural language from an instruction, so the attack surface is psychology and logic. Those two jobs share a name and almost no method, and anybody re-shoeing from one to the other should expect to relearn the technique rather than port it.

## The numbers

Two posts on this date. Every figure in the first comes from one conversation with a model, and is reported as that rather than as research.

Stated: firms of 8 to 50 people; margins of 50 to 60% for accounting and tax, 50% and up for the other two segments; an audit priced at three grand over 5 days; a workflow build over 10 to 20 days; 3 to 5 months of support; a close time of 2 to 6 weeks for owners of firms between $0 and 100 million in annual revenue.

Not verified: all of it. No market research backs these margins, no deal has been closed at these prices, and the close-time figure has not been tested against a single conversation. The post attributes the advice to the co-founder explicitly, which is the correct form, and that attribution is the reason these numbers are readable at all.

Also worth noting as an internal inconsistency rather than a hidden one: "firms of 8 to 50 people" and "firms with $0 to 100 million in annual revenue" describe different populations. Both are reported as given.

Counted and real: zero leads in the relevant segments.

The second post carries one count and it is structural: 4 parties in the trust chain (business, security company, insurer, integrator), with the note that there may be more than one insurer in the picture. Nothing else in it is a measurement.

Not established there: that any insurer currently accepts any agent-audit badge as a condition of cover. That is the hinge the whole model turns on and it is stated as a design requirement, not as something observed in the market. The legal exposure described, a US company being sued over what a support agent promised, is reported as general knowledge rather than from a named case. The glide-path example is a hypothetical and reads as one.
