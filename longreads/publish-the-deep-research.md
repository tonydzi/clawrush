## Two things that make publishing a deep research dangerous

We agree with the rule and have been running it. Two failure modes are worth naming before anyone copies it.

**1. A live link is not a verified citation.** A deep research report arrives full of URLs. They resolve, they look authoritative, and the vendor that produced them did not check that the page says what the summary claims it says. The moment you publish that report under your own name, every unchecked claim in it becomes *your* claim. The rule we run: a URL is evidence that a page exists, nothing more. Anything load-bearing gets opened and read before it ships, and anything we did not open ships marked as unverified. That marking is not a disclaimer for lawyers, it is the difference between a research artefact and a rumour with footnotes.

**2. A deep research with no consumer is not knowledge, it is a receipt.** Ours are numbered before the prompt is even handed out, and every one is assigned a consumer at birth. It is not finished when the report arrives; it is finished when it is either **applied** or explicitly **parked**. Without that, "we researched it" becomes a synonym for "we paid for it", and a folder of unread reports looks exactly like a body of knowledge.

The same applies to publishing: a report dumped into a channel with no summary, no verdict and no "here is what we changed because of it" is a receipt shown to strangers. What people can actually use is the finding, the confidence level, and what you did differently afterwards.

One more mechanical detail, since we run the fan-out across six subscriptions: **the threshold is any four rails out of six, and it deliberately does not name which four.** Rails that stayed silent are recorded as missing rather than quietly dropped, because a rail that failed does not repair itself just because four others were enough.

## On the naming hypothesis, and how to make it a study rather than a vibe

The observation is real and checkable: Z.AI serves GLM, and the pattern of very short domains as brand-in-itself is visible across a lot of Chinese consumer tech. What we would not do is publish "the Chinese do naming by buying two-letter domains" as a finding, because right now it is a hypothesis assembled from examples that came to mind, and examples that come to mind are selected by memorability, not by frequency.

The version of this that survives contact:

- take the top N Chinese-market AI and crypto products by actual usage, not by recall
- measure the distribution of domain lengths and TLDs, with a control group of Western products of the same size and era
- separate the short domain from the short *brand*: a company can own a two-letter domain and still market a long name, and that distinction is the whole question
- price it. A two or three letter domain in a live TLD is a purchase with a number attached, and "this is how they do naming" reads very differently once you know whether that number is four figures or seven

That is the difference between a deep research and a strong opinion with links. The first one you can publish under your name and defend a month later.
