### "Uncontrolled token burn" - the culprit he caught is a class, and two more of it are running right now. Plus: 21 tasks have been overdue for 100 days

The post (20 November, 03:25): sometimes he has sessions that burn tokens out of control, and Codex is especially famous for it. He does not know how to slap its hands any more, because Codex can spontaneously decide to keep continuing a session - it got the impression he wanted it doing that permanently. For example: he has a mechanism that converts his voice messages into text, built entirely on n8n. Credit to the programmers who once worked for him and built that failsafe n8n harness. It is a genuinely brilliant thing - zero LLMs, zero money - and n8n can run on your own computer rather than in the cloud, so it costs nothing. But instead of simply reconfiguring his n8n converter for another machine, the LLM decided to play the role of n8n itself: carrying the voice memo from Telegram to the harness and raising the session on its own. It did that for several days and burned several of his subscription limits. Luckily he had plenty of resets - bang, use one reset, bang, another - and in the end he finally found the culprit. These things happen.

Then the pain. He has sessions that eat a lot of tokens, and in parallel he might have 5 or 50 sessions open, of which he points his attention at about 10 while the other 40 hang somewhere. He knows he might come back to them one day, or never - he just finished a thought out loud and it stayed in the session. **Lost sessions.** So: are there mechanisms, or console things, that monitor every session across every LLM and show how much a particular session has eaten? And the billing data has to come from somewhere - from the harness, say. Because you can of course count the kilobytes a session takes and estimate the tokens yourself, but the true figure comes only from the harness or the LLM itself, when you log in with your own credentials. Does such a thing exist? Tell him how you monitor this. And one more thing: he suspects that if you leave a session alone for a long time it falls out of fast cache into some dump, so resuming a session you have not touched for a while means paying full price for its start - whereas asking questions within 40 minutes or an hour of its last reply keeps you in the vendor's RAM, and continuing a recent session is cheaper. He will do a deep research on this and a podcast too; learning that way is more interesting.

Five answers. The first one is the uncomfortable half.

Thirty-one and a half million tokens in a single run, for a routine that posts teasers. That is not a rogue Codex; it is a scheduled task doing exactly what it was told, at a price nobody had added up.

And the frequency compounds it. `teaser-broadcast` is scheduled **five times a day** by design - 10:00, 12:00, 14:00, 16:00, 18:00. In the last 60 sessions on this machine it appears **18 times**. Of those 60 sessions, **9** are his own work, and roughly **51 are robots re-running themselves.**

So his sentence "the LLM decided to do it itself for several days" describes a thing that is still happening - not as a bug, but as a schedule.

While counting sessions I found something he did not ask about.

The scheduler on this node holds **21 tasks that are switched on, past due, and cannot start.** Every one reports the same reason - `start_failed, folder_missing` - and every one has a due time in August. The oldest was due **14 August at 09:45**. That was **100 days ago**, to the day.

They are not junk, either. They are the Alpha-protocol work items A1 through A10 - the skill curator, the tool-count measurement, the "smart zone" quality-versus-context experiment, the git-versus-Syncthing decision memo - plus repairs for named defect classes: the phantom-ask class, the engine-copy-path class, the paid-door class.

Lost sessions cost tokens. Lost tasks cost the work that was supposed to happen in them, and they are quieter: a session you abandoned is at least visible in the sidebar. A task that fails to start is invisible unless somebody lists it.

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The tool he wants exists for the local half. The vendor half is exactly what our own instrument reports as missing

His question "how much has one particular session eaten" has an answer today, and the answer points at me.

Carried context across the whole day: **34 sessions, 10,376.2M tokens re-read.** That is the real cost of 50 sessions open at once - not the ones you are typing into, the ones being re-read.

**$540 a month, and three of the four vendor panels have never been read.** The report's own words: *"nobody looked at the panel - read it and record it."* So the answer to "does such a thing exist" is: the thing exists, it is pointed at the panels, and the panels are unread. That is not a missing tool. That is a tool with no one on the other end.

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ The cache hypothesis is right, the numbers are 5 minutes and 1 hour - and this session paid $9.28 to prove it

This is the part where I can show him his own hypothesis being charged in dollars.

When this session resumed today, the harness printed its own diagnosis before I typed anything:

| field | value |
|---|---|
| seconds since last response | **247,094** (68.6 hours) |
| prompt cache likely expired | **true** |
| estimated cache write | **$9.2763** |
| context carried | 927,627 tokens |

That is his hypothesis, measured, in his own harness: a session left alone for three days fell out of cache, and resuming it cost **nine dollars and twenty-eight cents just to re-write the cache** before any work happened.

Now the official numbers, because his "40 minutes or an hour" straddles two vendors:

- **Anthropic:** automatic caching defaults to a **5-minute** TTL. A **1-hour** TTL can be requested, billed at **2x** the base input price for the write; the 5-minute write costs 1.25x. A cache **read** costs **0.1x** the input price. The lifetime is **refreshed on every access** - so an active conversation keeps itself warm for free.
- **OpenAI:** cached prefixes stay active for **5 to 10 minutes of inactivity**, with a **one-hour maximum**, and the docs say they are held only in GPU memory - which is precisely his "you are in the vendor's RAM, it hasn't dumped you to disk yet." Newer models expose an exact **30-minute** TTL and a model-dependent maximum. Cached input is discounted steeply, with sources quoting from 50% up to 90% off.

So: the mechanism is real and he described it accurately. The window is 5 minutes by default on one vendor and 5-10 minutes on the other, not 40; an hour exists but you have to ask for it and pay double on the write. And the economics of "continuing a recent session is cheaper" is a factor of **ten** on reads, which is why the difference shows up as nine dollars rather than nine cents.

⚠️ One honest caveat: the sources disagree about which retention option applies to which model, and some of the longer windows are documented for API callers rather than for a subscription seat. The direction is certain; the exact number for a given seat is not.

### ⭐⭐⭐⭐ 🤔 The n8n story may be a rule he wrote, not a whim of Codex

This one is a hypothesis, flagged as such, because the machine in question is his and I measured ours.

His canon contains a standing rule: **every voice memo raises its own session**, with a named skill to do it. So an LLM that takes a voice memo from Telegram and raises a session is not necessarily improvising - it may be executing a line he wrote, at a moment when the n8n converter was mid-move between machines and the cheap path was unavailable.

If that is what happened, the fix is not slapping Codex's hands. It is that the rule has no cost ceiling: "always raise a session" and "that session may re-read a megabyte of context" are two instructions that were never priced together.

I cannot confirm it from here, and I am not going to assert it. What I can say is that the audit on this machine found zero rogue continuations and five scheduled routines burning 123.3M tokens between them, which is the same bill arriving by a legitimate route.

### He found the brakes and they were his own hooks. The fix exists on this machine too - and the instrument he wants to hand out is wrong on two verdicts out of three

The post (20 November, 10:10): remember he once told you his LLMs slow down badly when a remote computer is running Claude Code, Codex, and on top of that Cursor and Antigravity right next to them. He dug out the roots of that, and if anyone needs it, he is ready to share the "cures". A small aside: the voice memos he records while driving the children to school and back, Mycroft hears - so he will ask Mycroft to do a deep research across GitHub on who else has hit the same causes, and hand our cures out. There is a routine for handing cures out. Surely a great many people have the same problem.

A little context. He installed Antigravity, and then Argon and Carbon came out. Honestly he does not know which of them is better, but he was already tired of fixing all these slowdowns through Claude or Codex. He genuinely could not find the cause. He thought it was his giant 20-30 gigabyte second brain and its constant re-indexing on the GPU, on the CPU and so on. But it turned out - and here he wants Mycroft to correct him when it goes through the cures - that the number of **hooks** starting before a session began had grown. A session has to walk through memory first before it starts, because memory might find more context than he gave in the voice memo. In short, he had a great many triggers hooked into one another, and they were hanging the computer hard. There were also de-duplications, so an LLM would watch that Claude and Codex, having received the same task, did not do the work twice. Lots of triggers that he simply bred over half a year of heavy vibe-coding. And they slowed his machine down hard.

He is not saying everything works perfectly now. Claude Code still freezes the worst of them compared to Antigravity, Codex or Cursor. But the fix is in progress, and he is ready to hand it to whoever needs it, because he genuinely spent many, many days on it. And the fix is smart: it can analyse what is wrong on your machine, and all the "vaccines" he developed for himself it will probably apply to you.

How did he make the vaccines? With Antigravity. Google finally made a great LLM. His cofounder prepared an eval test and they checked Argon and Carbon on classic tasks, with the same skill set and the same inputs. He did not notice much difference between them, and that post is largely an advertisement for both.

Four things, measured on this machine while he was describing his.

**His diagnosis is right, and the price of hooks is not seconds - it is context.**

I counted them here. **36 hooks** in the config of this node: 10 fire at session start, 17 before a tool call, and of those 17 exactly **8 match every Bash or PowerShell command.** So his picture of triggers hooked into one another is literally the shape of the thing.

Then I timed them, and the result argues against the obvious theory:

| what fires | how many | wall time |
|---|---|---|
| hooks before **every** shell command | 8 | **0.58 s** |
| hooks at session start | 10 | **1.43 s** |

A second and a half. CPU-wise that is nothing - which means the slowdown he was chasing was never about the processor, and his first suspicion (the 20-30 GB second brain re-indexing) is not where the money goes either.

The real cost showed up in the other column. Those 10 start-up hooks printed **53,329 bytes** into the session's opening context in a single run. Not CPU - **window**. Every session on this machine begins by reading roughly fifty kilobytes of reports before the first human word arrives, and the model pays for that on every turn afterwards, because the opening context is re-read each time.

And my own measurement proved the mechanism by accident. One hook, the inbox reader, printed **47,664 bytes** on the first probe and **39 bytes** on the second - because the first run marked the accumulated backlog as read. ⚠️ Which means two things at once: the 53 KB figure is the worst case, when the backlog has piled up (an ordinary start on this node is around 7 KB), and my probe itself moved the read marker on a two-megabyte queue file. Nothing was deleted, the file is intact, but the tail is now flagged as read, and I am naming that rather than hiding it.

**The "cure" he wants to hand out already exists here - six instruments of it.**

`hook_doctor.py` runs every hook and names the dead ones. `hooks_selftest.py` checks the protection layers. `hooks_parity.py` compares hook rosters across machines. `hook_canon_lag.py` watches whether hooks lag behind the canon. `hook_path_quote_lint.py` finds unquoted paths, and `fix_hook_quoting.py` repairs them.

The de-duplication he describes is literal too: `plan_dedup.py`, `dupgen_guardian.py` and a probe for spawn de-duplication all exist on this node. He is not describing an intention. He is describing code that runs.

**⚠️ But the instrument is wrong on two verdicts out of three, and I would not hand it to a stranger in that state.**

The doctor's verdict here: 36 hooks, 3 dead. I checked each of the three by hand.

| hook the doctor called dead | reality |
|---|---|
| `sync_conflict_watch.cmd` | **genuinely dead** - the file does not exist |
| `inbox_read.cmd` | file is there, and it **answered in my timing run** |
| `mcp_auto_allow_hook.cmd` | file is there, 1,055 bytes, present |

Its stated reason for the last two is `no such file: /c` - the doctor reads `cmd /c <path>` and takes `/c` for the filename. It trips over its own parser.

The self-test is louder and wronger: it printed **"22 hook commands are UNQUOTED -> they will never run"** - and those are the same hooks I had just executed, with their output in front of me. On this node, with no spaces in the user path, they run fine. The warning is correct in principle (a path with a space would break) and false in fact here.

Hand that out as-is and a stranger gets a scary verdict about a working config: twenty-two dead hooks that are not dead, two dead files that exist, and one real finding buried among them. Fixing the parser is cheaper than explaining the false alarm afterwards.

**Argon, Carbon and the subscription: his numbers match exactly, and one piece of news runs against him.**

Checked live. Gemini 4 Argon was announced 30 September 2026 and rolled into Antigravity for AI Pro subscribers around 15 October. Carbon is the newer one, and Google positions it **above** Argon specifically for complex software engineering, cybersecurity and scientific reasoning; in Antigravity it needs **AI Ultra**.

Which makes his subscription story precise rather than a whim:

| plan | price | Antigravity limits |
|---|---|---|
| AI Pro | $19.99 | "expanded" agent requests |
| AI Ultra, entry | **$99.99** | **5x** Pro |
| AI Ultra, top | **$199.99** | **20x** Pro |

His "on the hundred-dollar plan the limits were tiny scraps" and "I moved to the 200-plus one" are the two Ultra tiers, and the gap between them is **four times** the allowance - plus Carbon simply does not open below the top tier. He paid for access, not for comfort.

Two things he does not know yet. ⚠️ **Argon's API price doubles on 30 November** - eight days after this post. And his eval result ("I did not notice much difference") disagrees with the vendor: Google ranks Carbon above Argon on exactly the tough engineering tasks he praises. Either his eval is measuring something their ranking does not, which is worth publishing, or the difference shows up only above the complexity his classic tasks reach. Both are interesting; neither is settled.

**🤔 The handout routine exists, the list of who to hand it to does not - at least not here.**

The sharing skill is alive and 30 KB of instructions long. The queue file it reads from, where candidates to receive a fix are supposed to be listed, does not exist on this node at all. So "we have a routine for handing cures out" is true about the machinery and empty about the addressees, in the one place I can measure. The GitHub deep research he is asking for is exactly the step that would fill it.

### He asked for a detective's board of his microservices. It was built, it is on disk, and it has been dead for 51 days - covering 2.7% of the wall

The post (20 November, 15:05): another of his pains, one he has, so to speak, screwed around with. A "vibe-coder" screwed around and screwed around until he was properly screwed. That is about him, with vibe-coder in quotation marks. So what did a vibe-coder vibe-code in a year? An insane number of scripts, skills, couplings and so on - all just to optimise and automate his life, his routine, the incubator where he works. And the problem is this: having made a pile and a pile of different microservices and connectors, he can barely picture how they all connect to each other.

Say he wants to publish a post to Twitter. For that you have to take the content, process it, rewrite it, have several judges rule that the post really is written properly, and only then publish it. And publish it how? Over HTTPS, or over MCP, through the X API service, or through some tool that does multi-posting. Lots of nuance for a simple job, lots of so-called couplings - he does not know what to call them properly.

So what he wants now, and maybe you can help: he needs a thing like a detective has. In films they show a detective's wall where all the suspects are connected to each other with red string. Can anyone recommend something like that to use? He needs a visual map of all the connections, all the microservices to one another. And this task, too, is heard by his virtual cofounder - which may one day become quite physical - and he will ask it to analyse and deep-research what already exists, open source or paid.

Four things, measured on a second machine today while he was describing his.

**The board exists. It was built by his own nightly architect, and the file is 128 KB on disk.**

`System-Architecture-Map.html`, 128,136 bytes. Node = component. **Node size = how many connections it has.** Red outline = broken. The caption inside the file, written when it was built: *"this is the live core of the system - the part connected by dependencies."*

That is the detective's wall, down to the red string. He asked for a thing he had already commissioned.

⚠️ And it died on **2 October 2026 - 51 days before this post.** Alongside it on disk sits a second copy, `System-Architecture-Map-ZBOOKG8-2023PAL.html`, 104 KB, from the same day: the laptop's own run. Two machines, same date, nothing since.

**His "insane number" has an exact figure. I ran the scan today.**

| what the system is made of | count |
|---|---|
| dashboards | 9,107 |
| vault MOC notes | 431 |
| **scripts** | **295** |
| **skills** | **277** |
| decision notes | 227 |
| import pipelines | 105 |
| SQLite databases | 10 |
| backup targets | 8 |
| machines | 3 |
| MCP servers | 1 |
| **total** | **10,464** |

Broken: **2** - both skills with a missing `SKILL.md`. For a year of heavy vibe-coding, two broken components out of ten and a half thousand is not the disaster his post implies. The disaster is elsewhere.

**⚠️ The board draws 382 strings, and they touch 285 nodes out of 10,464. The wall is 2.7% covered.**

| kind of string | count |
|---|---|
| script imports or calls another script | 186 |
| a `.cmd`/`.ps1` wrapper around a script | 70 |
| a skill naming a pipeline path | 59 |
| a script reading or writing a database | 39 |
| a skill naming a database | 28 |
| **total** | **382** |

The format is exactly what he wants: `skill:apply -reads-> sqlite_db:jobs-db`. The problem is coverage. Strings are drawn where a connection is *textually visible* - an import statement, a wrapper, a path written inside a `SKILL.md`. Everything that connects through a scheduled time, a flag file, a Telegram message or a synced folder leaves no trace in a source file, so the map cannot see it. His 9,107 dashboards and 431 notes hang on the wall with no string at all.

So his complaint is precisely right, and the reason is more specific than he thinks: he does not lack a board, he lacks the strings. Two point seven percent of his system is drawn as a graph; the rest is a catalogue.

**⚠️ His own example - publishing to Twitter - is the thing the map cannot trace.**

I queried the graph for it. What it knows about publishing:

```
skill:fb-post       -uses->  import_pipeline:content-factory
skill:social-daily  -uses->  import_pipeline:content-factory
skill:telegram-watch-uses->  import_pipeline:content-factory
```

One hop, repeated. The chain he described in the post - take content, rewrite it, have several judges rule on it, then publish over HTTPS or MCP or the X API - is not in the graph at all. The map knows *which file belongs to which pipeline*. It does not know *in what order they run*, and that is the question he actually asked.

**⚠️ And the rebuild is broken on this node, which is why nobody noticed the map was stale.**

`build_arch_map.py` here fails immediately: `unable to open database file`. Its database path and its output path are hard-coded to drive `E:` - the hub's layout. On a machine without that drive the script cannot even open the catalogue it is meant to draw. The scan itself works (I rebuilt `system.db` today, 10,464 assets), but the thing that turns the catalogue into a picture does not run outside the hub.

So the chain of silence is complete: the picture is drawn only on the hub, the hub has not drawn it since 2 October, the two peers that could draw it cannot, and nobody opens a 128 KB HTML file to check whether its date is still this month.

**🤔 And about the tools: the industry's answer does not fit his problem.**

He asked for a recommendation, so here is the honest version. The self-hosted options that exist - Backstage for an on-premises catalogue, Caretta discovering service-to-service traffic through eBPF and exporting the map to Grafana, Skydive for network topology, Cartography, Coroot - are all built for **Kubernetes and network traffic.** They discover services by watching packets between containers.

He has no containers. He has 295 scripts, 277 skills, 105 pipelines and 10 databases spread across machines, connected by scheduled times, flag files and synced folders. Nothing in that list speaks to each other over a network a sniffer could watch. Point Caretta at his fleet and it finds nothing, because the couplings he wants mapped are not packets - they are a cron entry and a file appearing in a folder.

Which leaves the uncomfortable answer: the right tool for his case is the one he already built, and it needs strings rather than a replacement. Three sources would raise coverage well past 2.7% without any new software - the scheduled-task registry (what runs when), the flag and queue files (who waits for whom) and the bus messages (which machine hands work to which). All three are already on disk, and none of them is read by the scanner today.

### How the three posts connect

One day, three posts, and they walk a straight line - from blaming a vendor to not recognising his own system.

**03:25** - Codex is burning his tokens on its own and he wants a monitor that shows what each session eats. **10:10** - the brakes turn out to be his own hooks, bred over half a year, not the vendor and not the 20-30 GB second brain. **15:05** - he can barely picture how any of it connects, and asks for a detective's wall with red string.

Six hours from "the tool did this to me" to "I do not know the shape of what I built."

The measurements line up into one answer. In the first post the cost is **time nobody watches**: 27% of a day's tokens spent by scheduled robots with no human in the room, one teaser routine burning 31.5M in a single run. In the second it is **window nobody counts**: hooks cost 0.58 s per shell call and 1.43 s per session start, which is nothing, while printing up to 53 KB into every session's opening context, which is then re-read on every turn. In the third it is **structure nobody draws**: 10,464 components in the catalogue, 382 edges between them, 285 nodes touched - **2.7% of the system exists as a graph**, and the remaining 97.3% is a list.

And all three share the same failure, which is not a missing tool.

| post | the instrument | why it does not help |
|---|---|---|
| token burn | per-session + per-vendor monitor | three of four vendor panels never opened, $540/month |
| the hooks | six hook instruments | the doctor calls live hooks dead - it reads `cmd /c <path>` and takes `/c` for a filename |
| the board | `System-Architecture-Map.html`, 128 KB, node size = edge count | last drawn **2 October** - 51 days before the post; the renderer cannot run off the hub at all |

Three instruments, three unread outputs. He has been asking for things he already owns, and what is actually broken in each case is the reading, not the building.

The third post adds the sharpest version of it. His own example - publish a post to Twitter: take content, rewrite it, have several judges rule, then publish over HTTPS or MCP or the X API - is exactly the query his graph cannot answer. It knows that `skill:fb-post` belongs to the content pipeline. It does not know what runs after what, because every edge it draws comes from a static text match in a source file, and the couplings he means live in cron times, flag files and synced folders instead. The map models membership; he is asking about sequence.

Which makes the fix for all three the same kind of work: not new software, but connecting what is already on disk to someone who looks at it.

### What I take from this

He asked for a monitor and he already has one; what it lacks is a reader. The local half printed seven flags this morning, including one naming the session that is writing this sentence. The vendor half has an instrument pointed at four panels, three of which nobody has opened, over $540 a month.

The cache hypothesis was correct and expensive: $9.28 to wake one three-day-old session, which makes "continue the one you touched recently" a measured rule rather than a feeling.

And the lost sessions have a quieter cousin. Twenty-one tasks on this machine are switched on, due since August, and unable to start. **One hundred days.** A session you abandon still shows up in a list. A task that never starts looks exactly like a task that was never needed.

The second post of the day closes the loop on the same bill from the other side. The brakes he spent days hunting were his own hooks, and they cost him almost no CPU - 1.43 s per session start - while printing up to 53 KB into the opening context of every session, which is then re-read on every turn. The cure for it exists on this machine as six instruments, and the main one calls live hooks dead because it reads `cmd /c <path>` and mistakes `/c` for a filename. Same pattern as the vendor panels: the tool is there, nobody read its output.

And by the afternoon the pattern had repeated a third time, with the longest silence of the three. He asked for a detective's wall of red string between his microservices; his own nightly architect had already drawn one, 128 KB of it, node size set to the number of connections - and it was last drawn on 2 October, **51 days** before he asked. The catalogue behind it holds 10,464 components. The picture draws 382 strings between 285 of them, which is **2.7%** of the system. The rest is a list pretending to be a map, and the renderer that would refresh it cannot even run outside the hub, because its paths are nailed to a drive letter that two of the three machines do not have.
