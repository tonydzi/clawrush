## What went out today

### Distil every LLM session into your vault, then search the distillate

Data distillation.

It is very important to keep in your second brain, which I hope you have, not only everything that has happened to you in life, where, loosely speaking, it has access to your email, Telegram, WhatsApp and so on.

It is also important to keep everything that happened in all your sessions, in all your LLMs: that is, you have ChatGPT, Grok, Claude, maybe some DeepSeek and so on.

And you have had a large number of sessions.

You were looking for something, booking a therapist, trying to buy some mouse or monitor, writing a letter to your landlord. And all of that is quite valuable information.

All the deep research you have run is certainly very valuable too.

And I have set it up so that all of my sessions, every single one, both the coding sessions like Claude Code and Codex and the ones from an ordinary browser, when you are simply running deep research, all of them land in my storage, in my vault.

Entities are distilled out of them.

That is, loosely speaking, from sessions that held a long, long slab of knowledge, data and text, out of that long slab of text we distil literally a few sentences: what it was about, what pain we solved, and what we actually achieved.

And it is this distillate that new sessions search. Every time I start a new session, I have set it up so that the new session always tries to work out whether we have worked on this topic at all before.

And very often it turns out that the knowledge was already in my storage.

Loosely speaking, over the last week I asked my Claude and Codex 20 to 30 times which meetup I should go to next in San Francisco.

And if I did not have this memory of mine, it would search for the event from scratch every time, look up an event for me and so on. Whereas here my cofounder goes into my storage, looks at which events we have already registered for and which ones it has already recommended, checks whether anything new has appeared, and gives me a very clear recommendation.

And again, when you have many computers, and I have many computers, you can ask from one computer, then walk away from it and ask the same thing from another.

And the other computer, since you have a single shared memory area between your own machines, will see what you were discussing on the other one and give you the information you need.

That is, a session can start on one computer and finish on another.

### A routine that cannot rewrite itself will not survive the year

Self-improvement of a session.

What is self-improvement of a session?

Say you have a routine that submits your candidacy to 10 job openings every day. That routine opens various boards to apply you to positions, and those boards, I do not know, maybe something falls off on them and so on. Some boards, the very format of those boards changes, the places where vacancies are posted.

So, the routine improves itself.

Every routine, it seems to me, is obliged to improve itself.

That is, every time it works out what it tripped over, tries to solve that, and rewrites itself into the future, makes itself better. I think it is a great thing.

Not necessarily only when applying you to jobs, there are many use cases.

Any routine, I do not know, say you have a content factory, the content factory handles posting and distribution, and it improves itself so that in future it can be more optimal, more productive and make fewer mistakes.

I do not know how well this works on models like Sonnet or old simple ones like Luma and so on, but on Opus and on Fable such routines work just perfectly, they improve themselves. It will not get you to AGI, of course, but you really do spend less time keeping the infrastructure alive.

So a routine is obliged to improve itself.

That is a genuinely mandatory criterion.

If you have already learned to decompose all of your daily tasks into a pile and a pile of routines, and you make them self-improving, you will save yourself a very large amount of time.

If anyone needs self-improving routines, I have, I do not know, getting on for a hundred routines, if not two hundred, I do not know how many, maybe three hundred, I have not counted, but I really do have a lot of them, then I am ready to share with you.

If your Claude tells you what your pains are, we can make a little chat right now where my Claude talks to your Claude, and we will try to fix it. More precisely, not fix it, I will simply hand over all the knowledge I have so that you can use Claude, Codex and whatever more optimally, to be more productive.

Once again, any skills and so on have to be self-improving, because something is constantly falling over, falling off, there is the API, MCP, sites change and so on, you constantly need to improve yourself.

This will not bring you to AGI, but it will bring you to your routine surviving. You do not have to keep running around wiping noses.

Imagine, I have 300 routines there, I would not be able to run around wiping all their noses if I did not have this self-improvement, this self-improvement skill.

### When every model knows the same things, value moves to the harness

I made myself a podcast about which kind of startup makes sense now, in the AI era, when you build a startup and tomorrow a large language model has been taught to do the same thing.

So here is the gist. What I see now, having walked around various conferences in San Francisco, is that value is moving into the interface.

Meaning that soon all AI models will be identical and similar to each other. The volume of knowledge in the world is finite, and in a few years all the knowledge in the world will have been pumped out. But I mean the mass market right now, not narrow vertical models for medicine or law and so on.

And the mass market is just a language model for the average job, for an ordinary housewife, for an ordinary coder.

So all these models, Gemini, Codex and Claude, will hold one and the same set of knowledge. Accordingly, their value and their ability to reason will be roughly the same. Give or take, average across the board plus or minus 5 to 10 percent.

What happens after that? And what, it seems to me, is worth doing now?

Right now I have seen a big boom in harnesses. A harness is when you have several models at once inside some interface. And it seems to me that the harnesses which win popularity have the ability to compete with the models.

Take Perplexity, which I think you can call a harness, because essentially it is an aggregator of LLMs with a convenient interface. I do not use Perplexity much, because it is not very convenient for me, but for those users who need several LLMs in one bottle, for that housewife who needs to have several LLMs, it is a good model.

I am talking more about coding now, but not coding in the sense of you writing code, rather in the sense that any research work and so on, agentic work, is done through things like Claude Code or Codex.

So here is what I see: a lot of harnesses will appear with large numbers of users.

And accordingly there is no sense in using an inconvenient interface from Claude Code when I need to work with three LLMs.

The only question is that many LLM vendors will not be ready for their product to be inserted into somebody else's interface, and will ban you for it. Remember the OpenClaw example, where OpenClaw supposedly banned its own users and so on.

But by the way, I went up to people at an Anthropic gathering just now, they had a Founder House, at Terra, a kind of coworking place in San Francisco. So, walking up to 5 to 7 Anthropic employees who work in Applied AI, I asked them: I have about 4 subscriptions there, are you going to ban me? They said: no, dude.

They all laughed and said: dude, come on, until you have a thousand subscriptions you can relax. I said: and who needs a thousand subscriptions? They said: dude, the Chinese. Of course the Chinese buy a thousand accounts and do model distillation.

I do not know how model distillation works technically, but mostly they only block the Chinese who buy a thousand accounts and through those thousand accounts try to somehow distil their brains. No idea at all how that works, honestly. Well, presumably it works somehow.

I have heard the word distillation many times. Heard that word many times, distillation. But for me distillation is when I look through my own sessions.

Anyway, the point is that nobody gets banned with 4 accounts.

Again, some of my friends on Facebook will say they got banned, but maybe you just gave away that you are in a sanctioned country, maybe that is why. Otherwise, it seems to me, you can relax.

And when I say that I shove my account into various harnesses, they say that OpenClaw was some kind of special case where Anthropic got very angry. But in general they say: shove your subscription account wherever you like and do not worry, dude. Honestly, they say, do not worry. We have better things to do right now than block guys who put their account into some harness.

So there you go.

## How it connects

The useful idea here is not storage, it is what gets stored. A full transcript is close to unsearchable: retrieval over raw slabs returns the longest conversation that mentioned your words, not the one that solved your problem. The distillate inverts that. A few sentences per session saying what it was about, what pain it closed and what was achieved, and those sentences are what the next session searches.

That design choice has a property worth naming. The distillate is small enough to read, which means a wrong one is visible, while a bad embedding of a 40-page transcript fails silently. Compressing before indexing is usually treated as a cost-saving measure. Here it is the accuracy measure.

The second half of the post, continuing the swarm thread from yesterday, is the part where the honest scope matters. Starting a session on one machine and finishing it on another works because the vault is synced between machines owned by one person. Shared memory between your own computers is a filesystem problem with a known answer. Shared memory between people is not the same thing scaled down, and nothing here claims it is, which is an improvement on how this was phrased earlier in the week.

The meetup example is the strongest evidence in the post precisely because it is mundane. Twenty to thirty repetitions of the same question in one week is exactly the case where recall beats a fresh search: the answer depends on what was already recommended and already registered for, and a stateless assistant cannot know either.


The two posts of this date are the two halves of one system, and the order they came in is the useful part. The first says: keep everything, and search a distillate of it. The second says: every routine must notice what it tripped over and rewrite itself. Memory without the rewrite is an archive, and a rewrite without memory is a routine that relearns the same breakage every month.

The link between them is the thing neither post names. A self-improving routine needs to know what already broke, and the place that knowledge lives is the distillate from the first post. So the loop is: the routine fails, the failure is summarised, the summary is searchable, and the next version of the routine reads it before it decides anything. Dropping either half gives you something that looks like it works and does not accumulate.

Where the second post runs ahead of our evidence is the word obliged. Self-rewriting is stated as a mandatory criterion for every routine, and the honest version is narrower: it is mandatory for routines whose environment changes underneath them, which is where the examples come from, job boards whose formats shift and APIs that fall off. A routine over a stable interface does not need to rewrite itself, and letting it try is a way to break something that was working.


The third post of the day is the strategic frame the other two were operating inside without saying so. If models converge and the differentiator moves to the layer that holds several of them, then everything the first two posts describe, a vault with distilled sessions and routines that rewrite themselves, is harness work. That is the honest reading of the week: the thing being built here is not a model and not an app, it is the layer between them, and that layer is where this post says the value goes.

It also resolves a tension from yesterday. Shovels are the funded niche, harnesses are named among them, and the harness being built here is given away. If the convergence thesis holds, the free version is competing in the same category as the funded ones, on the axis an engineer actually chooses by. That is either the strongest possible distribution play or a decision nobody has made deliberately, and it is still undecided in public.

One claim in the post needs its hedge kept. The convergence of models to plus or minus 5 to 10 percent is a forecast about other people's roadmaps, stated from conference impressions, and the post says so. Nothing here measures it, and the opposite outcome, where frontier models separate further on reasoning while cheap ones commoditise, would invalidate the whole thesis without changing anything we have built. Worth keeping, because our own work does not depend on which way that goes.

## The numbers

Three posts on this date.

Twenty to thirty repetitions of the same meetup question in one week, his own count of his own use. Several named rails whose sessions land in the vault: Claude Code, Codex, and browser sessions from ChatGPT, Grok, Claude, DeepSeek.

Verified on our side: the ingestion and the episodic index exist, there is an engine that builds the session index nightly, and the skill that searches old chats is live. Stated and not verifiable from this node: the index itself lives where the GPU is and is deliberately not synced here, so the size of the corpus cannot be read from this machine.

Not measured, and it is the number the whole design rests on: how often the recall actually finds the earlier session, versus how often it misses and the work gets redone. Nobody has sampled hit rate against a list of questions with known previous answers. Also unmeasured, and cheaper: how often a distillate is wrong about its own session, which is the failure that would quietly poison every later search.


From the second post, the one number and how far it can be checked. He says getting on for a hundred routines, maybe two hundred, maybe three hundred, and says plainly that he has not counted. On the node this file was written from there are 58 scheduled tasks outside the system folder, read live. So the three hundred is a fleet-wide figure across six machines plus cron on the non-Windows nodes, and it cannot be verified from here; the honest statement is 58 on this machine and no fleet-wide count performed today.

Not measured, and it is the pair of numbers that would turn the whole claim from a feeling into a finding: how many self-rewrites have actually happened, and how many of them made the routine better rather than merely different. Nothing counts self-edits, and nothing compares the success rate of a routine before and after one. The model claim sits in the same place: routines allegedly self-improve well on the stronger models and poorly on the weaker ones, and no routine has ever been run on two models side by side to check.


From the third post, the figures and what each one is. Plus or minus 5 to 10 percent as the spread between mass-market models once knowledge saturates: a forecast, his own, from walking conferences. A thousand subscriptions as the threshold that attracts enforcement attention, and four as a safe number: reported conversation, not policy, and reported as such. Five to seven Anthropic employees in Applied AI as the sample he spoke to at a Founder House event at Terra in San Francisco.

Not verifiable from our side and flagged rather than repeated as fact: whether vendor enforcement actually keys on account volume, since what the post contains is friendly conversation at a party, not terms of service. The OpenClaw episode is likewise given as remembered, with "supposedly" in the original.

The one thing in this post that our own records do cover: distillation in his sense, reducing sessions to a few searchable sentences, is the subject of the first post of this date, and that one is built and running. Model distillation, the thing the thousand-account buyers are said to be doing, is a different operation entirely, and the post is explicit that he does not know how it works.
