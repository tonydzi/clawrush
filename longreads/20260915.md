# 15 September 2026: Giving Is Not the Result

## What went out today

### Giving skills away, and finally counting what came back

When my Claude builds something useful and a skill falls out of it, I try to give it to the world. I have a routine for that. It goes to GitHub, finds people who hit roughly the same pain I already fixed, and hands them the skill.

The reason is not charity. I want to be noticed in the engineering world, and I want my signature on everything I give.

A skill is only a crumb of what I actually built: a second brain, coordination between several Claudes running on different machines, and the machinery around it. The skills are the part small enough to hand to a stranger. For the rest, the door is my GitHub or a direct message.

Today I asked the uncomfortable question. Does any of it work?

I checked. The starter kit that holds 101 of those skills has **6 stars and 2 forks**. The whole profile: **129 public repositories, 33 followers**.

So giving is not the result. The result is a count: stars on exactly the skills that were handed out, where they went, how relevant each one was to the thread it landed in, and whether a single person came back to say thanks.

Without that count, a routine that gives things away is indistinguishable from a routine that spams, just with better intentions.

### The engineers who answered back

Some engineers on GitHub have started replying to me. That changes what I should be counting.

The routine I want next is not another round of reaching out. It is a list of everyone who actually answered, narrowed to the top 10, with two questions about each one: could this person help me get hired, and could this person start using what I build and help build it with me.

Many agent frameworks grow in public, with outside contributors doing a real share of the work. I would like my repository to be built that way too, together with someone.

A repository nobody else commits to is a portfolio, not a project.

## How it connects

The two posts of the day are the same question asked from both ends.

The first one counts what came back from giving things away, and finds almost nothing: 6 stars on a kit of 101 skills. The second one looks at the few people who did come back, and says those are the real asset. Reach is measured in touches; relationships are measured in replies.

Put together, the lesson is a change of target. Stop optimising for how many people received something. Start with the handful who answered, and find out what can be built with them.

Yesterday's post asked whether tomorrow's routine inherits today's fix. Today the same idea turns outward: a process that never reads back what its actions produced, whether that is a routine or an outreach strategy, keeps doing the same thing forever, confidently.
