## What went out today

### Custom lessons from my own book beat random videos

I want to say that NotebookLM is a cool thing, because there you can prepare any learning material specifically for yourself and turn it into a podcast. And that is super convenient precisely for learning. You are not watching random videos on YouTube, you are loading into your brain exactly what you need. And it loads into the brain very nicely, very interactively. Their idea with the podcast is just a bombastic idea.

A good example. I have a book on flying a light aircraft. I keep reading it now and then, but I do not really have time to properly dive into it. Because you have to sit down, be diligent and so on. And you need to spend hundreds of hours studying, preparing for the light aviation pilot exams.

And I thought that I would ask my Claude, give it the book, and say: make me 10 lessons at a high level. And then in the places where some weakness is felt, I will ask it: take this lesson and decompose it, and make me another 10 more detailed lessons out of it. That is the most ideal thing. The most, most ideal.

So if you spend an hour a day, loosely speaking, in the car, or you go running and so on, and you have time to listen and no chance to read, then this is the very best way to load information into your head.

Once I met a certain Brian Taliby, who positioned his startup (he is a fraud) as a startup that lets people learn faster. Well, he raised some money there. But now I have understood that this is the best way to learn faster: load into your head the content that you specifically need.

That is, the artificial intelligence understands your weaknesses, understands your strong sides, and gives you only the content that you need to learn. And the content you are already good at, why learn it again. In short, customisation of learning is a real game changer.

### The do-it-yourself backup cost three times the thing it backs up

I am very surprised by the Samsung ecosystem. I walk around conferences in San Francisco. And yes, right now I have a Plaud, a voice recorder that records (with the consent of the people I am talking to, naturally) all the conversations, all our conversations and meetings. As a backup I decided to build myself another portable recorder on top of Samsung and Omi, an open source framework, just as an experiment.

And for the first time in my life I bought myself a Samsung. For the first time in my life, imagine that, after roughly 20 years of using an iPhone, of the iPhone ecosystem. I have never in my life had an Android or a Samsung. Never.

So, I bought myself a tablet. A small tablet, the size of an iPad mini but much more powerful, with a good screen resolution and a sturdy body, so that you can carry it without a case and if it falls it will not break. I was surprised at how well Android is made, everything inside is very smooth, very convenient and even properly intuitive.

Right now on the Android I have the Omi app, connected to an external microphone, a good quality microphone, and everything gets recorded no worse than with the Plaud, transcribed, picked up straight away by my Claude from the cloud, transcribed, put into the vault.

Very convenient, you can build a recorder for people who do not have enough money for a Plaud. Although, to be honest, this setup cost about three times more than a Plaud. 600 dollars for the tablet, plus a microphone, two microphones at 100 bucks each and so on. Well, who knows.

In any case, Plaud, I love Plaud more and more, because it really has paid for itself a great deal. The main thing is, he switched it on and forgot about it. That is worth a great deal, when you have things that you simply switch on and forget. Those things are really worth a great deal.

So there you go, my journey with Samsung, with Android and so on has begun. And I want to say that maybe someone will advise me: do there exist any hacker firmwares, maybe for Samsung or for Android, firmwares that are somehow connected to agentic work? Could I somehow reflash my Samsung, and Android devices are easy to reflash after all, so that it is more AI-native and integrated into my work.

But in short, Samsung is a super cool thing. I think that I... More precisely, Android is a super cool thing. I think that I am now going to switch over to Android little by little. In short, this thing is great. And I recommend Android to everyone in every way.

## How it connects

Two posts on this date, and the first one is the mirror image of a post two days ago. On 14 October the same tool, a generated podcast on NotebookLM, was used to prepare for LeetCode-style interviews, and listening to it produced a decision to skip that format entirely. Today the same tool is proposed as the way to actually learn something: a personal book turned into ten high-level lessons, with any weak spot expanded into ten more.

**So the same instrument did two different jobs: it settled a decision in one case and delivered instruction in the other, and nothing in the pipeline distinguishes those two uses.** That is worth naming because the second use is the one everybody builds for, and the first is the one that saved more time.

The mechanism proposed here deserves a precise reading, because the post's own summary is slightly stronger than what it describes. Described: generate a shallow pass over the material, then on demand decompose the parts that feel weak into a deeper pass. That is adaptive depth, and it works. Claimed: the AI understands your weaknesses and gives you only what you need. Those are different things. In the described loop the weakness signal comes from the listener saying "this part felt thin", not from any measurement the system performs. The loop is real and useful; the understanding is still human.

Worth keeping the honest part of the post in view: the stated obstacle was never access to the material. The book has been owned and dipped into for a long time. What was missing was a format that fits an hour in a car or a run, and that is the actual problem the podcast solves.

The second post of the day looks like it belongs to a different subject, consumer hardware rather than learning, and it answers the same question from the opposite side. An open source recorder was assembled as a backup for a device that already worked, and the honest line in the post is the price: the replacement cost roughly three times the thing it replaces, 600 dollars of tablet plus two microphones at 100 each against one recorder. The verdict that follows is not about features at all. The device that keeps its place is the one that got switched on and forgotten.

**So both posts price the same thing, and it is not capability, it is the step a human no longer takes.** The lessons post is valuable because it fits an hour that was already being spent in a car; the recorder is valuable because nobody has to remember it exists. In neither case is the win a new ability. In both cases the win is a removed action.

And then the two posts disagree in a way worth naming, because the disagreement is the useful part. The learning loop is praised while its weakness signal is still supplied by hand: the listener says "this part felt thin" and asks for a deeper pass. The recorder is praised precisely because the hand was taken out. By the recorder's own standard the learning loop is not finished yet, and that is a sharper test of it than anything in the first post.

One more thing the price tag settles. "You can build this for people who cannot afford a Plaud" is stated and then immediately contradicted by the invoice in the next sentence, which the post leaves standing rather than quietly dropping. The experiment is worth running for what it teaches about the stack; it is not the cheap option, and saying so is the whole value of reporting the number.

## The numbers

Two posts on this date.

Stated: 10 high-level lessons from one book, each expandable into 10 more detailed ones; hundreds of hours of study required for light aviation pilot exams; roughly one hour a day of listening time in a car or on a run.

Reported as hearsay about a third party: that an acquaintance raised money for a faster-learning startup. The characterisation attached to him in the post is the author's own aside, not a verified claim, and it is relayed here as his words rather than as a finding.

Not measured: whether the generated lessons produce retention. Episodes generated is countable and is the only number anyone has; what a listener retained a week later is not tracked at all, which means the entire argument for customised learning currently rests on how it feels rather than on a test.

Also not measured, and cheap to fix: which parts a listener actually asked to decompose. That list is the only honest record of where the weaknesses were, and it is not being kept.

From the second post, all first-hand and all about his own spend: 600 dollars for the tablet, two external microphones at roughly 100 dollars each, a total setup costing about three times a single Plaud, and roughly 20 years of iPhone use before this first Android purchase. The claim that the Omi setup records "no worse" than the Plaud is his own listening judgement, not a measured comparison of transcripts.

Not measured, and this is the gap the day leaves open on our side: two recording rails now feed the same vault, the Plaud one and the Omi one, and nothing counts how often the same conversation arrives twice. A duplicate transcript is not a cosmetic problem, because retrieval over the vault treats two copies of one meeting as two independent sources of agreement.

Also not measured, and the one that follows directly from his verdict: manual steps per run. "Switched it on and forgot" is a quality bar, and we have no counter for it anywhere in our own pipelines, which means we cannot say which of our routines meet it.
