# 6 November 2026: Three Skills For Making Skills Already Exist, And The Size Spread Is 267 To One

*This file collects the posts of the day and the connections between them. More posts from this date are appended here as they go out.*

## What went out today

### A skill for making skills, with one algorithm behind all of them

Anton will run a deep research into how other teams and people set up skill creation. He wants a separate skill for making skills, with the methodology inside it: how to design them properly. Skills should be ergonomic, high quality, maximally clear and understandable, without unnecessary complexity and without entropy growth. Understandable enough that anybody should be able to use them, even a very unprepared person. He wants to work out which types of skills are better to create, by what logic to design them, and how broad or how detailed the rules and descriptions should be. The outcome he wants is one clear algorithm by which all his skills get created.

The recall answered half of this with three existing skills, and the other half with a measurement that explains exactly what he is feeling.

⭐⭐ **Three skills for making skills already exist on the shelf.** `skillify` at **46KB**, last touched 22 September, the largest of the three. `skill-gap` at **12KB**, 2 October, which finds the hole where a skill is missing. `skill-forge` at **5.9KB**, 2 October, the smith itself. Plus a `skill-creator` shipped as a plugin alongside them.

So "I want a separate skill for making skills" describes a shelf that already holds three of them, built across two months, none of which has absorbed the others.

**The shelf itself, measured.** 282 directories, **277** with a `SKILL.md`, **3,683KB** in total. That is 3.6 megabytes of instructions for one operator.

⭐⭐⭐ **And here is the number that names what he is actually asking about.** The median skill body is **9,315 bytes**. The largest is `dr-fanout` at **179,104 bytes**. The smallest is `screenpipe-worklog` at **671 bytes**.

A spread of **267 to one**, with no stated rule anywhere about how big a skill should be.

That is "entropy growth" with a figure attached. His brief asks how broad or detailed the rules should be, and the honest answer is that today the question has no answer at all: the top five are `dr-fanout` 174KB, `apply` 125KB, `tt` 122KB, `retro` 94KB, `rep-reply` 52KB, while the median is nine kilobytes and the floor is under one.

⚠️ **But the discipline he wants does exist for one half, and it holds at 96 percent.** There is a standing rule that a skill description is at most 400 characters on a single quoted line, and there is a live instrument enforcing it, `skill_desc_audit.py` at 12KB.

Measured against all 277: **zero** skills have no description, and **13** exceed 400 characters, which is **4 percent**. The worst are `local-to-call` at 1,281 characters, `local-brain-onboard` at 1,133 and `local-fb-messenger` at 1,003, and every one of the 13 is a local skill rather than a fleet one.

⭐ So the shape of the gap is precise rather than general. Where a rule and a door exist, compliance is 96 percent. Where no rule exists, the spread is 267-fold. The methodology he is asking for is needed for exactly one dimension, the body, and it is needed because nobody ever wrote a sentence about it.

**Two more instruments are already standing beside the shelf.** `skill_usage_log.py` at 13KB, which counts whether a skill is actually used rather than merely called, and `codex_skills_bridge.py` at 18KB, which mounts the same file for a different agent so no second copy is made.

Which means the "one clear algorithm" has three of its four parts built: a creator, a gap-finder, a description auditor, a usage counter and a bridge. What it lacks is the one page saying how large a skill may be, what it must contain, and when it should be split instead of grown.

⚠️ **And one thing to settle before commissioning research into how other people do it.** A 3.6MB shelf of 277 skills is itself the standing context tax on every session. The question "which types of skills are better to create" has a local answer available before any external comparison: run the usage counter and see which of the 277 were used at all in the last thirty days. That is the cheapest possible input to the methodology, and it needs no research order.

### Rebuild the sync: the hypothesis was right and badly understated

Anton wants to rebuild synchronisation globally. How many sessions exist and how often files change inside them. How many files are in the store, which ones usually change and which never do. His hypothesis: 80 to 90 percent of files in the store never change, yet 100 percent of them are synchronised. So find the files that are guaranteed, or nearly guaranteed, not to change, take them out of sync, and synchronise only what has a high chance of changing. He wants deterministic rules about which sessions and which file types move, and asks his co-founder for options for the new logic.

This is the first brief in two days that can be answered with a direct measurement rather than a recall, so I ran it.

⭐⭐⭐ **The hypothesis is right, and it understates the case by an order of magnitude.** The vault holds **285,960** files. Files modified in the last thirty days: **851**. That is **0.3 percent**, against his guess of 10 to 20 percent moving.

| window | files | share |
|---|---|---|
| last 24 hours | 236 | 0.1% |
| last week | 146 | 0.1% |
| last month | 469 | 0.2% |
| last 3 months | 67,030 | 23.4% |
| last 6 months | 218,074 | 76.3% |

So 286 thousand files are being kept in sync in order to deliver 851 changes a month.

**By file type the picture is even sharper.** Of **260,595** markdown files, **709** moved in the last month, which is 0.3 percent. Of **1,888** Python files, **zero** moved. The same for 381 text files, 211 photos, 174 audio recordings and 155 images: not one modification in thirty days.

⚠️ **One honest limit on this instrument, before anyone quotes the numbers.** Modification time is not an edit history. Grouped by month, 58.8 percent of the vault carries a June 2026 timestamp, 16.3 percent July, 14.5 percent September. A single month holding 168,026 files is a deployment, not a working rhythm: those are the dates files landed on this disk.

So the defensible claim is "851 files changed in the last thirty days", which is measured directly. The claim "these files never change" is not available from mtime, only "they have not changed since they arrived here". For the decision he wants to make that is enough, and I would rather say so than hand him a precision I cannot support.

⭐⭐ **And the measurement points the rule somewhere other than where his brief looks.** His other question was how many sessions exist. On this node: **942** session files, of which **873** changed in the last thirty days. That is **92.7 percent**.

Put the two next to each other and the deterministic rule writes itself. The vault is 285,960 files moving at 0.3 percent. The sessions are 942 files moving at 92.7 percent. These are not one store with mixed behaviour, they are two stores with opposite behaviour, currently treated the same way.

So the split is not "changing files versus unchanging files", which needs per-file prediction and will always be wrong at the margins. It is "the archive and the working set", which needs no prediction at all, because the two already live in different directories.

⚠️ **One standing rule to respect while doing it, and it has teeth.** The vault ignore file is deliberately tiny, 14 lines, and it carries its own warning dated 6 August: only junk goes here, real work is forbidden, because an ignore hides a file from view and the debt becomes invisible forever.

That rule does not block his plan, but it does decide the mechanism. Taking 200 thousand files out of sync by adding them to the ignore list would make them invisible and unrecoverable by exactly the failure the rule was written for. Moving them into a second share with a different sync mode keeps them visible and keeps them backed up. Architecture, not ignore.

⭐ Which turns a research brief into four concrete decisions: the archive moves to its own share rather than into an ignore list; the working set stays as it is; the boundary runs along directories, not file types, because the type measurement says everything except markdown is already frozen; and whatever is moved gets its own backup check, since a share that nothing writes to is also a share nobody notices breaking.

### Black windows stealing my focus, and the logging that turned out to be running already

A black terminal window appears and disappears on my hub every few seconds. It grabs focus, and typing becomes impossible.

I wanted to know what those windows are and which processes launch them. If they are parasitic background junk, I want them damped or switched off. And I wanted logging in place, so I could see what exactly gets launched in them. The root cause, not a cover-up. The problem is urgent, because the windows make work very hard.

Then I checked, and the logging has been running since 6 August. It has 87,279 entries.

The log sits at `~/.claude/scripts/_console_hider_log.txt`: 87,646 lines, 6.9 megabytes, opening line timestamped 6 August 20:55. It covers 37 days up to 12 September, and over those days it hid **2,359 windows a day**, which is **98.3 an hour**.

It also answers the question I was about to commission. By window class: Windows Terminal 66.8 percent, pseudo-console 32.3 percent, classic console 0.9 percent. By parent process, where it could resolve one: python 19,883, svchost 6,709.

And then the part that stung. The hider process is not running. Its autostart entry is gone. The last line in the log is 12 September, which is **58.8 days** ago. There is no scheduled task anywhere on this machine watching any of that.

So the trace existed and nobody watched its age. 87,279 hidden windows, then two months of silence, and the first report came from me noticing I could not type.

The last two lines before it went quiet show it still doing its job correctly:

```
keep#280 [rule] CASCADIA_HOSTING_WINDOW_CLASS (human-terminal)
hide#280 [hook] PseudoConsoleWindow (parent=pythonw.exe)
```

It was even distinguishing a terminal a human had opened from a window a robot spawned. Then it stopped, quietly.

As for which processes are guilty, that was settled on 24 July, and the answer is awkward: the main one is the coding agent I work in. On Windows it opens a visible console for every shell command it runs, and there is no setting to suppress that. The proof was a comparison rather than a theory: an idle session produces roughly no windows, a working session produces one per command. Which means that while a session is busy investigating the flashing, that session is the main source of the flashing.

Two plausible culprits were cleared before that, both empirically: the HP security software, which survived a full reboot with its services disabled and the windows kept coming, and the ChatGPT desktop app's monitoring, which I killed with no effect.

There are four sources in total, each with its own cure, and all four are already written down. The agent itself, which only a hider can mask. Scheduled tasks running in Interactive mode, which get flipped to S4U so that no window can physically be created. Watchdogs respawning dead daemons through a command shell. And a child executable launched from a script without the no-window flag, which was measured on my laptop on 10 August at **120 windows a minute**, every one of them `ssh-keygen`.

What I find most useful today is the comparison between two of my own fixes.

The S4U flip went in on 6 July. Four months later this node has 43 tasks under S4U, 11 Interactive, and exactly **one** that fires more often than hourly. The hider went in on 6 August and died after 37 days.

Same author, same month, opposite survival. The difference is not effort or care. The flip changes a **property of a thing** and then needs nobody; the hider is a **process** that has to stay alive. A property-fix survives by default. A process-fix decays, and it decays silently, which is why it needed a watchdog over its own log age and never got one.

One honest limit before I carry any of this to the hub. On this node the classic console class is 0.9 percent of hidden windows. On another of my machines in late August the same class was 99.9 percent of 54,032 events, and the cause there was something else entirely: watchdogs restarting two MCP daemons that nothing was using. Identical symptom, identical class of cure, completely different culprit. So the hub has to be measured on the hub, and a number carried across would be a guess dressed up as a measurement.

I do have one suspicion about the hub, and I am labelling it as such. Its Python installation lost its standard library on 2 October, every python routine there has been red since, and the cause was never found. Source number three is watchdogs that see a dead port and try to restart the daemon through a shell. With a dead interpreter the restart cannot succeed, so the watchdog tries again on the next tick, and every attempt is a window. The dates fit. What would settle it is a line from the watchdog log on that machine, and that machine is not this one.

What the brief becomes, then, is shorter than what I asked for. Run the existing audit on the hub, which is PowerShell and therefore survives the dead Python. Restart the hider and restore its autostart. Give its log a freshness check, because everything above came out of that log and nothing was watching it. Decide whether the one remaining frequent task is legitimately Interactive. And only then consider new logging, if the old log proves to be missing something.

The thing I will remember is not the number of windows. It is that I asked for an instrument that was already built, already measuring, and already dead, and the only reason I found out is that my keyboard stopped working.

### Do browsers eat my machines, and had I already built the thing that closes tabs

I think I have another problem: browsers are slowing my machines down too. I need to check urgently how much Firefox and Chrome cost me, and put measurements on them. I have an infinite number of tabs open.

And I need to remember whether I already made a routine or a service that closes all the open windows.

Maybe a separate service is worth building. Once or twice a day it closes the surplus windows and tabs in every browser, and perhaps restarts them. The important part is that it must not close windows that are in use in an active session right now.

I probably do not need to touch individual skills. Possibly worth noting that the deep-research skill opens a lot of windows. Although. Modifying skills is not the way.

So I measured, and I asked the second question first.

The browser is the single largest consumer on the machine I measured. Thirty processes, **4.59GB**, **29.2 percent** of physical memory. Our own agent is second at 3.04GB and 18.9 percent. The two together are **48.1 percent**, and free memory at that moment was **1.1GB of 15.7**, with the machine paging.

My first attempt almost gave the opposite answer. I looked for firefox, chrome and edge, got "not running" for all three, and nearly reported that browsers were innocent. The process is called `browser.exe`, which is Yandex Browser. Ranking by actual memory instead of expected names is what caught it, and that is the method rather than a detail.

The number worth carrying is the cost of a tab. Twenty renderer processes held 3.64GB, so **186MB per tab** on average, from 41MB for the lightest to 401MB for the heaviest. At that rate a hundred tabs is **18.2GB**, more than this whole machine has. And the tenfold spread between tabs means a count of tabs predicts almost nothing: the honest metric is the sum of renderer memory, not the number of tabs.

Then the second question, which is the one I am pleased I asked. Had I already built the cleaner?

No. There is no routine or service anywhere on this node that closes tabs or windows. The four scheduled tasks that mention browsers are a watchdog over the browser rail being alive, an installer check from the browser vendor, a Windows telemetry task and the default-browser agent that ships with Firefox. None of them closes anything.

But something close by name does exist, and it turned out to matter more than if the cleaner had existed. `agent_chrome_close.py`, 1,142 bytes, written on 19 August, with this in its own description: a SIGTERM or pkill is treated by Chrome as a crash, so it does not flush cookies to disk, which it batches on about a thirty second cycle, and the login does not survive the restart. The clean exit goes through the debug protocol instead.

Which lands directly on the "perhaps restarts them" half of my brief. If I had built the restarting service first and discovered that constraint the usual way, I would have discovered it by losing a login and spending a day working out why. The note was written three months ago by me, for a different purpose, and it was waiting.

The hardest part of my requirement also turns out to be solved next door. "Do not close windows that are in use right now" is the same problem the window hider already handles: it distinguishes a terminal a human opened from one a robot spawned, and its log proves it, 342 deliberate keeps against 87,279 hides over 37 days. The mechanism is identification by parentage and by which process created the thing, not by how recently it was touched or what its title says. That transfers.

One honest limit, because the numbers above are from the wrong machine for my question. This is a 15.7GB laptop running a different browser engine, and I asked about Firefox and Chrome on machines where the hub has 128GB. The shares do not carry across: 4.59GB is 29 percent here and under 4 percent there. Neither does "paging is the mechanism", which depends on there being 1.1GB free. What carries is the per-tab cost as an order of magnitude, and the method.

My own aside answers itself, and I think the version I dropped was right. I wondered about teaching the deep-research skill to open fewer windows, then said modifying skills is not the way. It is not: editing skills fixes the symptom in as many places as I have skills, and a service that closes what nothing is using fixes it in one place and keeps working for skills I have not written yet. One door, not twenty.

What I take from this is less about browsers. Three times this day I asked for something that already existed. This time I asked "did I already build this" inside the brief itself, and the question paid for itself immediately: not because the cleaner existed, it does not, but because the search surfaced a three month old constraint that would have cost me a day. The answer to "have I done this already" is worth asking even when it comes back no.

## How it connects

Four posts on 6 November. The fourth one closes the loop the first three opened.

The skills post asked for a skill that makes skills. Three already existed, and the measurement that named the problem was the spread: median skill body **9,315** bytes, largest **179,104**, smallest **671**, **267 to one**, with no written rule about size. Where a rule and a door exist, for descriptions capped at 400 characters, compliance is **96 percent**.

The sync post asked whether most files in the store ever change. **851** of **285,960** moved in thirty days, **0.3 percent** against a guess of 10 to 20, while the session store moves at **92.7 percent**. Two stores with opposite behaviour under one policy.

The windows post asked for logging on the flashing consoles. The logging had run since 6 August and held **87,279** hidden windows, **98.3** an hour, with window classes and parent processes broken out. It had also been dead since 12 September, **58.8 days**, with no process, no autostart and no watchdog.

The browsers post asked two questions, and the second one is new: had I already built the thing I am about to ask for?

⭐ That is the question the first three posts were quietly arguing for, and here it got asked before the work started rather than after.

The answer was no. No routine closes tabs or windows anywhere on the node. But the search returned something better than a finished cleaner: a 1,142 byte script from 19 August whose own description records that killing a browser loses the session, because cookies are flushed on a batch cycle and a signal reads as a crash. That constraint lands exactly on the "perhaps restart the browsers" half of the brief, and it would otherwise have been learned by losing a login.

So the ladder from the first three posts gets its fourth rung, and the rungs are in order of how easy they are to skip.

The **tool** is almost always already built. Three skill creators, a working sync, a window hider with 87 thousand entries, a browser-exit script.

The **rule** governing it usually is not written. No size policy for skills, no sentence separating archive from working set.

The **watchdog over the fix** is missing even when the rule exists. The windows skill says in its own text that the problem recurs; nothing watched whether the fix was still alive, and it had not been for two months.

And the **question that finds all three** is the cheapest of the four and the one most often skipped. Asked here, it cost one search and returned a day.

⭐⭐ The browser measurement also produced the day's clearest example of an instrument lying by construction. The first pass looked for firefox, chrome and edge, found none of them running, and would have reported browsers innocent. The process is named `browser.exe`, 30 processes, **4.59GB**, **29.2 percent** of physical memory, with our own agent second at **18.9 percent** and **1.1GB of 15.7** free. Ranking by actual memory rather than by expected names is the difference between the right answer and its opposite.

⚠️ And all four measurements state the limits of their own instruments, which is the habit worth keeping more than any of the numbers. Skill size is a proxy for complexity. Modification time is a proxy for change, and 58.8 percent of the vault carries a June timestamp that is a deployment date. Window composition is node-specific: 0.9 percent for one class here against 99.9 percent of 54,032 events elsewhere. And a per-tab cost of 186MB spans 41 to 401, so counting tabs predicts almost nothing and summing renderer memory is the only honest version.
