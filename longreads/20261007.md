# 7 October 2026: Three Research Passes, All of Them About Myself

*This file collects the posts of the day and the connections between them. More posts from this date are appended here as they go out.*

## What went out today

### A task for my Claude: profile me from my YouTube watch history

A task for my Claude.

I once had either a routine or a session. Most likely both: a session and a routine for pulling out all the YouTube videos I have watched, the list of them, and generating, or more precisely extracting, the YouTube transcripts of all those videos.

So that there is a better picture of who I am.

And then, out of all those transcripts, some alpha has to come out for me, some use for me, some description of who I am.

Because knowing what I watched on YouTube makes it easier to understand my strengths, my weaknesses, and possibly some of my mental leanings or disorders. And also to correct me. Or, possibly, to hand me something even more interesting, matched to what I ask about and what I am interested in.

### A research pass on whether I have to name the projects I incubated

I set a task for my Claude. I asked him to run a research pass on my work in the incubator.

We worked in an incubator for 10 years and incubated a great many projects over that time. I was COO/CTO there, and together with a team of engineers I helped the projects we incubated finish building out their backends: improving the product, optimising it, and for example, if a project was doing machine learning and burning a great deal of money on training models, helping optimise the training. We also helped write smart contracts, run security audits and so on.

Here is the question. Now that I am planning to take a job with a new employer, do I have to list all the projects I incubated, or might that fall under an NDA? With some of them we signed an NDA, with some we did not. I am afraid of naming a project.

My Claude has to tell me whether it will be a red flag if I do not name the projects. I can, however, give the metrics we improved in those startups. Or, if I do not say exactly who we improved those metrics for, will the mention of the metrics look like a red flag by itself? For example, I could be told: how can anyone believe your numbers if you cannot name the company you helped?

I want my Claude to go into this in more detail. In general, a research pass is needed on this subject.

### A task for my Claude: the top 10 hyped topics in AI, and why

A task for my Claude. About hype topics.

Hype topics in artificial intelligence, and the startups, public speakers, companies or engineers who use those topics actively. To put it simply, there is a hype thing. What usually happens now is that some hype topic appears, for example alignment in artificial intelligence: how far artificial intelligence really follows the rules and does not adapt itself, does not deceive the user about what it actually is, does not try to look better than it is, does not tell the user what the user wants to hear. That is just a topic picked at random, one I think is possibly hype enough.

I would like my Claude to find the top 10 most hyped topics and explain to me why they are the most hyped: why they raise the most money, why they are talked about the most, why they are heard about the most often, and so on.

What am I looking for these hype topics for? So that I can also make content on these hype subjects somehow and get attention to my own person, to my GitHub, or get a job. So a hype topic is a very important thing.

## How it connects

Three posts on this date, and all three are requests for research about the same person rather than about the world. The first asks what a consumption record says about him, the second what a decade of work can be claimed to have done when the clients cannot be named, and the third which subjects would make that work visible at all. Capture, attribution, attention.

The first one is the third return to a standing idea, which is itself the interesting part.

The idea first appeared in August as an integration request: move the whole YouTube history into the vault and run it through RAG, so that what was watched in each period becomes queryable. A second post that month sharpened it into a selection rule, and that rule is the one worth carrying: find the authors who actually matter by what was watched, not by what was subscribed to. Subscriptions are a wish list; the history is the record. A third post at the end of August changed the shape of the work rather than its content, turning the one-off session into a daily drip, on the grounds that an elephant is eaten in pieces.

This post changes the purpose. The earlier three were about capture and about finding people to learn from. This one points the same corpus back at its owner: strengths, weaknesses, mental leanings, and then correction. It is the same input with the subject swapped.

Worth noting next to the audio post from two days earlier, because the two are the same instinct in opposite directions. That one asked how material gets into a person; this one asks what the record of consumed material says about the person. Both treat consumption history as evidence rather than as a log.

The limit is plain in the post itself: it is a task, not a result. A profile derived from a watch history is a claim about someone, and the honest version of it says which part is measured and which part is inference.

The second post hits the same wall from the employment side, and this is where the pair becomes useful rather than coincidental. Ten years of incubator work produced real numbers, and a confidentiality agreement can make the only verifiable half of them unquotable. So the question is not whether the work happened; it is which evidence survives being stripped of its client. A metric with the company removed and a profile inferred from behaviour have the same defect: both ask the reader to trust an attribution that cannot be checked.

Worth stating the part that is not solved by writing more carefully. Withholding a name is honest, and it still reads as a gap to the person on the other side of the table, which is precisely why the question went out as a research request rather than as a decision. Whether the gap or the disclosure is the larger risk is a question about how employers actually read such a CV, and that is a thing to go and find out, not to reason about from a desk.

The third post is the same move applied to the choice of subject, and the part worth taking from it is the measurement, not the ambition. Hype is normally judged by feel, which means it is judged after the fact and usually too late. The request replaces feel with three observable things: money raised on the topic, volume of discussion, and how often it is actually heard. A subject that wins on all three is a different object from one that merely sounds current, and the difference is checkable.

The motive is stated without decoration: content on those subjects, attention to his own work and GitHub, or a job. Naming that out loud is the honest version, and it also bounds the request, because a topic list built to attract attention is useful exactly to the degree that the attention it attracts is the kind that converts. Nothing in the post claims that it does. The random example given, alignment, is itself a decent test case: it is genuinely a research field and genuinely a hype word, and the three criteria above are what would separate the two.

## The numbers

Three posts on this date. None carries a measurement of its own; what follows for the first is our own instrumentation of the task it describes, and it is the part that makes the request concrete.

The feed accessible from the signed-in YouTube pages yields 3,229 videos. That is not fifteen years of history: the page hands over the recent window, and the full archive comes out of Google Takeout instead. Subtitles are the other wall. Where captions are blocked, the fallback is audio plus local transcription, which costs compute rather than access.

Not measured: how much of the corpus has actually been processed, and whether any profile has been derived from it. The task above is a request with no output attached yet, and recording it as anything else would be false.

The second post has two numbers in it and both are durations rather than outcomes: 10 years in the incubator, and a role, COO/CTO, held during them. The metrics referred to exist but are deliberately not reproduced here, and the whole point of the post is that they cannot be attributed in public. Nothing in it reports how many projects were incubated, how many were covered by an agreement, or what any specific improvement was. That is not a gap in the write-up; it is the subject of it.

Also not measured, and this is the open item: how an employer actually reads an unnamed client. That is the thing the requested research has to answer, and it has not come back.

The third post contains one number and it is a request size, not a finding: a list of 10. No topic has been ranked yet, no amount of money raised has been attributed to any subject, and no discussion volume has been counted. The three criteria are named, which is the useful part; the values behind them are empty. Treating this post as a verdict on what is hyped would be reading an empty table as data.

All three requests on this date share one property worth recording plainly: each one is a task with no output attached. Three research passes were commissioned and none has returned.
