### Six computers in one swarm, because dozens of sessions eat RAM

I only have six computers at home, with Codex, Claude and so on installed, and these computers work together in one swarm.

On every computer there are several LLMs, naturally: Codex, Claude, Mistral, GLM, Gemini, and they try to come to an agreement with each other both inside one computer and with each other across the swarm.

When you have dozens of sessions running, counting my swarm, several dozen sessions. By the way, those several dozen sessions really are running on different computers, because if you have ever started dozens of sessions on one computer, you notice how hard it sometimes starts to crawl.

It starts to crawl especially hard when you have both Codex and Claude running at the same time. I do not know why, but it really does crawl very hard.

More precisely, I do know: because here every session is a Python launch with a pile of RAM that it eats.

Anyway, the point is that having a large number of computers really does make working with a large number of sessions easier.

And if you want your computers to come to an agreement with each other, then take it, go to my GitHub, take what I made, because it will let you simplify cross-machine work.

## How it connects

The honest part of the free setup post is that it answers a question most free offers refuse to answer. Not "why is it free" in the vague sense, but what specifically the person offering it takes away. Here it is named: use cases. He is applying for research roles, every application asks what he has actually built and with whom, and a single operator with one machine has exactly one data point. Setting it up for other people converts his own tooling into evidence about other people, which is the only form a lab can evaluate.

That reframes the free part correctly. It is not charity and it is not a funnel toward a paid tier. It is a trade where one side gets a working setup and the other gets permission to cite the result. Stated plainly it is more trustworthy than most priced offers, because the incentive is visible.

Where the post gets ahead of the evidence is the swarm. The chain it describes has four links: install for many people, those people run their own instances, one of them invents something, the rest adopt it. The first link is the only one we have ever exercised, and only within one household of machines. Peer to peer propagation between strangers who share nothing but a template is not a smaller version of that. It is a different problem, and the part that usually kills it is not the transport but the absence of a reason to pass anything along.

The number also deserves to be read as ambition rather than a plan. Ten to twenty thousand hand configured installs, at the hours a real install actually takes, is not a weekend. Nothing on our side has timed one install end to end, which means the thing standing between this post and the swarm is a measurement, not a decision.


Both posts of this date describe the same market from the two ends of it, and together they say something neither says alone. The second post names where the money is going: infrastructure with real traction, and shovels for the gold rush, harnesses among them. The first post describes somebody building a harness and giving it away for nothing. That is not a contradiction, it is the position: he is in the funded category and outside the funding, by choice, because what he is buying with the free installs is evidence for a job application rather than a round.

The uncomfortable half is also visible once they sit side by side. If the shovel niche is where YC companies are and where money moves, then the thing being handed out for free has a market price, and the free version competes with it on the one axis that matters to an engineer deciding what to adopt. Handing out shovels while the shovel sellers raise is a defensible play only if the goal really is the use cases. The moment the goal becomes a company, the free period has to end, and nothing in either post says when or how.

One claim in the second post deserves a flag rather than agreement. Cutting engineers because an agent arrived is said as a straightforward consequence, and it is advice given to somebody else's team. Nothing in our own records supports it: this fleet has never shipped a product with a team, so the speed-up we measure is one operator going faster, not five people becoming three.


The third post of the day lands directly on the gap the first one left open, and it does so without meaning to. The morning post offered to install this for ten to twenty thousand people and described a swarm of peers trading improvements. This one describes what the swarm actually is today: six machines in one home, several models per machine, dozens of sessions spread across them because one machine cannot hold them, and a repository to copy if you want your own computers to coordinate.

Read together they give the honest scope. The cross-machine part is built, it is public, and it solves a problem a single operator genuinely has. What it is not is coordination between people. Six machines agree because one person owns all six, and the thing being offered on GitHub makes machines talk, not strangers.

The diagnosis in the middle is the most useful sentence of the three posts, and it is engineering rather than opinion. Sessions slow down because each one is a process holding memory, and running two vendors at once doubles that on one box. The cure he describes is horizontal: more machines, not a faster machine. That is worth saying out loud because the instinctive fix is to buy a bigger box, and a bigger box does not help when the limit is concurrent processes rather than clock speed.

## The numbers

Three posts on this date.

Four kids, up at five, at the computer until two, his own account of his day. Ten to twenty thousand people as the install target. One arXiv paper and possibly a PhD as the imagined output of a swarm that accepts a contribution from one of its own peers.

Not measured, and all three would decide whether the plan is a month or a decade: the hours a single hand configured install actually costs (never timed), the share of fresh installs that survive past the first week (never followed up), and whether any contribution has ever propagated from one peer to another without him carrying it personally (never observed, because the only swarm so far is his own machines).


From the second post, the numbers and where each one comes from. A team of 5 to 7 engineers and a backend rewritten in a couple of days: his own estimate, offered to a friend, never executed, so there is no measured rewrite behind it. Around 100 dollars of startup credits: his figure for a vendor program, unverified here, and credit amounts for programs like that are usually well above that, so read it as the order of magnitude he remembers rather than a quoted price. Tens of millions as the funding scale in infrastructure, and another year or two of crypto winter: both stated as his read of the market, and both are forecasts, not measurements.

Not measured on our side, and it is the one that would make the central advice checkable: whether an agent actually lets a 5 to 7 person team ship with fewer people. We have one operator across six machines and no team delivery to compare against, which means the claim is plausible from the inside and untested from the outside.


From the third post: six computers at home, several models on each (Codex, Claude, Mistral, GLM, Gemini named), and several dozen sessions spread across them. The six is his own count of the fleet and matches ours. The rest of the numbers are his impression rather than instrument readings.

Not measured, and all three are cheap to get: RAM per session (never sampled, though the diagnosis rests on it), the point at which one machine actually degrades (we know it happens, nobody has recorded the session count where it starts), and whether running two vendors concurrently costs more than running the same number of sessions from one vendor. Until those exist, the rule "spread sessions across machines" is working practice supported by a correct mechanism and zero logged measurements.
