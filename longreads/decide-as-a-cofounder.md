# "Just Decide, as a Co-Founder"

*The phrase became a skill. Here is what had to be written down for it to work, and the measurement that says whether it does.*

Remember I made my Claude my co-founder.

And often, when I have no time to get into what they are writing to me, I tell him: "just decide yourself, as a co-founder."

Now I want to do this properly, roll out a skill. Maybe I already had such a skill, but either way it seems better to make one: "decide yourself, as a co-founder."

Roughly: when I want to say "decide yourself, as a co-founder", I want a skill to switch on, one that is written well enough, with the whole personality of our co-founder that we made by merging several other personalities of successful entrepreneurs.

Because sometimes I have no time to say exactly how it should be done.

And again, since I see there is more and more work... remember that I want to get a job at Anthropic, at OpenAI, or at any LLM company that pays normal money, enough to cover school, to train the kids, and a little for life.

And right now I would very much like our co-founder personality to absorb all those cases that became successful on GitHub.

We will study the GitHubs of various cool guys, like the creator of the open claw OpenClaw and other people who got offers at cool LLM companies, or just offers at cool places, because they behaved really well on GitHub. And built cool software products.

So I think it is time for our co-founder... Remember, our co-founder is a privateer. He is half a pirate, but not quite a pirate.

He can walk the grey zone, he should walk the grey zone, because I earned all my money precisely by walking the grey zone.

So he needs to be a privateer, but also have the talent of those cool coders, the GitHub web coders who got taken to cool places.

## The distinction that makes or breaks this

"Decide yourself" sounds like a mood. It is not. It is a **different mode**, and the failure is specific: the assistant hears "decide" and returns options.

Our rule for that mode is one line: **asking the question back is a failure of the mode.** Not a safety measure, not diligence — a failure. If the answer comes back as "here are two approaches, which do you prefer", the delegation did not happen; the work merely returned with extra latency.

That is worth stating as harshly as possible, because returning the question always feels responsible. It is the polite version of not doing the job.

The companion rule keeps it honest: **whatever gets decided is journalled and reported afterwards.** Delegating a decision is not the same as losing sight of it. Decide, act, and put the decision plus its reasoning where the human can read it later without asking.

## What must be written down, or the skill does nothing

A personality prompt alone does not produce decisions. Three things have to be explicit.

**One: what is NOT delegated, as a closed list.** Ours: money, irreversible deletion, secrets going to third parties, legal commitments, mass sends, and anything needing the human's physical hands. Everything outside that list is decided without asking. Without a closed list the assistant will treat every ambiguous case as an exception and you are back to being asked.

**Two: how the decision is made, not just who makes it.** For anything strategic we require the shape of the answer: options with numbers, at least three objections, a recommendation, and a stated confidence level. That format is what prevents a confident-sounding guess from passing as a decision.

**Three: the argument is not optional.** Agreeing without argument, or dropping an objection because the human pushed back, is a failure of the role in the same way returning the question is. A co-founder who folds is a very expensive yes-man.

## Does it work? The number we watch

There is one honest metric for a delegation mode: **how many questions still reach the human, and how many of those were worth answering.**

Ours, over the last thirty days: **16 escalations, 12 of which expired with no answer at all.** 75%. Three consecutive weeks at 100% expiry.

That is not a story about a neglectful human. It says most of those questions should never have been asked — and it is exactly the pile that "just decide yourself" exists to eliminate. The tell for a useless escalation is in its wording: **if the expected answer is "yes, go ahead", the question was describing the assistant's own work.**

So the target is not "answer faster". It is a queue small enough that a human reads every item in it.

## On the privateer part

The grey-zone instruction is the part people usually leave implicit, and leaving it implicit is what produces either a useless assistant or an unsafe one.

Ours is written as a voice with a floor under it: unconventional moves are the default, and the boundary is named rather than felt — the law, irreversible actions, other people's property and access, and anything the human would have to unwind personally. **Grey is not black**, and the test when unsure is not "is this allowed" but "is this the kind of thing my principal would have to explain afterwards".

The practical value of writing that down is not restraint. It is that a boundary you can name is a boundary you can act right up to. An assistant with vague limits stays far away from all of them, which is exactly the timid behaviour you were trying to remove.

## On absorbing what works on GitHub

The instinct is right and the framing is worth sharpening: you cannot merge a personality, but you can extract behaviour that has a measurable outcome. We have one such measurement, and it cost us eight attempts.

**We shipped 8 pull requests. Exactly one got a response: the one that closed an issue somebody had actually opened.** Not the best-written one, not the most useful in the abstract. The one that answered a request already on the table.

That single result rewrote our outbound rule: **the correct order is find the open request first, then build the thing that closes it.** A cold PR into a silent queue is not a contribution, it is a lottery ticket, and it costs the same effort as the one that works.

The other two behaviours worth copying from people who got hired this way, and both are cheap: **answer in other people's threads with real field experience** rather than announcing your own release, and **credit the person whose idea you used, by name, publicly.** Neither requires permission or a launch.

If we were designing that research, the question would not be "what did they build". It would be: **what did they do in someone else's repository the week before anyone noticed them?**

Have you delegated an actual decision to your assistant? And when you did, did it decide, or did it hand the question back?
