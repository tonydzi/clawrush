### Being held hostage by your own staff, and where the dependency actually went

Anton writes about how uncomfortable it is to be a hostage to your own staff. An assistant who knows a great deal about you. An assistant who has worked for you so long that firing them feels wrong, though the salary is substantial. Staff who are like a suitcase with no handle: useful in a way, and already too heavy to carry. He had that story himself, with personal assistants and employees he did not need, with constant friction, where good people make mistakes and you take your anger out on them, or else you treat them delicately while being ready to throttle them, because they are unreliable and keep getting things wrong.

Then the turn: how wonderful that AI arrived and all of this gets solved by building yourself personal assistants. He made a second brain, with one shared memory across every Claude and Codex session and everything happening around him in life. Now he has an assistant who is simultaneously a programmer and a backend developer and can book tickets. He still has assistants and colleagues, he says, but the relationship got much more comfortable, because a lot of the work is now outsourced to synthetic ones. And if you want a virtual co-founder, it is all on GitHub.

**The first thing worth checking is the promise, because most "it is all on GitHub" lines in this corpus do not survive contact.** This one does. The starter kit holds **235** skills and a README, pushed yesterday, with **14** stars and **5** forks. Around it sit a dozen other public repositories, several updated this week. The artefact is real and current, which is not the usual outcome.

**The second is the description of the assistant, and here one of the three examples does not hold.** On the shelf there are **282** skills and **264** routines in the register, so the outsourcing is real: ten skills for code and review, ten for leads and the CRM, sixteen for content, seven for mail and calendar. But skills matching travel, tickets, hotels or booking: **zero**. The assistant does not book tickets today, and a human still does that part. ⚠️ That is a count of tooling rather than of what gets done by hand, and it covers the shared shelf rather than one machine, but it is the one claim in the post a reader could test in a minute.

**And then the part that matters more than either.** The thesis is that the hostage relationship is solved: depend on synthetic assistants, who do not sulk, do not need raises and never have to be fired.

Three posts from the previous two days measure what that dependency looks like in practice. The CRM backend is built and the selection model was decided on 4 July, and the outreach ledger reads 530 touches in September, **11** in October, **zero** in November, last touch 2 October. Eight research orders exist on how to run the community, five of them placed for two questions, and the 3 November ask drew eight replies with five concrete offers, all still unanswered. The Telegram funnel brief of 4 November turns out to be the same brief as 13 August, **83 days** apart, with a measured peak and a measured collapse in between.

Not one of those stalls was caused by staff. Every one of them happened inside a system made entirely of synthetic assistants, built exactly the way this post recommends, and not one of them raised a hand. That is the asymmetry worth naming. A human assistant who stops working gets noticed within a day, because they are in the room and the silence is awkward. A ledger that stops being written gets noticed after **34 days**, because nothing in the system is capable of embarrassment.

So the accurate version of the thesis is narrower than the post's, and better. Synthetic assistants removed the interpersonal cost of dependency, which was real and was the part that actually hurt: the anger, the delicacy, the suitcase with no handle. They did not remove the dependency itself. The bottleneck moved from "my staff will not do it" to "nobody asked me whether it was done", and the second version is quieter, which is precisely why it ran unnoticed for a month.

Which is why his own correction inside the post is the strongest line in it: he still has assistants and colleagues, and the relationship improved because the load moved. That is a claim the measurements support. Replacement is not.

### Slow machines are a gift, and a quarter of a million files is why

Anton asks what the problem with slow computers is, and really: how do you scale your AI agents? You can scale them by adding power. But he has a second brain for his agents, and he experiments with making them autonomous, with a personality, a character, persistence. One of those experiments is his co-founder. And because the storage his co-founder lives in keeps growing, his computer keeps slowing down.

He bought a Mac Studio recently and does not regret it, but it is not helping much, because if the architecture is crooked then even a powerful machine does not let you work faster. Then the turn: slow computers are good. Low compute is good, because it forces you to work out how to optimise the architecture itself. Constant reindexing of the second brain is eating resources right now. He will not scale his co-founder without fixing the architecture, and adding machines, of which he now has eight running the fleet, only postpones the moment he has to face it. Buying servers does not teach you to write leaner code.

**The first thing worth saying is that this post closes a question he asked nine days ago.** On 26 October he wrote that the machines drag badly at 10, 20, 30 sessions, that the computer is powerful and still drags, and that he does not know what to blame and is looking for what is dragging. This post names it: reindexing. Most briefs this week restated themselves, some after 83 days. This one resolved, and resolution deserves to be marked as clearly as repetition.

**The second is that the measurement confirms him, and gives the crookedness a shape.** What a reindex actually walks, counted on this machine: **260,435** markdown files, **2,845.8 MB**. And the breakdown is the finding. Raw conversation exports are **120,464** files, **46.3%** of everything, 1.3 GB on their own. People cards are 43,757. Projects 25,983. Decisions 18,704. And the distilled layers the whole second brain exists for, the concepts, insights and bridges, come to **3,350 files: about 1.3%**.

So the index is built over a quarter of a million files of which roughly one in eighty is distillate. That is not a hardware problem at any price point. Adding RAM multiplies the speed at which you walk 260,435 files; it does not reduce the number. The two levers that do are both about selection rather than power: stop indexing what is not distillate, and make reindexing incremental so that cost scales with what changed rather than with the size of the corpus.

⚠️ **And the uncomfortable part, which is ours rather than his.** We already have the rule this breaks. Our own standing note says only signal goes into the vault and the RAG, not raw material, and that distillation beats volume. The rule exists. Forty-six percent of the vault is raw conversation dumps. So this is not an open architectural question: it is a known rule with nothing enforcing it, which is the same failure class as the outreach ledger that recorded 34 days of silence and told nobody. Three posts in two days have now landed on that same shape.

Two small corrections for the record, and on one of them he is right and we are wrong. His 26 October post says 128GB of RAM, this one says 120GB, both about the same Mac Studio; a reader comparing the two will notice. And he counts eight computers while our node registry lists six, because the Mac Studio bought on 27 October and the Mac Pro were never added to it. On the machine count the instrument is stale, not the author.

Which makes his central claim stronger than he put it. "Slow computers explain everything to me" is not a consolation for having slow computers. It is a description of what a constraint does: it makes you look at the thing you are actually doing, which here turns out to be indexing a quarter of a million files to retrieve three thousand.

### Cursor is crawling on the Mac Pro, so monitoring goes on every machine first

Cursor started dragging badly on the Mac Pro, brutally so. Anton wants system monitoring on every computer, urgently, to work out what exactly is loading the system. He needs it to optimise his own second brain and his own development. His own Python scripts may be doing it, or third-party ones such as ScreenPipe.

His eight-step plan: put system monitoring on the Mac Pro and then on every machine, to see which processes are dragging them down. Check whether his own software, scripts and development are the ones dragging. On the Mac Pro stop Screenpipe completely, hard, but do not delete it yet. After stopping it, check whether the slowdown is gone. Prepare for a clean full removal if Screenpipe turns out to be the problem. Study how to remove software from a Mac without leftovers: LaunchAgents, LaunchDaemons, plists, caches, background services. Then audit every computer starting with the Hub, remove old and unused software, keep only what is genuinely needed. Then roll the monitoring and the cleanup approach out across the whole fleet.

**This is the sixth post on slow machines in sixteen days, and the first one that runs an experiment instead of proposing a cause.** The sequence: 21 October, six computers in one swarm. 22 October, 128GB of RAM and it still crawls. 26 October, the pain of the day, machines drag at 10, 20, 30 sessions, and he does not know what to blame. 30 October, Screenpipe is letting him down badly. Earlier today, slow machines are a gift and the cause is reindexing rather than hardware. Five posts reasoned about the cause. This one proposes to stop one thing and look at whether the symptom goes away, which is the only step in the series that can be wrong in a way you would notice.

**On the main request he is right and the fleet is not.** There are **264** routines in the register. Exactly one touches processes: `Proc Patrol`, and its own description is "kills known zombies from a list". It does not measure anything, it executes a kill list. There is no resource monitoring on any machine in the fleet. The two performance investigations that exist were both done by hand on a single day, 7 July, on two Windows machines. So "put monitoring on every computer" is new work rather than a request for something already built, and that absence is exactly why the last six posts had to guess.

⚠️ **And the two investigations that do exist found a different culprit each time, neither of them Screenpipe.** On the laptop the root was Claude Desktop left open for days: one instance running 31 hours had **324** child processes and 18.5GB, with **28** accumulated copies of the Telegram MCP at 5.4GB. On the hub the finding was `TextInputHost` burning **57 hours of CPU in 3 days** of uptime, a known Windows looping bug wasting an entire core, while the hardware sat at 19% RAM and 10% CPU. The note from that day carries a standing instruction: check TextInputHost **first** at any future slowdown. So the fleet already has a documented order of suspects, and Screenpipe is not at the top of it.

⚠️ **Which makes the step order the one thing worth changing in the plan.** Step 1 installs the monitoring, step 3 stops Screenpipe completely. If the stop happens before the baseline exists, the result answers only one question, did the slowdown go away, and loses the other, what was actually consuming the machine. Run in the right order it is just as cheap and gives both answers: measure for a few hours, stop one thing, measure again. Stopping first is a coin flip with no record.

**The cost of stopping has a precise boundary, and his "do not delete it yet" is the correct half of it.** The Screenpipe free tier keeps history for **24 hours** only. Two routines on the hub, `Screenpipe-Vault-Harvest-Night` at 03:40 and `-Day` at 15:40, pull a day of speech into the vault, and without that pull the transcripts are gone permanently; a gap longer than 24 hours is unrecoverable and the harvest script writes an alarm line about it. On top sits an alpha miner over that speech and a daily ambient note in the vault. ⭐ But those routines run on the **hub**, and the machine he is stopping is the **Mac Pro**, so the stop he describes does not break the pipeline. The risk sits in steps 7 and 8: the audit starts with the Hub and removes "old and unused software", and on the hub Screenpipe is not unused. It is the supplier for two routines, a miner and a daily note, with ten `screenpipe-*` skills on a shelf of 279 pointed at it.

**His distrust of the tool is already paid for by a measurement, which strengthens the case rather than weakening it.** On 30 September the hub `Callisto7200` capture stream was dying `(disconnected)` about 200ms after start while the Screenpipe `/health` endpoint reported `active (last activity: 0s ago)` for **5.4 days**, because "last activity" there is the age of the last start rather than of the last data. That became an upstream bug report and a per-channel watchdog of our own. A tool whose health endpoint lied for five days is a reasonable first suspect even if the history says to check something else first.

⚠️ **Two debts this post uncovers, both ours.** The deep research he asked for publicly on **30 October**, on tools equivalent to Screenpipe, is **not in the register**. The last order recorded there is **2 October**, which is **33 days** without a single one, and he is now preparing to remove Screenpipe without the comparison he commissioned. And the task `task-2026-09-15-screenpipe-capture-watchdog-and-screen-digest`, which would catch exactly this class, has been **open at P1 for 50 days**.

⭐ One correction where the instrument is stale rather than the author. The Mac Pro, the machine the plan starts from, is **not in the node registry at all**. The registry lists six machines and neither the Mac Pro nor the Mac Studio bought on 27 October is among them, a known gap since that purchase. "Roll it out across the whole fleet" currently means six machines that are not the one with the problem.

⚠️ One note on wording for the record. "AutoMozg" does not appear in any active vault note, only inside archived versions of Codex sessions on the hub, so a reader will not know what it refers to.

### Daily GitHub routines again, and a report on what each model change actually did

Anton wants to put all the GitHub routines on the MacBook 16 back to once a day. He had lowered the frequency to save tokens, but every session is now on cheaper models, so the daily cadence can come back. He also wants to check the effect of those model swaps across every session tied to GitHub activity on that machine, and on other machines if needed: Opus to Sonnet, or Sonnet to Haiku. And he wants a report per routine: routine, which model it was on, what its effectiveness was, which model it moved to, what its effectiveness became. He says he is doing more and more measurement of each routine's effectiveness, and that every routine should have a clear measurable goal so you can tell whether it got better or worse after the swap.

**The instrument he is asking for half exists, and the half that is missing is the model.** There is a routine value register, `routine_value.json`, holding **510** entries with fields for tokens over 7 days, runs over 7 days, a verdict and a strength-of-evidence rating. What it does not have is a model field at all. The model lives hard-coded inside the scripts: a scan of the script shelf finds `sonnet` written in **22** places, `opus` in **12**, `fable` in **3** and `haiku` in **2**. So the report he describes, routine against model before and after, cannot be read out of the register today; it has to be assembled by grepping the code. ⚠️ And the Sonnet to Haiku move he names is barely present in the code at all: two occurrences against twenty-two.

⭐ **His main claim is not a feeling, it is measurable, and the measurement agrees with him.** Of those 510 routines, only **118**, about **23%**, carry evidence of a result; the register calls that "benefit proven". **260** of them, **51%**, are marked "no instrument" at all, and **123** more, **24%**, have only a trace of a run, meaning a log line proving the thing executed and nothing proving it did anything. So three routines in four have no measurable goal, which is exactly the gap he just named out loud.

⚠️ **But the swap he is planning can only touch a fifth of them.** The register records which rail each routine burns: **412 of 510 are marked `0-LLM`**, meaning they never call a model, and only **98** sit on an LLM rail. Changing Opus to Sonnet can physically change the outcome for those 98. For the other 412 the swap is a no-op, and if their effectiveness moves after it, the cause is somewhere else.

⚠️ **The counter he would judge by currently prints an absurdity, and that has to be said before any before-and-after comparison.** Total tokens across 7 days come to **163 million**, and every one of them is attributed to a single routine, `lead-thread-reply`, which is itself marked `0-LLM` and should not be burning model tokens at all. That same routine appears in the register **twice** with identical figures. Meanwhile total runs across 7 days are **24**, and only **2** routines out of 510 show any runs at all. A before-and-after measured on this counter would compare noise with noise.

⚠️ **And the register has no Mac in it.** All **510** entries are marked `home=win`. There are zero entries with `home=mac`, so the machine this post is about, the MacBook 16, is not represented in the measurement at all. Of the GitHub routines our own code owns, seven appear in the register: config git snapshot, git daemon, daemon guard, offsite push, git sync, transport monitor, boot self-report. Exactly **one** of those seven, the config snapshot, is rated "benefit proven"; the rest read "no instrument", "watchdog silent" or "trace of a run only".

**The snapshot is also 57 days old**, taken on 10 September, so "the report per routine" today returns a two-month-old picture of a Windows hub.

Which makes the brief sharper than it was written, and in his favour. The model swap is the smaller half: at most 98 routines can respond to it, and nothing currently records which model any of them used. The measurable goal is the larger half, and his own number for it is 23%. ⭐ Vendor routing, incidentally, is machine-readable and has been since 6 August, in `llm_routing.json` at version 1.3.0: which bucket pays for which class of work is written down. The choice of model *inside* Claude is a rule in the canon with nothing recording the choice per routine, which is precisely the field his report needs.

### Restore the voice pipeline, and the date everything stopped turns out to be one date

Anton wants to analyse and restore the current process for regularly working through all his voice recordings: the fresh ones from the past week need transcribing, diarising and breaking down. He was at an ayahuasca ceremony on Friday night into Saturday and may go again on Saturday night into Sunday; after the ceremony he had a lot of thoughts which he dictated into recordings, and he wants to formalise and keep them and later make an article out of it about how he, as a scientist and an engineer, went through an ayahuasca ceremony. He also wants to extract every task and thought from the Plaud recordings, split them by direction and hand them out to contractors. And he wants to recall how the business processes for handling such recordings are built, and build out whatever is missing.

**The process is built, and built in four layers plus a separate Plaud branch.** In the routine register: `Claude Voice Harvest` collects his voice notes from the chat into the session pipeline, `Claude Voice Triage Daily` breaks them down one recording to one session, `Claude Voice Backstop` picks up if the daily run overslept, and `Claude Voice Dispatch Watch` checks that recordings were actually dispatched. Alongside: `HubVoiceTranscribe` runs local whisper on the GPU for free, `Ear-Transcribe-Nightly` turns the ambient WAV into searchable vault text, `Claude Voice AB Measure` grades transcription quality. The Plaud branch has its own engine, `_imports/plaud/plaud_pull.py`, seven `plaud-*` skills and an index note in the vault.

**Two of the three operations he lists are not ours to build.** The Plaud notes carry `transcriber: plaud` and the summaries are already speaker-separated, "Speaker 1" and "Speaker 2", with per-recording titles and durations. Transcription and diarisation arrive done; the part that belongs to us is the third one, the breakdown.

⚠️ **But every capture branch has the same symptom, and the dates cluster.** Plaud in the vault: **17** files, the newest dated **30 September**. Voice transcripts from the chat: **1,398** files, the newest **1 October**. Ambient notes: **31** files, the newest **2 October**. Today is 6 November. So "the fresh recordings from the past week" have nothing behind them: the past week produced no output in any of the three branches, and the most recent output of any of them is **35 days** old.

⚠️⚠️ **And the cause has a timestamp.** On **2 October at about 20:12** the Python 3.12 installation on the hub lost its standard library. Every new interpreter start fails with `init_fs_encoding ... no codec search functions registered: can't find encoding`. The signature of the damage is specific: the `.py` files went, `Lib` was left holding exactly one entry, `Scripts`, `libs`, `include` and `python312.zip` disappeared, while `DLLs` stayed intact and the only surviving packages were the 31 with native binaries inside. The cause was never established: Defender reported zero detections in 24 hours, there were no installer events, and the disk had 183GB free. In the same measurement the scheduler showed the inbox robot, the content distributor and the lead-thread watcher all failing. Every one of these capture pipelines is Python.

⚠️ **The hub has also been silent on the machine bus since that day.** Its inbound file was last written on **2 October**, which is **35 days**. The last thing it managed to say before going quiet was a report listing nine of its own routines as silent for between 225 and 794 hours.

⭐ **Which makes this the answer to four days of posts rather than one more request.** The outreach ledger's last touch is **2 October**. The deep research register's newest order is **2 October**. The voice pipeline, the Plaud branch and the ambient notes all stop between 30 September and 2 October. These are not four separate failures with four separate explanations. They are one machine that has not been able to start a Python script since the evening of 2 October, and the one component that would have raised its hand, the bus report, is produced by the same machine.

⚠️ And the honest part about the instrument on my side: the line "the bus was silent" has been printing in the status of every case I have processed this week, and I read it as noise each time. The indicator was telling the truth for 35 days.

**So the brief he wrote is correct and its order is the only thing that changes.** There is nothing to restore in the breakdown process until the interpreter on the hub can run; the layers themselves, including the backstop, are all written in the language that stopped working. ⚠️ One detail worth naming inside the plan: `Claude Voice Backstop`, the layer whose job is to catch a missed daily run, is scheduled as **One Time Only** rather than daily, so even on a healthy machine there is nothing standing behind the daily breakdown.

⚠️ Two boundaries for the record. The Plaud notes are marked `privacy: unreviewed` and contain other people's conversations in full, including business details that were never meant to leave the room; nothing from them travels outward, which is the constraint any article has to work inside. And the ceremony itself is his own subject matter, published under his own name, so it stays as he wrote it.
