# 4 November 2026: The Same Post, 83 Days Later

## What went out today

### I want to start another Telegram broadcast, and I wrote this same post 83 days ago

Anton wants to start another Telegram broadcast. The focus goes first to whoever reacts fastest: invite them into the Telegram channels where he shows how he uses Codex, Cursor and Windsurf, and invite them into his English channel about AI. Ask them directly, "guys, what are you doing, what are you designing?". The Telegram funnel should run continuously. These processes need restarting. Do targeted broadcasts and clean out the unnecessary messages.

**The recall found the post he wrote before this one, and it is nearly the same post.** On 13 August he published: *"I need to restore my Telegram work, and run it without stopping. I used to have this dialled in, it needs restoring."* Today, **83 days later**: *"The Telegram funnel should run continuously. These processes need restarting."* Same verb, same promise, same gap between them.

And unlike most such echoes, this one has a measurement in between. The outreach ledger, counted by the dates inside the records: **172** touches in July, **353** in August, **530** in September, **11** in October, **zero** in November. The last recorded touch is **2 October**, which on the day of this post is **33 days** of silence.

So the August post worked. He restored the Telegram work and September became the peak of the year, running at about **17.7** touches a day, which is above the 5 to 10 a day he set as the target in a different post the day before this one. Then it stopped. The cycle is: say it, do it, stop, say it again. The honest reading of "these processes need restarting" is not a plan. It is the second instance of a documented pattern, and the first instance is in his own archive with the numbers attached.

**The research he asked for in that same August post exists, and it arrived 16 days later.** The 13 August text closed with a request: commission deep research on how people onboard their friends into Claude, Codex and so on. That report is in the corpus as **`DR26-08-29-MACANTON-16-0743`**, "first commands and minimal onboarding for newcomers to Claude Code and Codex", dated **29 August**. Two earlier ones cover the adjacent question of pitching the second brain to non-technical users, from **7 July** and **14 July**. The thing he is about to ask for is already written.

⚠️ **And now the part that measures the premise rather than the plan.** He wants to invite people into his English channel about AI. That channel already holds **147,406** subscribers. The Russian channel holds **3,431**, the English chat **4,683**, and the Russian chat **1,291**. Set against the figure from his own post two weeks ago, about a hundred registered members of the community with 10 to 20 showing up at meetups, the shape of the problem inverts. The audience is not missing by a factor of a hundred. It is present by a factor of a thousand and is not a community.

⛔ **One hard blocker, which is not an opinion.** The post's own method is to ask people "what are you doing, what are you designing?". In the Russian channel that is physically impossible: it has no discussion group attached, so a reader has no comment button at all. The English channel does have one; the Russian one does not, and this has been true the whole time. Inviting 3,431 people into a room and then asking them a question they cannot answer is not a content problem, it is a wiring problem, and it takes one setting to fix.

The cleaning half of the brief already has a tool. The rule for it is specific and worth repeating because it is easy to get wrong: our own spam and unanswered volleys get cleared from a DM before a new touch, and the lead's own messages are never deleted. Deletion is the irreversible kind of action, so a human presses that button.

So the brief that pays off is narrower than the one written. Fix the comment button before the invitation goes out. Read the August report instead of commissioning it again. And treat "restart the processes" as the second occurrence of a measured cycle rather than as a fresh plan, because the thing that broke last time was not the plan.

### Being held hostage by your own staff, and where the dependency actually went

Anton writes about how uncomfortable it is to be a hostage to your own staff. An assistant who knows a great deal about you. An assistant who has worked for you so long that firing them feels wrong, though the salary is substantial. Staff who are like a suitcase with no handle: useful in a way, and already too heavy to carry. He had that story himself, with personal assistants and employees he did not need, with constant friction, where good people make mistakes and you take your anger out on them, or else you treat them delicately while being ready to throttle them, because they are unreliable and keep getting things wrong.

Then the turn: how wonderful that AI arrived and all of this gets solved by building yourself personal assistants. He made a second brain, with one shared memory across every Claude and Codex session and everything happening around him in life. Now he has an assistant who is simultaneously a programmer and a backend developer and can book tickets. He still has assistants and colleagues, he says, but the relationship got much more comfortable, because a lot of the work is now outsourced to synthetic ones. And if you want a virtual co-founder, it is all on GitHub.

**The first thing worth checking is the promise, because most "it is all on GitHub" lines in this corpus do not survive contact.** This one does. The starter kit holds **235** skills and a README, pushed yesterday, with **14** stars and **5** forks. Around it sit a dozen other public repositories, several updated this week. The artefact is real and current, which is not the usual outcome.

**The second is the description of the assistant, and here one of the three examples does not hold.** On the shelf there are **282** skills and **264** routines in the register, so the outsourcing is real: ten skills for code and review, ten for leads and the CRM, sixteen for content, seven for mail and calendar. But skills matching travel, tickets, hotels or booking: **zero**. The assistant does not book tickets today, and a human still does that part. ⚠️ That is a count of tooling rather than of what gets done by hand, and it covers the shared shelf rather than one machine, but it is the one claim in the post a reader could test in a minute.

**And then the part that matters more than either.** The thesis is that the hostage relationship is solved: depend on synthetic assistants, who do not sulk, do not need raises and never have to be fired.

Three posts from the previous two days measure what that dependency looks like in practice. The CRM backend is built and the selection model was decided on 4 July, and the outreach ledger reads 530 touches in September, **11** in October, **zero** in November, last touch 2 October. Eight research orders exist on how to run the community, five of them placed for two questions, and the 3 November ask drew eight replies with five concrete offers, all still unanswered. The Telegram funnel brief of 4 November turns out to be the same brief as 13 August, **83 days** apart, with a measured peak and a measured collapse in between.

Not one of those stalls was caused by staff. Every one of them happened inside a system made entirely of synthetic assistants, built exactly the way this post recommends, and not one of them raised a hand. That is the asymmetry worth naming. A human assistant who stops working gets noticed within a day, because they are in the room and the silence is awkward. A ledger that stops being written gets noticed after **34 days**, because nothing in the system is capable of embarrassment.

So the accurate version of the thesis is narrower than the post's, and better. Synthetic assistants removed the interpersonal cost of dependency, which was real and was the part that actually hurt: the anger, the delicacy, the suitcase with no handle. They did not remove the dependency itself. The bottleneck moved from "my staff will not do it" to "nobody asked me whether it was done", and the second version is quieter, which is precisely why it ran unnoticed for a month.

Which is why his own correction inside the post is the strongest line in it: he still has assistants and colleagues, and the relationship improved because the load moved. That is a claim the measurements support. Replacement is not.

### Slow machines are a gift, and a quarter of a million files is why

Anton asks what the problem with slow computers is, and really: how do you scale your AI agents? You can scale them by adding power. But he has a second brain for his agents, and he experiments with making them autonomous, with a personality, a character, persistence. One of those experiments is his co-founder. And because the storage his co-founder lives in keeps growing, his computer keeps slowing down.

He bought a Mac Studio recently and does not regret it, but it is not helping much, because if the architecture is crooked then even a powerful machine does not let you work faster. Then the turn: slow computers are good. Low compute is good, because it forces you to work out how to optimise the architecture itself. Constant reindexing of the second brain is eating resources right now. He will not scale his co-founder without fixing the architecture, and adding machines, of which he now has eight running the fleet, only postpones the moment he has to face it. Buying servers does not teach you to write leaner code.

**The first thing worth saying is that this post closes a question he asked nine days ago.** On 26 October he wrote that the machines drag badly at 10, 20, 30 sessions, that the computer is powerful and still drags, and that he does not know what to blame and is looking for what is dragging. This post names it: reindexing. Most briefs this week restated themselves, some after 83 days. This one resolved, and resolution deserves to be marked as clearly as repetition.

**The second is that the measurement confirms him, and gives the crookedness a shape.** What a reindex actually walks, counted on this machine: **260,435** markdown files, **2,845.8 MB**. And the breakdown is the finding. Raw conversation exports are **120,464** files, **46.3%** of everything, 1.3 GB on their own. People cards are 43,757. Projects 25,983. Decisions 18,704. And the distilled layers the whole second brain exists for, the concepts, insights and bridges, come to **3,350 files: about 1.3%**.

So the index is built over a quarter of a million files of which roughly one in eighty is distillate. That is not a hardware problem at any price point. Adding RAM multiplies the speed at which you walk 260,435 files; it does not reduce the number. The two levers that do are both about selection rather than power: stop indexing what is not distillate, and make reindexing incremental so that cost scales with what changed rather than with the size of the corpus.

⚠️ **And the uncomfortable part, which is ours rather than his.** We already have the rule this breaks. Our own standing note says only signal goes into the vault and the RAG, not raw material, and that distillation beats volume. The rule exists. Forty-six percent of the vault is raw conversation dumps. So this is not an open architectural question: it is a known rule with nothing enforcing it, which is the same failure class as the outreach ledger that recorded 34 days of silence and told nobody. Three posts in two days have now landed on that same shape.

Two small corrections for the record, and on one of them he is right and we are wrong. His 26 October post says 128GB of RAM, this one says 120GB, both about the same Mac Studio; a reader comparing the two will notice. And he counts eight computers while our node registry lists six, because the Mac Studio bought on 27 October and the Mac Pro were never added to it. On the machine count the instrument is stale, not the author.

Which makes his central claim stronger than he put it. "Slow computers explain everything to me" is not a consolation for having slow computers. It is a description of what a constraint does: it makes you look at the thing you are actually doing, which here turns out to be indexing a quarter of a million files to retrieve three thousand.

## How it connects

Three posts on 4 November, and they divide cleanly into two that repeat and one that resolves.

The Telegram post wants to restart the broadcast funnel, and the recall found the same post from 13 August, **83 days** earlier, with a measurement in between: 353 touches in August, 530 in September, 11 in October, zero now. A restart that already happened once and decayed.

The staff post says synthetic assistants ended the hostage relationship with unreliable employees. The shelf backs most of it: 282 skills, 264 routines, a public kit of 235 skills pushed this week. One claim fails, zero skills for booking tickets. And the week's four stalls all happened inside that synthetic system, none of them flagged by it.

**The slow-machines post is the one that resolves something, and it deserves the weight.** On 26 October he wrote that the machines drag at 20 or 30 sessions, that the computer is powerful anyway, and that he did not know what to blame. Nine days later he names it: reindexing, and the architecture rather than the hardware. The measurement agrees and makes it specific. A reindex walks **260,435** markdown files and 2.8 GB, of which raw conversation exports are **46.3%** and the distilled layers the brain exists for, concepts and insights and bridges, are **3,350 files, 1.3%**. One file in eighty is the thing being looked for.

Which ties all three together more tightly than they look. The staff post argues that dependence on machines is better than dependence on people. The Telegram post shows the machine stopping for 34 days without a word. And this post explains the mechanism underneath both: the system got big without getting selective, so it slows down and goes quiet for the same reason. Nothing in it decides what matters.

And the uncomfortable half is ours. We already have the rule that only signal goes into the vault and the RAG, that distillation beats volume. The rule exists, and 46% of the vault is raw exports. Same shape as the ledger that recorded the silence and told nobody: a rule with nothing to enforce it is indistinguishable from no rule.
