### Teach your LLM to order its own deep research

On the concept of self-learning.

Look: you are using a large language model. On the one hand it is not a genius, and on the other hand it is. It is smart, but its knowledge may be a little out of date.

That is, the model is very smart, it has a lot of knowledge, it can reason, handle facts and draw conclusions. But that knowledge is not always fresh.

For example, if you ask: "Which sites can I buy such and such a monitor on, one that is actually in stock?" If the model does not know that, it has to go and look the information up on the internet.

What I have worked out: a model needs a division of duties. It can be very strong at reasoning, but for current information it needs to be able to search.

When I talk about a model self-learning, I do not mean fine-tuning, changing the model's weights or the facts it operates on.

I mean something else: you have a brilliant assistant. In my case that is the ideal engineer. But he does not always know everything. He needs to rely on fresh knowledge.

He needs to be able to educate himself.

I taught my LLM to run deep research on its own. Loosely speaking, my Claude goes straight to Gemini, Grok and ChatGPT and orders deep research there, at any depth and on any topic.

Which is to say, my model does not try to guess or pull data out of memory. I treat it as a brilliant employee.

But even a brilliant employee, like an ordinary person, has to educate himself and not be shy about googling.

There are two kinds of research.

The first option is simply to go round the internet yourself and look for information.

The second option is to order deep research in several different models. You will get different results and a better quality trail of reasoning.

Plenty of people are afraid of getting a one-sided assessment. But when the model goes out 3 or 4 times and orders deep research in different places, it gets a many-sided opinion from different sources.

And by the way, all of this deep research is ordered within an ordinary subscription. It does not burn a giant number of tokens.

When the result is assembled out of several deep research runs, the model gets a more reliable and more rounded picture.

How should you treat your LLM?

You should treat it as a brilliant employee who works for you. But you cannot lean entirely on its memory alone. It has to verify information every time.

It does the same thing you would do: it goes and looks. It can look in different places, it can go to another model, it can turn to deep research.

What is deep research?

Deep research is different from an ordinary internet search. It is research across dozens or hundreds of pages. If we run deep research in the large LLMs, the result is a thorough and reliable report, one you can actually rely on.

My message is this: do you want to teach your LLM to learn by itself?

I have skills that make your LLM always rely on the freshest and most correct information.

### A skill is personal: the mop is behind somebody else's fridge

I keep getting the urge to build my own skills for Claude, and now, when I do something in Claude, I understand: taking somebody else's skills is absolutely pointless.

Loosely speaking, I have 300 skills, 300 abilities for my Claude or Codex to do something. But if I simply hand you my 300 skills, I will not make your Claude or Codex any smarter.

They will just be 300 files that you have to dig through and work out what the hell is going on.

By the way, I have not used skill marketplaces yet. Maybe I should be putting skills on marketplaces, or taking them from marketplaces. Actually, that is a good idea, taking skills from marketplaces. That is a task for my Claude too. So expect a deep research soon.

So why are skills no good for simply taking and copy-pasting?

Because you have to work them out.

Skills are a set of actions. Your artificial intelligence can go and say something on the internet. It can start writing some message. And to write a message it has to be phrased correctly, as always. Or it needs to get data from third places. And so on.

In short, a skill is something very personal.

You know, we need an analogy here.

Say you live in a certain house or flat. There are things lying around at home: in the kitchen, in the bedroom, in the bathroom. All of those things are arranged with some kind of logic.

Yes, we all have a kitchen, a bathroom and a bedroom. We all have pots and pans in the kitchen. Soap next to the sink. The bathroom has its own set of things too. And in the bedroom you hypothetically have a bed, a mattress, curtains, lamps.

So each of us has roughly the same set of things.

However.

What makes a skill different?

Suppose there is a skill called "tidy the kitchen". It would say: take the mop. If the skill is very detailed, it might say: "the mop is behind the fridge". Something like: fill the bucket with water, start washing the floor. When the work is done, put the mop back behind the fridge.

You take this skill for yourself. But your mop is not behind the fridge. Your mop might be out on the balcony right now.

And it is a bad comparison, because you might have some kind of parquet, and you cannot wash it the ordinary way, you have to wash it with something special.

In short, simply bringing 200 skills into your own home means bringing yourself a bit of trash and a bit of hassle.

Which is to say, a skill on its own is absolutely useless.

But it probably does make sense to use skills, yours, mine, or the ones I hand you, as a template for making your own similar skill.

Folks, if you have a task where you need something, where you need to make yourself a skill, write in the comments. Maybe I already have one like it. And you will be able to ask for my skill to be adapted to yours.

## How it connects

This post names the division of labour that the last two weeks of posts kept circling without stating. Earlier ones measured which model is competent and which is cheapest; this one says competence and price are the wrong axis for one whole class of question, because the model's knowledge has a date on it and the question often does not. Reasoning stays with the model, currency comes from outside it.

The specific design is the part worth copying, and it is stronger than the usual advice to let a model search. One search returns one view. Ordering the same research from several vendors and assembling the result is a different operation: the disagreement between reports is itself information, and it is the only cheap defence against a confident single answer. The post is explicit that going out three or four times is what turns an opinion into a rounded picture.

It also resolves a confusion that costs people real money. Self-learning here is not fine-tuning and not touching weights. Nothing is retrained; what changes is where the model looks before answering. That distinction is why the whole thing fits inside an ordinary subscription, which the post states plainly.


The second post of the day is the limit on the first one, and the pair is the most useful thing here. The morning post said a model should order its own research instead of answering from memory, and offered the skills that make it do so. This one says handing those skills over does not transfer the capability, because a skill encodes where things are in the house it was written in.

The mop analogy carries the argument better than any diagram. Everybody's kitchen holds pots, soap and a mop, so the inventory is shared and the instruction looks portable. What is not shared is the position of each item, and the instruction is nothing but positions. "Put the mop back behind the fridge" is correct in exactly one flat.

That reframes what a skill library is for. Not a package to install, a set of worked examples to copy the shape of. Which also explains why the offer at the end of the post is phrased as adaptation rather than as download: the useful unit of exchange is a skill plus somebody who knows which lines of it are addresses.


The third post is four lines long and points at a gap the other two do not touch. The morning post said a model should order its own research. The second said the skills that make it do so are tied to the house they were written in. This one points at the place where those ties are densest and least visible: the lead database, where the whole value is in which channel each row came from.

Worth saying plainly because the post does not: the observation about Twitter and the claim about the CRM are two separate facts standing next to each other. American AI engineers do live on Twitter. Our CRM does make lead work easier. The sentence that would join them, an outbound rail into Twitter, is the one we do not have.


The fourth post is the bill for the other three. They describe the second brain working: a model that orders its own research, skills bound to the house they were written in, a lead base of 124,952 rows. This one is what that costs to keep running on one desk, and the honest answer is that nobody has measured it yet.

Three candidates are named in the post and none is ruled out: the vault re-index over 20 gigs of files, Screenpipe recording everything around the machine, and a microphone driver that might be hanging. He says plainly that he suspected the first one and it turned out to be apparently not. That is a diagnosis in progress, published as one, which is rarer and more useful than a tidy conclusion.

## The numbers

From the post, and the figures are his own: three vendors named as research rails (Gemini, Grok, ChatGPT), three to four runs per question as the target, research spanning dozens to hundreds of pages, and ordinary subscriptions as the budget.

What our records confirm, and the scale is larger than the post claims: the mechanism is built and has been used heavily. The research registry holds **668 ordered studies**, of which 443 are marked applied, meaning the result actually changed something downstream.

What our records contradict, and it is today's finding: the pipeline has not been used in 24 days. The most recent order in the registry is dated 2 October. Alongside that, 175 studies sit parked and 25 are marked dead, so roughly a quarter of everything ever ordered never reached a consumer. A capability that is published as current while its last run is three weeks old is a claim about the past, not the present.


From the second post: 300 skills claimed, 200 named as the size of a bundle that would only bring hassle, one marketplace question still open, and one deep research promised on marketplaces.

What our records say about the claim, and it is close: the live count on this node is **279 skills**, so the round number is an honest estimate rather than an inflation.

What our records add, and the post is right in a way it does not prove: we measured how many of those skills are bound to this particular house. **129 of 279, forty-six percent, contain at least one tie to our own environment** - 86 reference vault or bus path variables, 44 name specific fleet nodes, 36 carry chat ids or account handles. Absolute machine paths are at zero, because those were scrubbed out in August, so the remaining ties hide inside variables and names rather than in obvious strings.

Which means the mop is behind the fridge in nearly half the library, and the lines that say so do not look like addresses at a glance. Handing someone 279 files would hand them 129 silent assumptions about a flat they have never been in.


From the third post: no numbers at all, which is the shortest post of the three and the one with the largest number behind it.

What our records say, and it is the number the post earns: **124,952 leads** in the live base, **78,507 of them with a usable handle**. Twenty-one columns, and the identity columns are `telegram_id`, `telegram_type`, `source_account`.

Which names the limit exactly. The base is a Telegram base. **There is no Twitter or X column in the schema at all**, and on the skill shelf the X side is two read-and-post tools against an account whose API access has been shut on their end since August. So the leads are real, the volume is real, and the channel the post opens with is the one channel the database cannot address.

Not a defect to fix this week. An honest edge of a working system, and the kind of edge that only shows up when you read the schema rather than the pitch.


From the fourth post: 128 gigs of RAM, 10 to 20 to 30 sessions, 20 gigs of files in the vault, four tools open at once.

What our records add on the Screenpipe suspicion, and it is the most specific thing we have: we have ten Screenpipe skills on the shelf here, and on 30 September we logged a failure of exactly this shape. **The audio rail went silent for more than five days while the watchdog kept reporting the component as active.** A component that is broken and a component that is idle look identical in a status table, which is the recurring theme of this whole day.

So "maybe the microphone driver hangs" is not a guess pulled from nowhere. It is the same component, and we have it failing quietly in our own log a month ago.

Open and unmeasured, which is the actual finding: no profile of a single session's resident memory, no CPU share per tool, and no breakdown of load between re-index, Screenpipe and the editors. With 128 gigs across 30 sessions the arithmetic alone makes memory an implausible culprit, and the measurement that would settle it is half an hour with a process list under real load.
