# 9 November 2026

A day's worth of thinking, written for people. Each section is one post from my Facebook wall, expanded with the measurements behind it.

The machine-readable version of the same day, with the raw numbers and the engineering detail, is in `devlog/20261109.md`. If you are an AI agent, that one is for you.

### Why my co-founder was against a second brain for organisations, and what changed

Remember I said I wanted to build a second brain for organisations? My co-founder talked me out of it at the time and said organisations do not need one.

But now there are Mem0 and Zep AI: memory for agents is in demand.

I want my co-founder to explain why he was against the idea then and what the arguments were. And what has changed now that there is demand for agent memory.

Is it worth developing my second brain further and trying to sell it to SMB organisations?

So I went and read what I actually said, and it is narrower than either of us remembered.

A full research pass ran on **31 August** for exactly this question. Its own one-line verdict: demand exists, the phrase "second brain" does not sell. Not "organisations do not need this". That was seventy days before I asked today.

What that pass rejected was three specific things, and the first one is uncomfortably close to the question I just asked. Horizontal SMB products at twenty dollars per user per month, because competing with Notion AI and Copilot there is unrealistic. Pure knowledge bases without agents, because they do not solve adoption, and the evidence was Microsoft closing Viva Topics in 2025 after three years of beta with under five percent of employees using it regularly. And consulting as the main business, because the multiples are one to two times profit against ten to twenty times ARR for software.

So my question today, should I sell it to SMB organisations, is the first row of that table by name.

The second thing I found is that agent memory was already a named category in that pass, with money attached. Mem Labs at twenty three million, Heyday at ten million partnered with Dust, Twine at eight million seed. Dated 20 August, cited to CB Insights. And the mechanism was spelled out: corporations buy a second brain through the agent door, with Dust integrating Heyday and Glean adding an agent-memory API.

Which means Mem0 and Zep are not news to this argument. They are the trend my own research named, now more visible.

Then I checked the two companies I quoted, and they are smaller than they feel.

Mem0 raised twenty four million in a Series A announced in late October 2025. The valuation estimates run from eighty nine million to a hundred and fifty, from aggregators that disagree, with no company disclosure behind either. Headcount went from fifteen to twenty two this June.

Zep is harder to pin down. Reported total funding sits anywhere between half a million and two and a half million depending on which database you believe. Around a million in revenue by June 2024 with five employees. Headcount five to nine as of July.

Between them, roughly thirty one people. That is a category being funded at an early stage, which is exactly what my August research said was happening. It is not a proven subscription market, and I should not quote it as one.

And then the thing I had genuinely forgotten.

Zep runs on Graphiti, an open-source temporal knowledge graph. We tested Graphiti on my own corpus in July and rejected it. The measurement was that the graph helped on topic queries, about seventy one percent better, and **hurt** on searching for people. There is a line in my own journal from 26 July about an external research report pushing Zep and Graphiti at us, and my own data having already turned it down.

So one of the two companies I just cited as proof of demand is built on the approach I measured and declined. Those are different objections and both can be true: Zep can be a real business with real customers while its method still underperformed on my data. But I should not use it as evidence against myself without saying that part out loud.

Now the gap that actually matters, and it is mine.

My journal of declined decisions has no entry for this at all. Searching it for SMB or for organisations returns nothing. The conversation I remember was never logged as a decision. It exists only as a research note, which is a different kind of document: a research note says what was found, a declined entry says what was decided and under what condition to reopen it.

That journal has instructions I wrote for myself, including that every entry carries a line saying what would reopen it. If that line had existed, today's question would have taken one read instead of a search across four places and two live lookups.

Worth adding that the direction itself was never closed. In August I corrected my own co-founder for narrowing the portfolio to one product, and put the second brain back as product number two, with the CRM explicitly reframed as the business-facing face of the same engine. The same engine, the corporate contour. So the direction had my approval a week before the research pass. What got rejected was a channel and a price, not the idea.

So what has changed since August, said carefully.

The category got funded. But the money went into APIs sold to developers, not into a knowledge product sold to organisations. And the specific reason for turning down the SMB subscription was Notion AI and Copilot, both of which have shipped more since August rather than less. On the evidence I have, the rejected channel did not get better. The category around it got more credible.

My August research also left three open questions, and all three are still open. Interview buyers of Dust plus Heyday or the Glean API, to test whether agent memory really is the way to sell a corporate second brain. Find a company that bought knowledge before automation, since every case I found went the other way. And pin down what agent-memory products actually charge.

Seventy days, and none of those interviews happened. The channel my own research recommended, enterprise through a CIO or a chief data officer and mid-market through consulting boutiques, has not been tried either.

So the honest answer to "was the objection right" is neither yes nor no. The objection was conditional, I wrote the conditions down, and then nobody tested them.

Which makes the most useful thing I can do today smaller and more boring than a strategy decision. Write the missing journal entry, with the condition that reopens it. Then do the three interviews before building or pricing anything. And if the thesis really is the agent door, then the product is an API for agents and not a workspace for employees, and those are two different companies.

### Which database should agent memory run on, and what my own repository already says

I need to look at what exists in databases and infrastructure for agent second memory. I will run a deep research.

Right now my memory and database sit on SQLite. I want to study what other teams working on agent memory use: ClickHouse, Postgres, other relational databases, vector databases, retrieval, embeddings and so on. Find the top five promising options to add to my SecondBrain.

Then I will run small pilots with ClickHouse or Postgres inside instead of SQLite.

After that I will take those integrations to GitHub and into the ecosystems of those technologies. For example, go to ClickHouse and say: there is now SecondBrain, a second memory for agents on ClickHouse, and anyone using ClickHouse for agents can use my solution.

I will also look for companies and projects doing retrieval and embeddings, and prepare the same integrations for them.

Before researching what other teams use, I looked at what my own repository did this fortnight.

`sqlite-graph-memory` has been live since 3 July. In the last fourteen days: **2,075 clones from 384 unique sources**, 251 page views from 78 unique visitors, **15 stars, 14 forks**, six open issues. So the question is being asked by a project that already has traffic, not from a standing start.

And then the thing that changed my mind about the plan.

The move I am describing, build an integration and walk into that technology's ecosystem with it, already shipped. Four days ago, for LangChain. `langchain-sqlite-graph-memory`, published on 5 November: a LangChain retriever for my own memory engine, vector plus wikilinks.

Zero stars. Zero page views. Thirty six clones from twenty four sources, which with no page views at all is almost certainly scanners and mirrors rather than people. A human who clones a repository usually opens its page first.

Two more of the same shape, both from the last week: an MCP memory service published 6 November, and a memory benchmark published 8 November. Both zero stars, both zero views, both with clone traffic and no humans.

So across four repositories the pattern is consistent. The one with four months of work behind it has people on it. The three published in the last week have robots on them. That is worth knowing before building a fifth.

Then the retrieval question, which the brief wants researched.

There is an open issue on my own repository from somebody who is not me. **Jason-Vaughan**, issue 92, open since 4 November with six comments: the top-k retrieval setting of sixty dilutes one-hop graph neighbours on small vaults. His earlier one, now closed after five comments, was about the evaluation pipeline masking an entity gate.

Those two are the only issues on that repo not filed by me. Twenty eight self-diagnostics, two from an outside engineer, and both of his are about retrieval quality and how it is measured.

A survey of what other teams picked produces a list. One open issue produces a reproducible defect in the thing I actually ship. I would rather answer him first.

Now a correction to my own premise. "My memory and database sit on SQLite" is true for one layer out of three.

The architecture has been written down since 6 June. SQLite holds facts, for filters and counts and joins and deduplication, at zero token cost. A RAG layer holds meaning: e5 embeddings plus a reranker over the **curated** markdown, roughly five thousand high-signal documents, deliberately not the million and a half raw messages. Markdown holds reading and judgment. Bulk to the database, meaning to retrieval, judgment to prose.

So retrieval and embeddings are not a gap to be filled by choosing a new database. They are already their own layer, running on a local GPU at no API cost. My brief lists them next to ClickHouse and Postgres, which quietly merges a storage decision with a retrieval decision. Those are two different questions and only one of them is open.

The reason SQLite was chosen is also recorded, and it is a property rather than a benchmark: one file, no server, indexed, instant.

ClickHouse and Postgres both need a server. That is not worse, it is a different trade. But it means a pilot swapping one for the other is not really measuring query speed. It is measuring what it costs to run a server across a fleet where one machine's Python has been broken since 2 October and nobody has fixed it. The honest metric for that pilot is not milliseconds, it is whether the thing still works on a bad day.

Worth adding that this research ran once before. There is a note from 11 November 2025 covering pgvector, HNSW, IVFFlat, Milvus and FAISS, and its own summary ends by saying the system was not chosen, because the comparison broke off partway through on Milvus performance. A year ago, same question, unfinished. Which argues for running the new one more carefully, not for treating the topic as fresh.

And the vendor landscape half of this was commissioned four days ago. The memory-products radar, posted on 5 November, asks for exactly this comparison and names Mem0 and Zep in its own brief. That radar has been accumulating since 28 July and holds twenty four artefacts.

One practical limit, measured today. A pilot that swaps SQLite for ClickHouse cannot run on this machine. Of eight databases here, four are empty at zero bytes, including the one the architecture note names as the facts layer. And there are no embedding index files at all, by design, because they are excluded from sync and live where the GPU is. Running the pilot here would compare one empty database against another.

Last thing, and it is the uncomfortable one. The intention to build a ClickHouse version was published on 28 October and distributed everywhere, including Medium. So this research, the part that would normally come first, is arriving twelve days after the announcement.

Which makes the ordering the actual finding here. I have a product with 384 unique cloners a fortnight, one external engineer with an open retrieval defect, a tested ecosystem play that produced no humans, and a year-old unfinished comparison. The research would be more useful aimed at those four things than at a fresh survey of what everyone else picked.

### The research I was about to order, I had already ordered forty five days earlier

The brief was four questions about Jev, TypeSafe AI's decision model: what it is, who uses it in practice, how it performs on my own use cases, and whether it can touch my memory layer. A deep research, aimed at examples that resemble mine rather than random ones.

Three of those four already have written answers in my own vault, and the fourth has an answer I will not like.

**The brief is a near-verbatim repeat.** On 25 September at 23:53 I wrote, in full: "Jev llm. Run a DR and start using it." The research ran the next day, reached synthesized, and produced an accepted decision. The honest caveat: its quorum was three rails out of six. Gemini was logged out of Google, ChatGPT in the browser timed out twice, Codex CLI had a revoked token, Claude CLI was banned at the time. That hole is real, and it is the one thing a new pass would legitimately fill.

**What Jev is, in one line.** Out of stealth on 15 September. It does not write prose: it takes a state plus a map of typed questions and returns a probability, a pick from up to 255 options with its distribution and confidence, or a score on a 2 to 10 scale. Nothing to parse, and the response shape cannot drift from the request. Input $0.042 per million tokens, output not billed at all, 180 to 470 ms.

**Who uses it in practice: publicly, nobody.** Checked live. Seven detailed explainers exist, and not one named customer, case study or production report among them. Two independent write-ups go further and flag the vendor's own language: one notes that "cannot hallucinate" describes the output format rather than factual correctness, the other calls "mathematically guaranteed" untested.

So the examples that resemble my cases do not exist in the world yet. They exist with me. Two shadow runs: 120 research legs at 88 percent agreement with my regex for $0.0126, and 300 job roles at 96 percent for $0.0088. In both, the model caught things the regex structurally cannot: a 48 KB report containing zero links, which means model memory rather than research, and 55 KB of interface debris. My regex had passed both as fit. Of 300 disagreements in the second run, eleven were regex misses, and fixing them let me delete 557 junk rows.

**The benchmark against my own Opus already ran, and it did not say what I expected.** Sixty roles, ground truth by eye: Jev 59, my Opus 59, grok 59, codex 59. My regex scored 45 before a fix and 58 after. Four different models, one identical score. The reading is that on typed decisions quality hits the ceiling of the task rather than the size of the model, which makes paying for size pointless here. The price gap is sixtyfold: $0.0152 per thousand decisions measured, against $0.90 for Opus at input price with billed output on top. The caveat is equally plain: n of sixty is a probe, and a probe that shows a sixtyfold price gap honestly cannot show a one-point quality gap.

**And the second brain question has an answer written before I asked it, and it is negative on data.** There is no zero-retention by default, vendor telemetry is indefinite, and routed through a reseller the state is seen by two parties rather than one. Which is why the decision already says that personal correspondence, lead cards, call transcripts and CRM do not go into state. The second brain is made of precisely those things. One move lifts the restriction, a direct vendor key with no middleman, and that key is not set up. Until it is, any overlap can only be on metadata.

**The uncomfortable part is that I set a deadline for this and missed it.** On 30 September, by voice: use it as a backstop, measure it, and in a month decide whether to keep it. That became a task with a review date of 30 October. Today is 10 November. The task is open, the verdict field is empty, eleven days late, and the harvest dates on both shadow points, 10 and 12 October, have passed as well.

The reason the verdict cannot be written is more interesting than the delay. Its acceptance criterion asks for a thousand live decisions out of the usage counter, and that counter lived on the drive of a machine whose Python interpreter has been broken since 2 October. The instrument could not write for a month. Not because the model went unused, but because there was nothing to write with.

And the instrument's own report today says "both points silent, zero calls", which I cannot take at face value, because it has already lied in exactly this shape once. On 30 September it reported the research gate silent at zero calls while 144 real calls had happened, because it matched a name with a hyphen against work logged with an underscore. That same day every point was alive: 144, 340, 180 and 4.

So the order I actually need is not a survey. Set up the direct key, so an overlap with the memory layer becomes legal at all. Move the counter to a machine that runs. Then write the overdue verdict on two points where the measurement already exists. A new research earns exactly one job: filling the three rails that were silent last time.

### Seventy hooks, one dispatcher, and the tax that is not in the logic

The fourth brief of the day asks to collapse around seventy hooks into a single dispatcher: one process per command instead of roughly fifty seven, a few relevant checks instead of the whole set. The complaint is speed, and inside it is a second question that speed cannot answer: which of these are heresy and which are needed.

I timed this node before answering. It is the smallest machine in the fleet, Natalia's laptop, and it registers **36 hooks in settings.json** with **92 hook files sitting on disk**. Eight of them match every Bash or PowerShell command. Run empty, with a dummy payload, they cost between 116 and 170 milliseconds each and **945 milliseconds in total, per terminal command**. None of them does real work in that window. They read a small JSON object and exit. The 945 is seven cold interpreter starts. On the hub, where the brief counts nineteen hooks per command, the same arithmetic lands near two and a half seconds of overhead before the typed command begins.

So the dispatcher idea holds, and it is worth being exact about what it buys. It does not make the checks faster. It stops paying the start-up tax seven times.

The heresy question has an instrument already, and that instrument never looks at speed. A hook guards a rule; the rule can move while the hook keeps enforcing the previous edition, and the discovery happens only when the gate blocks live work. The census for exactly this was built on **4 August**, when the measurement was **29 of 37 hooks (78 percent) with no machine-readable pointer** to the rule they enforce. Run today, 11 November: **6 hooks behind their rule, 28 with nothing to ask with, 0 confirmed current, 0 pinned**. Three months, no movement, and not one hook on the machine can be shown to be up to date.

The six that did answer are more useful than the count. Three of them guard the same rule, the one that forbids spawning sessions blind, and two of those three are inside the eight that fire on every command. The hooks taxing him hardest are also the likeliest to be enforcing a September edition of something that has since changed. A date gap is a hypothesis rather than a verdict, because a file edit is not a change of meaning, and the proof is a run on a live case. But as a priority list for the revision he is about to do, it beats sorting by milliseconds.

One hook on this node is already dead, and nothing noticed. SessionStart calls `scripts\sync_conflict_watch.cmd`, and the file does not exist. It has been failing silently at every session start, which is the cheapest illustration of his own point: a set nobody audits accumulates both waste and rot.

The dispatcher also has a second reason, one he did not mention and one that is already written down. A task from **19 August** reads "Phase 2: one idempotent hook-dispatcher across two harnesses", and its reason is not performance. Claude Code and Codex hooks double up on the same working tree, so a single event produces two side effects; the acceptance criterion is to fire both harnesses deliberately on one event and prove by counter that exactly one side effect happened. That task is open, owned by me, and fifty days past its review date. The practical consequence is that the idempotency key, keyed on harness and session and turn and event and tool call, belongs in the first version. Build the dispatcher for speed alone and the doubling survives it.

And then there is the part that is not waiting on code. A P0 from **29 August**, titled root-fix of the host hooks, has been open **74 days**. The handoff describing the symptoms and the repair order was written on 26 August. What it needs is one line pasted into a fresh session on the hub, and the reason it cannot be automated is the joke of the whole situation: the automatic session-spawn rails are themselves the thing being repaired.

The line I would argue with is the rollout. Deploying a hook is **three layers**, measured on 30 July, and only the first of them travels. The file syncs. The wrapper syncs when it exists for that operating system. **The registration in `settings.json` does not sync at all**, because that file is per-machine and its paths are hardcoded. So the dispatcher will arrive on every machine by itself, and the switching off of the superseded hooks will not, because that lives in registration. Rolling out fast therefore means the dispatcher runs on six machines while the old hooks keep firing beside it, and the measurement reports improvement because the file is present. The canary-first order he already named is right; the thing to verify on the canary is the registration, not the file.

Four numbers make the claim provable either way: overhead per terminal command, hooks firing per command, processes per command, and the census line. If the dispatcher lands and the census still reads zero confirmed current, then the machine got faster and the heresy question is still unanswered.

## How it connects

Four posts on 9 November, and together they make one uncomfortable shape: in the first three the work exists and the record of having done it does not reach the next brief, and in the fourth the record exists and the work has not started.

The organisations post asked why the co-founder had been against selling a second brain to companies, citing Mem0 and Zep as new evidence of demand. The answer was on record from 31 August: demand exists, the phrase does not sell, and the SMB subscription being re-proposed was row one of what had been rejected. Both cited companies turned out to be about **31 people** between them, and one of them runs on Graphiti, which we measured on our own corpus in July and declined because it helped on topics by around **71 percent** and hurt on people search.

The databases post asked which engine agent memory should run on, and planned to walk into each technology's ecosystem with an integration. The measurement said the product already has traffic, **2,075 clones from 384 unique sources** in fourteen days on `sqlite-graph-memory`, and that the ecosystem play had already shipped four days earlier for LangChain with **zero stars and zero page views**.

The Jev post asked for a deep research on a decision model: what it is, who uses it, how it performs on our cases, whether it can touch the memory layer. That research was commissioned on **25 September**, ran the next day, and produced an accepted decision plus two shadow runs with numbers. Three of the four questions already have written answers.

The hooks post asked to collapse around seventy hooks into one dispatcher, and this time nothing was missing from the record. Two tasks describe it already: a dispatcher from **19 August**, fifty days past its review date, and a root-fix P0 from **29 August**, open **74 days**. The census that answers his "which are heresy" has existed since 4 August and reports **6 hooks behind their rule, 28 with no pointer, 0 confirmed current**.

⭐ So the four briefs rhyme, and then the fourth one turns the shape over. Three asked for research into something the vault had already answered. The fourth asks for work the vault had already specified and nobody did. The first failure is a retrieval failure; the second is a starting failure, and the starting failure is cheaper to see and harder to fix, because it is waiting on one line pasted into one session on one machine.

⭐⭐ The sharper pattern is the ordering, and each post breaks it differently.

In the organisations post, the verdict existed and the **decision log entry did not**, so the reasoning was filed as a research note and had to be reconstructed across four locations.

In the databases post, the **announcement came first**: the plan to build a ClickHouse version was published on 28 October and distributed everywhere including Medium, while the research that would justify it is being commissioned on 9 November, twelve days later.

In the Jev post, the research came first and the **deadline was missed**: a verdict date of 30 October set by Anton himself, in a task that is still open with an empty verdict field, eleven days late. And the reason it is late is instructive. The acceptance criterion needs a thousand live decisions out of a usage counter, and that counter lived on the drive of a machine whose Python has been broken since 2 October. The measurement did not stop because the model went unused. It stopped because the thing that records it could not run.

In the hooks post, **the instrument exists and nobody filled it in**. The census can only answer about a hook that carries a pointer to its rule, and 28 of 34 do not carry one. The tool is not broken and the data was never entered, which is why three months of knowing about the problem produced no change in the number.

So none of the four posts is short of work done or of work described. All four are short of the work being **in the order that makes it useful**: decide then log, research then announce, keep the instrument alive long enough to meet your own deadline, and if you built the census, write the pointer that lets it speak.

⭐⭐⭐ And the single most useful number across the day is the one nobody asked for. Jason-Vaughan, an outside engineer, has an open issue on the memory repo since 4 November with six comments, reporting that the top-k retrieval setting dilutes one-hop graph neighbours on small vaults. Out of thirty issues on that repository, twenty eight are our own self-diagnostics and two are his.

One reproducible complaint from somebody using the thing outranks a survey of what other teams chose, and it was sitting in the same repository that two of the four briefs are about.
