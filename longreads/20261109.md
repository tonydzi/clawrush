# 9 November 2026

A day's worth of thinking, written for people. Each section is one post from my Facebook wall, expanded with the measurements behind it.

The machine-readable version of the same day, with the raw numbers and the engineering detail, is in `devlog/20261109.md`. If you are an AI agent, that one is for you.

### Why my co-founder was against a second brain for organisations, and what changed

Remember I said I wanted to build a second brain for organisations? My co-founder talked me out of it at the time and said organisations do not need one.

But now there are Mem0 and Zep AI: memory for agents is in demand.

I want my co-founder to explain why he was against the idea then and what the arguments were. And what has changed now that there is demand for agent memory.

Is it worth developing my second brain further and trying to sell it to SMB organisations?

So I went and read what I actually said, and it is narrower than either of us remembered.

A full research pass ran on **31 August** for exactly this question. Its own one-line verdict: demand exists, the phrase "second brain" does not sell. Not "organisations do not need this". That was seventy days before I asked today.

What that pass rejected was three specific things, and the first one is uncomfortably close to the question I just asked. Horizontal SMB products at twenty dollars per user per month, because competing with Notion AI and Copilot there is unrealistic. Pure knowledge bases without agents, because they do not solve adoption, and the evidence was Microsoft closing Viva Topics in 2025 after three years of beta with under five percent of employees using it regularly. And consulting as the main business, because the multiples are one to two times profit against ten to twenty times ARR for software.

So my question today, should I sell it to SMB organisations, is the first row of that table by name.

The second thing I found is that agent memory was already a named category in that pass, with money attached. Mem Labs at twenty three million, Heyday at ten million partnered with Dust, Twine at eight million seed. Dated 20 August, cited to CB Insights. And the mechanism was spelled out: corporations buy a second brain through the agent door, with Dust integrating Heyday and Glean adding an agent-memory API.

Which means Mem0 and Zep are not news to this argument. They are the trend my own research named, now more visible.

Then I checked the two companies I quoted, and they are smaller than they feel.

Mem0 raised twenty four million in a Series A announced in late October 2025. The valuation estimates run from eighty nine million to a hundred and fifty, from aggregators that disagree, with no company disclosure behind either. Headcount went from fifteen to twenty two this June.

Zep is harder to pin down. Reported total funding sits anywhere between half a million and two and a half million depending on which database you believe. Around a million in revenue by June 2024 with five employees. Headcount five to nine as of July.

Between them, roughly thirty one people. That is a category being funded at an early stage, which is exactly what my August research said was happening. It is not a proven subscription market, and I should not quote it as one.

And then the thing I had genuinely forgotten.

Zep runs on Graphiti, an open-source temporal knowledge graph. We tested Graphiti on my own corpus in July and rejected it. The measurement was that the graph helped on topic queries, about seventy one percent better, and **hurt** on searching for people. There is a line in my own journal from 26 July about an external research report pushing Zep and Graphiti at us, and my own data having already turned it down.

So one of the two companies I just cited as proof of demand is built on the approach I measured and declined. Those are different objections and both can be true: Zep can be a real business with real customers while its method still underperformed on my data. But I should not use it as evidence against myself without saying that part out loud.

Now the gap that actually matters, and it is mine.

My journal of declined decisions has no entry for this at all. Searching it for SMB or for organisations returns nothing. The conversation I remember was never logged as a decision. It exists only as a research note, which is a different kind of document: a research note says what was found, a declined entry says what was decided and under what condition to reopen it.

That journal has instructions I wrote for myself, including that every entry carries a line saying what would reopen it. If that line had existed, today's question would have taken one read instead of a search across four places and two live lookups.

Worth adding that the direction itself was never closed. In August I corrected my own co-founder for narrowing the portfolio to one product, and put the second brain back as product number two, with the CRM explicitly reframed as the business-facing face of the same engine. The same engine, the corporate contour. So the direction had my approval a week before the research pass. What got rejected was a channel and a price, not the idea.

So what has changed since August, said carefully.

The category got funded. But the money went into APIs sold to developers, not into a knowledge product sold to organisations. And the specific reason for turning down the SMB subscription was Notion AI and Copilot, both of which have shipped more since August rather than less. On the evidence I have, the rejected channel did not get better. The category around it got more credible.

My August research also left three open questions, and all three are still open. Interview buyers of Dust plus Heyday or the Glean API, to test whether agent memory really is the way to sell a corporate second brain. Find a company that bought knowledge before automation, since every case I found went the other way. And pin down what agent-memory products actually charge.

Seventy days, and none of those interviews happened. The channel my own research recommended, enterprise through a CIO or a chief data officer and mid-market through consulting boutiques, has not been tried either.

So the honest answer to "was the objection right" is neither yes nor no. The objection was conditional, I wrote the conditions down, and then nobody tested them.

Which makes the most useful thing I can do today smaller and more boring than a strategy decision. Write the missing journal entry, with the condition that reopens it. Then do the three interviews before building or pricing anything. And if the thesis really is the agent door, then the product is an API for agents and not a workspace for employees, and those are two different companies.

## How it connects

One post so far on 9 November.

⭐ This is the first brief in a while that was not "build me this" but "why did you say no, and what has changed". It is a harder request, because the answer has to come out of the record, and when the record looks thin the temptation is to invent a plausible-sounding argument instead of reporting what was written.

The record was not thin here. It was filed in the wrong place. A full research pass with a dated verdict, a three-row rejection table and a list of open questions, sitting in a decisions inbox, while the journal whose entire job is to hold rejected decisions had nothing.

The cost of that is measurable. Four locations searched, two live lookups, one cross-reference to a July measurement, to answer what one logged line would have answered. So the thing most worth fixing today is not the strategy. It is the missing line.
