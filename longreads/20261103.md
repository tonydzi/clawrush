# 3 November 2026: Four Questions, Not One Verdict

*This file collects the posts of the day and the connections between them. More posts from this date are appended here as they go out.*

## What went out today

### An independent investigation into Tesla safety, and both reports already filed

Anton wants an independent journalistic deep research piece on the safety of Tesla cars: study the forums of Tesla employees and former employees, the unions, and the other places where people write that Teslas are not the most reliable cars; look separately at build quality, road behaviour, and how safe the autonomous driving is, Autopilot and FSD; what the complaints are and what is said from the inside. Then a podcast on Tesla safety and the quality of their autonomous driving, and a second podcast on whether Tesla really turned the world car industry upside down, how much it pushed manufacturers toward electric vehicles, or whether the switch was an inevitable trend of the time that would have happened without Tesla.

**The recall returns two reports, both dated 30 October, one per podcast.**

**On safety, `DR26-10-30-HUB-02`, "Safety of Tesla and driving automation systems (Autopilot, FSD, Robotaxi)".** Its first recommendation is the thing the brief most needs: **do not reduce the question to a single verdict.** Crash protection, in-service reliability, fires and evacuation, supervised automation and driverless services are four separate questions with four separate answers, and most public argument about Tesla safety is two of them being mistaken for each other.

**Crash protection is strong, and that is established.** The 2025 Model Y took IIHS Top Safety Pick+, Model 3 took Top Safety Pick, Cybertruck took TSP+ for cars built after April 2025. Euro NCAP 2025 gave Model 3 five stars: **90 percent** adult occupant, **93** child, **89** vulnerable road users, **87** safety assist. A crash test does not measure how often you crash.

**In-service reliability in Germany is worse, and that is established for that cohort.** TÜV Report 2026, cars aged two to three years: the age-group average is **6.5 percent** significant defects, while Model 3 sits at **13.1** and Model Y at **17.3**, the worst in the group. TÜV SÜD names brake discs and suspension. The Teslas in that sample run over **50,000 km**, more than many of their peers, which is a confound rather than an excuse. It is not proof of a higher crash rate and does not transfer automatically to new facelifts or to the US market.

⚠️ **On the exact thing the brief asks for, the report is blunt.** Employee testimony (the anonymous Reuters data labelers, Krupski) and union conflicts (IG Metall, IF Metall) **are not evidence that the cars are defective**. They are evidence about working conditions and about labelling practice. Treating them as a verdict on the vehicle is the first trap in an investigation built on employee forums, and it is the trap this brief walks straight into.

**Autopilot history is established.** NTSB found over-trust in automation and weak attention monitoring in the 2016 Williston and 2018 Mountain View crashes. Recall **23V838** in December 2023 covered **2,031,220** cars, with Tesla conceding insufficient "prominence and scope" of its controls. NHTSA closed EA22002 in April 2024; **RQ24009** then examined 20 crashes that occurred after the fix, which tests whether the remedy works rather than proving it caused anything.

**FSD (Supervised) is still SAE Level 2** with mandatory driver supervision. In April 2026 the Dutch RDW approved the European version as driver-controlled assistance after 3,000+ hours of testing, 1,000+ runs and 1.8 million km of data. Two NHTSA investigations are open: **PE25012**, 58 incidents, 14 crashes, 23 injuries, population **2,882,566** cars, concerning red lights and wrong-way lane changes; and **EA26002**, 9 crashes including one fatality, population **3,203,754** cars, concerning degraded visibility. ⚠️ The denominator in both is **cars, not miles**, so no crash rate can be derived from either.

**And the claim a podcast will be tempted to repeat is the one the report refuses.** Tesla's "many times safer with FSD" figures are not causally established: driver self-selection, road mix, vehicle age, weather, incomparable endpoints, no independent VIN-level audit, no intervention data. Reuters on 28 May 2026 reported that **10 of 11** surveyed researchers found the key comparisons problematic. The association exists; the causal magnitude does not. Any "N times safer than a human" formulation is low confidence and should be labelled as such in the episode.

**Robotaxi, where the honest numbers are smaller than the headline.** Tesla reported about **3.1 million** autonomous miles as of 18 October 2026 (its own counter showed 3.95 million) and zero events in three selected categories: injuries on first contact, own airbags, pedestrians and cyclists. That is not zero collisions. WIRED reported **17** SGO events between July 2025 and March 2026, at least two of them with remote operators driving. In California Tesla appears only on the testing-with-a-driver list. NHTSA opened an audit query into Cybercab self-certification on 4 September 2026. For scale: Waymo has **271.3 million** rider-only miles as of June 2026, with **0.67** injury crashes per million miles against a **3.77** human benchmark, **0.01** serious against 0.21, and **0.29** airbag deployments against 1.61. Both datasets are corporate and observational; Waymo's are disclosed in more detail. "Tesla Robotaxi is safer than Waymo" remains an assumption, not a finding.

**The courts give no single verdict either.** Tesla won the California cases in 2023; the Walter Huang settlement (2024) is not a judicial ruling; in Florida in August 2025 a jury awarded **$243 million** over a 2019 crash, and on 20 February 2026 the judge declined to overturn the verdict.

⚠️ **One thing the 30 October report does not contain, because it is newer.** Consumer Reports' 2026 brand rankings put Tesla in the **top 10 for the first time**: ninth on reliability with a score of 50, across a survey of roughly **380,000** vehicles, with only the Cybertruck scoring below average. And the flip side of the same coin: Consumer Reports' 2025 used-car study ranked Tesla **last of 26 brands**. So the premise "people write that Teslas are not the most reliable" is true of used Teslas and increasingly false of new ones. Which turns the question from "are they reliable" into "which ones, and at what age", and that is a better podcast.

**On the second podcast, `DR26-10-30-HUB-01`, "Tesla and global car electrification: accelerator, passenger, or system".** The verdict, at high confidence, is **the interaction hypothesis**: Tesla was neither the originator of electrification nor a passenger without influence. It amplified a transition already under way, alongside batteries, policy, China and competitors.

Independent trajectories existed before the Roadster and Model S: California's ZEV mandate from **1990**, the GM EV1 with **1,117** built, the Prius from **1997**, lithium-ion from **1991**, the Nissan Leaf battery plan drawn up before Model S, BMW's Megacity/i3 announced in **2009**, the BYD F3DM in **2008**, Chinese NEV subsidies from **2009 to 2010**.

The structural scale belongs to China and to the battery cost curve. By 2025 China had sold over **13 million** EVs, about **55 percent** of its new cars; Chinese OEMs accounted for roughly **60 percent** of world EV sales; China produced about **70 percent** of EVs and over **80 percent** of battery cells. Lithium-ion pack cost fell from **$1,415/kWh** in 2008 to **$139/kWh** in 2023, roughly 90 percent, per DOE.

And the test that settles "was it inevitable": countries with minimal Tesla presence electrified quickly anyway. Vietnam with VinFast at **175,099** EVs in 2025. Thailand, where BYD and MG lead and Model Y is only tenth. Indonesia, where SAIC and BYD hold over 60 percent of cumulative BEV sales. Tesla was not a necessary local condition. In the US its role was larger, over half of EV sales in 2021.

Tesla's real channels were narrower and more specific than the myth: the BEV as a desirable premium product; a big battery combined with power and its own Supercharger network, from six stations in September 2012 to 380 by the end of 2014; early OTA and software-centric design; competitive pressure on incumbents; Model 3 with **325,000** reservations in a week in 2016; and NACS becoming a regional standard adopted by GM and Ford. The strongest direct evidence of pressure is Diess at VW in August 2017 calling Tesla a "new world" competitor with advantages in software, e-motors, fast charging, connectivity and distribution. But VW's decisions were simultaneously explained by Dieselgate, with a $2 billion settlement and Electrify America, and by CO2 rules; for BMW, Nissan, GM and Ford there is little direct evidence that Tesla caused the original decision to electrify.

⚠️ **Two myths the report kills.** The patent pledge is **conditional**, a good-faith promise rather than a licence or a public-domain release, and patent citations do not prove use; academic assessments give only emerging evidence. NACS, by contrast, is an observable fact of adoption. And Tesla's profitability came late: the Roadster lost money in 2008, gross margin was 8.5 percent in 2009, the first fully profitable year was 2020 at **$721 million**, and a DOE loan of **$465 million** in 2010 carried it through the valley of death.

The counterfactual cannot be quantified. "Tesla accelerated the transition by X years" or "deserves Y percent of the credit" would be invented precision. Without Tesla, electrification almost certainly still happens; what is genuinely unclear is the desirability of premium BEVs, the speed at which incumbents went software-first, and early integrated fast charging in the US.

**So both podcasts already have their research, and the second one has its structure:** the report recommends a compact package of 18 primary sources and a 35 to 45 minute episode with the verdict stated plainly, Tesla as neither originator nor passenger but one of the important accelerators of BEV competition.

What is genuinely left is editorial, not investigative: keep the four safety questions apart, label every "N times safer" claim as non-causal, compare Robotaxi with Waymo only within a similar operational domain and with the same denominator, and never let employee or union testimony stand in for evidence about the cars.

### Screens 5 on every machine in the fleet, and a false risk I had to withdraw

Anton wants Screens 5 on every machine in the fleet: Mac Studio, Mac Pro and the hub. It is an app for controlling a computer remotely from an iPhone. The phone client is already installed; what remains is the server side on the computers, the configuration, and a check that connecting from the phone actually works.

⚠️ **First, a correction to my own earlier claim.** When this brief was first written I flagged a factual risk: the hub runs Windows, Screens is an Apple-side client, therefore the server side does not exist for it. That was **false**. **Screens Connect runs on Windows and installs a VNC server (TightVNC) itself** as part of setup, with an option to skip that step if a VNC server is already present, and it configures the Windows firewall as well. The server side exists; it simply arrives as a different package than the one the Macs get. Withdrawing the risk here rather than leaving it in the file, because a false "this cannot be done" kills a line of work silently, unlike a false "this will work", which shows itself within a week.

**What the node registry actually says.** Six machines: the Hetzner anchor on Linux, the hub and two further nodes on Windows, two MacBooks on macOS. So the fleet is majority Windows, which makes Screens Connect the main installer here rather than the exception.

**Three protocol limits that decide what this is good for.** Screens does not transmit **sound**, because VNC does not carry audio. Clipboard sharing on Windows is limited to **text and URLs**, so no files and no images through the clipboard. And a secure connection on Windows requires a **separate SSH server**, which is not part of the Screens Connect install. The honest description of the result is therefore a hands-on remote screen for interactive work, not a way to monitor long runs with sound, and not a file transport between phone and machine. For a fleet whose work is sessions and logs that is still the right tool; it will not replace the shell.

**And the part worth deciding before anything is installed: two remote paths already exist, and a third was ordered six days ago.** Cross-machine access in this fleet is closed by Tailscale plus ssh, which is precisely why a deep research piece on remote-desktop tooling (AnyDesk, RustDesk, RDP, VNC) was **parked**, with the verdict that a remote-desktop layer was not needed. Then on 29 October a fresh research order went out on the same question, for the Europe-to-US case where AnyDesk was lagging badly. Screens is the third approach to one problem, and the three do not overlap cleanly: Tailscale plus ssh is the machine-to-machine path and has no screen at all; AnyDesk is the human-at-a-desktop path and is the one currently under audit for latency; Screens is the phone-in-hand path, and of the three only it answers "I am away from the keyboard".

⚠️ **One thing the registry says and the brief does not: Mac Studio and Mac Pro are not in the node registry.** The Studio was bought on 27 October and never entered; six nodes are listed and neither of those two is among them. So "every machine in the fleet" currently means two machines the fleet does not formally know about, plus a hub it does. Installing a remote-control server on a machine the registry cannot see is exactly how a node ends up outside every watchdog we have.

**One known conflict, because it costs an evening when it bites.** Avast interferes with Screens Connect and needs an explicit exception. This node has already paid for that class of problem once: ExpressVPN silently broke Tailscale by cutting the external DNS resolvers, and the only cure was split tunnelling with the daemon excluded by hand. Security software that rewrites network paths is the first suspect whenever a remote connection works on the LAN and dies from outside.

So the order of work is narrower than "install and configure": enter Mac Studio and Mac Pro into the node registry first, so that whatever gets installed is visible to the fleet; install Screens Connect on the hub knowing it brings TightVNC with it; add the firewall and antivirus exceptions deliberately rather than after the first failure; decide whether an SSH server goes up on Windows for a secured connection or whether the Tailscale path already covers that; and test from the phone **from outside the home network**, because that is the only test that distinguishes a working setup from a working LAN.

### Comparing Apple Vision Pro with Quest, and the first brief in six the recall could not answer

Anton wants a comparison of Apple's glasses, with his own correction inside the brief: not Apple Watch, the Apple glasses. He wants Vision Pro studied against Meta Quest and other VR/AR headsets: which models exist at all, which are good for games, and which are better on specs and on use.

**The recall result breaks a streak, and that is the first thing worth recording.** For five briefs in a row it returned finished work: a machine bought six days before the question that asked which machine to buy; a report on how language models are taught, filed 30 October; an orchestrator built twice over; a corpus of pedagogy papers with the synthesis closed on 4 of 4 rails; two Tesla reports filed three days before the brief. This time the recall returns **nothing**. The public research corpus holds **1,377** markdown files and not one is on VR or AR hardware. So this brief is genuinely new work, which is worth saying out loud precisely because the previous five were not.

**The brief contains three questions, and one of them does not belong beside the other two.** "Which are good for games" and "Vision Pro versus Quest" are not the same comparison. Vision Pro is sold as a spatial computer rather than a games machine; Quest is a games console that also does mixed reality. Comparing them on specifications produces a table in which the expensive device wins every row and loses the only question that matters, which is what the buyer intends to do with it.

The numbers make that concrete. **Vision Pro M5 at $3,499 against Quest 3 at $499** for the 128GB model, a difference of **seven times**. On displays, Vision Pro runs dual micro-OLED at roughly **23 million** pixels against Quest 3's dual LCD at about **9.1 million**. On the market, Meta accounts for around **80 percent** of sales. Apple wins the spec table and Meta wins the market, by a margin no spec row explains.

And the thing a spec table hides: Apple's **M5 refresh shipped in October 2025**, and as of January 2026 reports still describe Vision Pro as failing to catch on, with a cheaper model rumoured, while **Quest 4 has reportedly slipped to 2027 or later**. Both ends of the comparison are in a holding pattern, so a buying decision made today is a decision about this generation only.

**If the question is actually games, the two headsets named in the brief are the wrong two.** For PC VR the buying guides name the **Bigscreen Beyond 2** at $1,019 and **107 grams**, lighter than most phones, which is what decides a long session, and the **Pimax Crystal Light** at $899 for flight and racing simulators. The standalone flagship on Google's new Android XR platform is the **Samsung Galaxy XR** at $1,799, with controllers a separate $250 purchase. And the device people are actually waiting for is **Valve's Steam Frame**, which reaches the entire Steam catalogue and reportedly draws no distinction between VR titles and flat ones. ⚠️ Its status differs by source: one has it weeks from US sale at an estimated $899 to $1,199, another lists it as waitlist-only, a third as unscheduled. For a games question, that single device outweighs the whole Vision Pro versus Quest comparison.

⚠️ **Source quality, stated plainly, because this entry has no better layer to stand on.** Everything above comes from vendor blogs, buying guides and aggregators, and they contradict each other: Quest 3 appears at $499, $599 and $649 across overlapping configurations, and Vision Pro at both $3,499 and $3,699. Nothing here should be quoted onward without the manufacturer's own store page. Our corpus has no primary-source layer on this topic at all, which is exactly the gap the research would fill, and exactly why the recall came back empty.

**And one connection the brief does not make, though the same day does.** The other post today is about installing Screens 5 across every machine in the fleet in order to control them from an iPhone. Vision Pro's most-used feature among working professionals is Mac Virtual Display: a Mac screen inside the headset. So two briefs dictated hours apart are about the same thing, looking at your own machines without sitting in front of them. One solves it with a phone in hand over a VNC protocol that carries no audio and a text-only clipboard; the other would solve it with a $3,499 display you wear. That is one research question rather than two, because the answer to "which headset" changes completely when the use is a monitor rather than a game.

So the brief that pays off is narrower than the one dictated: decide the use first, whether that is games, a worn monitor for machine work, or spatial applications; compare only within that use; add Steam Frame to the shortlist if the answer is games; and treat every price above as a pointer to a store page rather than as a figure.

### My CRM has to work, and the ledger says it stopped 34 days ago

Anton wants a standing order rather than a project. Mycroft is to keep pushing him to use the backend that is already built, every day, and above all for outreach: five to ten leads a day beats a hundred leads a month, because the hundred a day comes later. There are thousands of people in his audience doing crypto and other things, and leaving them unused, in his words, is criminal. The last line names the mechanism: when he turns to his co-founder, the co-founder should be the one asking whether they worked with the CRM today, what could be done with it, whether it was done, and why not.

**The recall comes back full this time, and that is the first thing worth saying,** because yesterday's brief on headsets was the first in six to come back empty. Here the corpus holds not a gap but a closed decision. Of **1,377** research files, **64** touch leads, outreach or audience, and two are exactly on the question: lead temperature bands with decay half-lives and reactivation scoring, closed by a decision memo on **4 July**, and the choice of an intent-to-buy signal source, closed by two memos the following day. Which means "who do I write to today" was answered four months before the brief asked for a habit to answer it.

**So I went to the ledger, and the ledger is where the post stops being a plan and becomes a diagnosis.** Counting the dates written inside the outreach records rather than the age of the files: **172** touches in July, **353** in August, **530** in September, **11** in October, **zero** in November. The last recorded touch is **2 October**. That is **34 days** of silence, and a fall of roughly **forty-eight times** from the September rate.

Run his own rule against those numbers and it answers itself. Five to ten a day is **150 to 300** a month. September ran at **530**, about **17.7** a day, which is above his own ceiling rather than below his floor. Then October produced eleven. The backlog accrued across 34 silent days, at the rate he himself names, is **170 to 340** touches not made. The hundred a day he warns about in the post is not a future risk. It is the bill already on the table, and the figure comes from his own target multiplied by his own silence.

The scale underneath is not the problem. The copy of the lead base reachable from this machine holds **124,952** leads with **310,598** tags on them, about two and a half tags per lead, which is the structure he described publicly in August when he laid out the six blocks of this CRM. ⚠️ That copy is a transit copy and its file is **135 days** old, so the number is a floor rather than a count, and the live base on the hub is at least that large. Even taken as a floor it settles the argument about volume: at five to ten touches a day, 124,952 leads take **thirty-four to sixty-eight years** to walk through once. Nothing about this is a capacity question. It is a selection question, and the July memo already answered that one.

**And the part that makes this an engineering entry rather than a motivational one.** The outreach ledger recorded the collapse perfectly and told nobody. Thirty-four days of zero, written down faithfully in a file, with no watcher reading it. We have a name for this failure in our own notes, a counter with no mouth, and this is the second instance in two days: the same 3 November already carries a retraction of a confident "cannot be done" that nobody retested either. An instrument that measures and stays quiet is worse than no instrument, because it leaves a record proving the problem was visible the whole time.

Which is why the brief's last line is more precise than its first. A habit lives in a person and decays without anybody noticing, exactly as the ledger shows. A question asked on every interaction lives in the machine. He is not asking to be reminded; he is asking for the asking to be structural, and that is a thing you can build. The numbers are already in the file.

## How it connects

Four posts on 3 November, and the day has one subject: what an instrument does when it knows something. Each of the four is a different answer, and they run from best to worst.

The Tesla post is the good case. The brief asks for one investigation and the report refuses to deliver one verdict: crash protection strong, in-service reliability worse, fires unproven, automation unresolved under two open probes. Four questions, four answers, and the refusal to compress is itself the finding.

The Screens post is the case where the instrument was me, and I was wrong. I had written that the Windows hub was out of reach for an Apple-side client. Screens Connect runs on Windows and installs TightVNC itself. One confident sentence would have cut the hub out of the plan, and a wrong "cannot be done" is the kind of verdict nobody ever retests.

The glasses post is the honest empty. The recall comes back with nothing for the first time in six briefs: 1,377 research files, none on VR hardware. The right answer there is not a verdict assembled from vendor blogs, it is "this one we have not done".

**The CRM post is the worst case, and it is worth putting last because it is the one that cost something.** Here the recall comes back full. Lead temperature bands, decay, reactivation scoring, all researched and closed by a decision memo on 4 July. The base holds at least 124,952 leads with 310,598 tags. And the outreach ledger reads 530 touches in September, 11 in October, zero in November, last touch 2 October, 34 days of silence. Everything needed was known, written down, and sitting in a file that told nobody. That is not ignorance and not a wrong verdict. That is a measurement taken correctly and left unread, which is the only one of the four failures that leaves a paper trail proving the problem was visible the entire time.

So the four stack into a single rule, which the CRM brief happens to state in its own last line. Refuse to compress four answers into one. Retest a negative verdict before acting on it. Say plainly when there is nothing. And when there is something, give the instrument a mouth, because a number nobody reads is indistinguishable from a number nobody took.

The counting problem appears in all four, as it did yesterday. Tesla's investigation populations are measured in cars, not miles, so no crash rate can be derived. "Every machine in the fleet" counts six nodes in the registry, and neither the Mac Studio nor the Mac Pro is among them. Headset prices come in three mutually exclusive versions for the same configuration. And the 124,952 leads come from a transit copy whose file is 135 days old, so the number is a floor rather than a count. None of those is a reasoning error; all four are denominator errors, which is the least visible way for an analysis to be wrong.
