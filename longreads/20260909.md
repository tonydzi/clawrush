## What if mortality is a feature, not a bug?

The deeper Anton goes into longevity, the more he thinks about a basic constraint: every biological system ages. Trees may survive for centuries, or even thousands of years, but almost nothing living is permanent.

That leads to a thought experiment. Suppose the universe is a game running on a server. In that case, aging and death may not be defects waiting to be fixed. They may be foundational rules that prevent the game world from filling indefinitely with living organisms.

From this perspective, programmed mortality and some form of respawn would make sense as default mechanics. A player may lose memory, possessions and accumulated armor during a respawn, but the alternative is a world with no room for anything new.

Radical life extension then raises a stranger question: if the server has administrators, what happens when some of its NPCs learn to bypass one of the main rules? A sufficiently successful longevity technology could look less like medicine and more like an exploit.

Anton compares this to a scientist observing an ant colony. If part of the colony suddenly discovered a way to live far longer, its social structure might change completely. The scientist could continue watching, intervene, or reset the experiment.

Historical catastrophes can be described metaphorically as previous resets, although that does not establish who or what caused them. The useful point is personal rather than historical: if mortality is an unavoidable rule of the game, understanding that may make it easier to accept.

Respawning may not be such a bad outcome. The harder question is whether we respawn at all.
