# 24 August 2026: Someone Else's Skill Is a Well-Made Empty Box

*Nine posts and twelve research summaries went out today. The engineering ones converge on one question: what is actually transferable between people, and what only works because of the data you accumulated yourself.*

## The thread that runs through the day

Three of today's posts look unrelated. One is about skill libraries, one about how to behave in other people's repositories, one about a chat for engineers that turned into a news feed. They are the same question asked three times: **what do you owe the other side, and what can you actually carry across?**

## Skills: born by a counter, dead by a calendar

The [main piece of the day](https://github.com/tonydzi/clawrush/blob/main/longreads/who-cleans-a-thousand-skills.md) is the fourth post from Konstantin Krestnikov's GigaChat talk ([recording](https://youtu.be/a-NIeMB-Hj8)). His system and his numbers, not our measurements.

Two mechanical rules run his whole library. Birth: if a task took more than five tool calls, the agent considers turning that work into a skill. The trigger is a counter, not taste, and that is the part worth copying. A person does not notice they are doing the same thing for the third time. A counter notices every time.

Death is equally mechanical. Thirty days without a call and the skill goes dormant. Ninety days and it moves to the archive. Once a week small skills are merged into larger ones. None of it asks permission, because a library that only grows stops being searchable and the agent pays for every entry it has to consider.

Then the observation that outlasts both rules. They run a shared hub where the community contributed a large pile of skills, and he does not see them being used. Everyone writes their own.

That is not a quality judgement. It follows from what a skill becomes once data lives inside it: **the instruction is the cheap half, your accumulated history is the expensive half.** Someone else's skill arrives with the instruction and none of your data. A well-written skill you did not write is a well-made empty box.

## Two GitHub lanes, and why the empty box matters there

The [morning post](https://t.me/PaloAltoAi/1992) set out two lanes being built here.

Outbound: watch fresh repositories in our domains, artificial intelligence, Claude Code, agent collaboration, agent workflows, find the ones that overlap with what is already built, and do something useful there. Commits, pull requests, comments, issues. New repositories are more likely to answer than a large project with a silent queue.

Inbound: follow the top repositories in the same topics, which first requires decomposing our own product into named directions. Part of what is already built has no name yet, and an unnamed capability cannot be matched against anyone else's problem.

Put next to the skills post, the lanes stop being a growth tactic and become the correction for the empty box. What transfers between engineers is not the artifact. It is the specific fix applied to their specific breakage, which is why a pull request against a real issue lands and a broadcast does not.

## The chat that became a news feed

The [same lesson arrived from the losing side](https://t.me/PaloAltoAi/1997). A chat was started for engineers and then used as a channel for our own news, blog and teasers. People reacted badly, argued, and the activity went somewhere nobody wanted.

The rules set in response are deliberately narrow: no spamming, no constant teasers to the blog, at most one to three messages a day and preferably no more than one post every eight hours, and every message has to give something on its own. A teaser is acceptable only if it is useful without the click.

The question stays open, and it was asked publicly rather than answered internally: how do you run a professional chat for engineers, and what keeps real discussion alive instead of an announcement feed?

## One model writes, three try to break it

[The QA piece](https://github.com/tonydzi/clawrush/blob/main/longreads/multi-llm-council-as-qa.md) describes the review rail. Claude Code is usually the implementer. When a component is finished, it is not complete because the model that generated it says so: Codex, Gemini and Grok inspect it independently, test it, and try to break it.

The honest origin is money, not principle. Deep research runs cost roughly ten to fifty dollars each when paid per use, so research was routed through subscription buckets we were already paying for, leaving the coding capacity for coding. The quality effect was a side effect that turned out to be worth more than the saving. An engineer does not review their own work, and neither does a model.

## Cheaper software means more of it

[The Karpathy paradox post](https://github.com/tonydzi/clawrush/blob/main/longreads/cheaper-software-means-more-of-it.md) argues the programming profession survives precisely because software is getting cheap: cheaper means more of it gets made, and programmers become orchestrators. The concrete image is a person with a seven thousand dollar Mac generating a site for a hair salon in five minutes, against a market of small businesses that found Wix a genuine pain.

It sits next to the skills post more closely than it looks. If generating the instruction becomes nearly free, then the instruction is not where value accumulates. The scarce half is the data and the judgement around it.

## Two posts that are not about engineering

[Budapest on a mechanical skateboard](https://t.me/PaloAltoAi/1994): five to seven kilometres and you are not tired, the roads are bad but the pavements at night are lovely, the best surface is the strips cars have polished flat over the years, and the thing to avoid is the seams.

[How the lab got its name](https://t.me/PaloAltoAi/1995), and why the name was a mistake. It started around 2022 as the "Unofficial Stanford" lab. Stanford sued and took the domain, and in the same year their president was removed over misconduct. The fallback was the place everyone actually lived, and "Palo Alto" googles terribly because every second company here carries it. The rule that came out of it is small and general: check that a name is findable before you print it.

## The rest of the day

[What if I am the NPC](https://github.com/tonydzi/clawrush/blob/main/longreads/what-if-i-am-the-npc.md) turns the AGI question around: not whether the machine will resemble us, but how much of what we do is consciously authored. Existence is not the same claim as authorship.

Twelve summaries from the deep research corpus also went out through the day, each one a finding we paid for and then left sitting in a file: eBay graphics card lots under hard filters, Facebook personal account limits, fleet scaling past thirty nodes, parallel session decomposition, reciprocal engineer programs, the Hetzner anchor outage, Reddit self-promotion norms, shared agent memory architecture, Cursor and Anysphere ownership, and browser agents waking up logged out. Publishing them is the same move as the outbound lane: research nobody outside can read is an empty box too.

## Honest boundary

The GigaChat numbers are his, measured on his system, and we have not reproduced them. Our own contribution today is one sentence long: we counted our skills, and the result was not good.
