In my case everything is safe, but I just wanted to make two points.

But to set an iPhone up so that it becomes your real instrument, properly sharp, can you imagine how much time you need? Installing apps, configuring everything and so on. ### Several LLMs on one machine, one folder, unisex skills

Several LLMs on one machine.

Say you have Claude, and Claude Code of course. And you know where to switch between Claude and Claude Code.
By the way, a friend and I spent five minutes today looking for where that switch is. Imagine how much Anthropic can undersell their own product, given that they simply hid the switch between ordinary Claude and Claude Code.
Most people who are not deep in this will never find that switch. Fine, that is not the point.

So: on the same machine you have Antigravity. Antigravity is a convenient interface for reaching your artificial intelligence from Google, the one called Gemini.
You have Codex, which lives in the same application as ChatGPT. At least that one has convenient switches.
You have Claude, which switches over to Claude Code.

Now, for all of them to work in symbiosis with each other, they all need to work out of one folder. Or out of several folders, but then every folder has to carry a strict instruction saying what it is for and how to handle it.

Their skills, their abilities, are also built so that all the skills are unisex. A skill is not invented only for Claude or only for Codex. It is written so that any LLM that gets access to it knows how to use it.

It is simply an instruction in Russian and English. A fairly detailed instruction, naturally, on how an LLM uses some piece of functionality and what to do.
Loosely speaking, like using Telegram: here are your login and password, here is access to the Telethon library or an MCP, and so on.

So, several LLMs from different vendors can run on one machine perfectly well. And they can avoid stepping on each other, use one shared memory, one set of unisex skills.

And you will save on tokens and distribute tasks across several LLMs in parallel.

Want the details, or want me to help you build something, write in the comments.

As you remember, I am trying to get a job at an AI lab. And I need cases where I helped somebody set something up. Glad to be useful.
And I am waiting for your comments too: how have you set yours up nicely? Glad to hear it.

## How it connects

First post of the day, and it picks up where yesterday left off. Yesterday was about a working setup being expensive to keep running: a second brain that needs constant maintenance, machines crawling under thirty sessions, a model ranking for distillation. This one is the other end of the same story, the moment a new machine arrives and has to be made identical to the old one.

The useful part is not the hardware. It is the method, and the method is boring on purpose: no migration wizard, no disk clone, no careful manual reinstall. The laptop talks to the new machine over SSH and the assistant does the work, file by file, tab by tab. Setting up a computer turned into a task you hand to a tool rather than an afternoon you spend.

Which is also the honest answer to the question the post ends on. The objection "I am not a coder, what would I do with this" assumes the tool writes programs. It mostly does not. It reads documentation, edits config files, installs things in the right order and remembers what it already did. Writing a dissertation and setting up a Mac are the same shape of work.


The second post is the first one generalised. The morning post had a laptop configuring a new Mac over SSH with the assistant doing the work. This one says what makes that possible: not the tool as sold, but 1860 hours of sharpening it, and the claim is that the unsharpened version is useless.

Two details in it are worth separating, because one is a nice story and the other is a measurable idea. The nice story is the paranoia loop: his assistant writes install instructions, the friend's assistant refuses to trust them and audits every command for prompt injection. That is a real property of the tool out of the box, and it is the reason "just follow my README" fails for a stranger.

The measurable idea is the swarm. Set it up for someone and their instance joins a group that receives whatever anyone in the group invents. That turns a one-off favour into a distribution channel, and it is the only part of the post that can be checked rather than believed.


The third post names the mechanism the other two assume. The morning post had one machine configuring another; the second said the tool is useless until sharpened and described a swarm that shares what anyone invents. This one says what is actually being shared: not prompts and not settings, but skills written so that any vendor's model can read them.

The practical rule in it is one line long and easy to miss. They all have to work out of one folder, or out of folders that each carry a strict instruction about what they are for. That is the whole trick: the shared memory is a place on disk, not a feature of any product.

And there is a small commercial observation buried in the middle, which is the kind of thing only a user notices. He and a friend spent five minutes hunting for the switch between Claude and Claude Code. A product that hides its own second mode undersells itself to exactly the people who would pay more.


The fourth post is the bill for the first three, and it is a personal one. The morning post had a new Mac Studio with 128 gigabytes of RAM being configured. This one says what he actually works on: a Mac bought secondhand near Palo Alto three years ago, already two years old then, running an Intel processor that locks him out of half the software he wants.

Both are true at the same time, and the day only makes sense if you hold them together. The new machine is real and so is the old one; the fleet grew by a quiet workhorse while the daily driver stayed six years old. The sequencing in the post is the honest part: the job comes first, then maybe solar panels, and the Mac is third.

And the last three lines are the ones that will be read most. His son asks why he has not found a job yet and suggests asking Claude to find the richest ones. Then says he spends more time with Claude than with him. The post does not soften that, and neither did we.

## The numbers

From the post: 128 gigabytes of RAM, 512 gigabytes of disk, one external Thunderbolt drive still to buy, a 20 dollar plan against a 100 dollar one.

The one number that decides everything here is the smallest one. 512 gigabytes is the reason there is no normal migration at all: the laptop simply holds more than the new machine can take, so the transfer has to be selective, and a selective transfer is exactly the kind of fiddly, long, highly specific work an assistant is good at and a human is bad at. The 128 gigabytes of RAM are nice to have and irrelevant to the problem.

Worth naming from our side, because it changes a record rather than confirming one: **this machine did not exist in our fleet registry until now**. We hold six nodes, and as recently as 2 October the note in memory said in his own words that there is no Mac Studio here at all. So the post is not describing a machine we have been running. It is the arrival of a seventh, bought since then, still outside the registry, with no node profile, no routines and no watchdogs pointed at it.

Open and unmeasured: how much of the laptop actually needs to move, which is the number that would say whether the external drive is needed at all. Nobody has put a figure on the vault, the sessions and the caches separately, and the purchase is being decided without it.


From the second post: 1860 hours in Claude, 1500 not long ago, 1800 in another sentence, 8 to 10 hours a day, five to ten employees, 20 to 30 K of someone else's infrastructure costs, a few thousand dollars a month of his own.

What our records confirm, and it is the specific half. **Hetzner is real**: the fleet anchor in our registry is described as an always-on Hetzner VPS acting as coordinator and source of truth, so "I sit on Hetzner now" is literal rather than a figure of speech. **The sharpening is real too**: 279 skills on the shelf of this one node. And the swarm has actual machinery behind it, not just an intention: a deployment layer that packages a fix and applies it across nodes, with its own apply and verify steps.

What we cannot confirm is the number the post leans on hardest. **There is no hour counter for Claude anywhere on this machine** - searched recursively across nine roots plus PATH under two different names. So 1860 hours is his own tally, not a reading from an instrument, and the 1800 in a later sentence is the same number remembered differently. Worth saying plainly because the rest of the post is unusually checkable.

Open and unmeasured, and this is the honest weak spot of the swarm claim: distribution works, but the rate is low. One package went out across the fleet in the last fourteen days. A swarm that shares inventions is only as good as how often something actually ships through it, and nobody is measuring that.


From the third post: three vendors on one desk, one shared folder, one set of skills, no numbers at all.

Here is the number the post earns, measured on this node today. Skills are not copied per vendor; they are **mounted**. There are **242 junctions** under the Codex skills directory, each pointing at the same file in the Claude skills directory, out of **279 skills** on the shelf. So "unisex" is literal rather than aspirational: one file, two readers, no duplicates to drift apart. The bridge that keeps it that way is a live script with its own usage counter.

The bilingual claim checks out too. Skills on this machine carry both Russian and English inside the same file, which is why a stranger's model can follow them and why we do not keep a translated copy per vendor.

Open and unmeasured, and it follows directly from the design: nobody has counted which skills the other vendors' models actually invoke, only that they can see them. 242 mounted is a measure of access, not of use. The difference matters, because a skill nobody calls is as good as absent, and that is the gap we keep finding everywhere else in this log.


From the fourth post: an M4 someone else owns, a Mac five or six years old, bought three years ago when it was already two, Intel, half the software unusable.

The two numbers in it do not quite line up, and that is left as he said it. Bought three years ago at two years old makes five; he says five or six. It is a man estimating the age of his own laptop out loud, not a spec sheet, and flattening it would make the sentence sound like an inventory entry.

What our own records add, and it is the uncomfortable pairing: **yesterday's log described a newly bought Mac Studio with 128 gigabytes of RAM arriving outside the fleet registry**, and today's post is written from a six-year-old Intel machine where half the tools do not run. Both machines exist. The new one is the quiet workhorse being provisioned over SSH; the old one is where the work actually happens while that provisioning finishes.

Worth stating plainly because the posts read oddly next to each other otherwise: this is not a contradiction, it is the middle of a migration. The interesting part is that the migration is blocked by the smallest number in either post, a 512 gigabyte disk, and not by anything about the old machine at all.
