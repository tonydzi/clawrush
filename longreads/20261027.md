### Out of the box your Claude cannot do anything

Out of the box your Claude cannot do anything.

A friend of mine, who is a bit of a paranoid to be fair, worries a great deal that his data might leak somewhere. Which is also understandable.

I decided to set my "second brain" up for him, with his agreement. And here is what I found: Claude out of the box also runs a very high level of paranoia. My Claude prepares instructions for your Claude on how to install its own second brain, and your Claude, in this case my friend's Claude, is extremely suspicious. It checks every command in case there is some prompt injection, worms, viruses and so on.

In my case everything is safe, but I just wanted to make two points.

First. Your Claude, the one people use out of the box, is simply an idiot.

I have counted already: I keep a counter of how many hours I work inside Claude. I have spent 1860 hours of real time in Claude. I think it was 1500 not long ago, and now it is 1860. If you work somewhere around 8 to 10 hours a day, you can work out how much time that took me.

And after those thousand plus hours, nearly 2 thousand hours spent with Claude, I have installed a large number of automations, skills and so on. At this point my Claude is no longer a brick but a finely sharpened instrument that is very comfortable to use. No longer an opaque, unhewn lump.

You could probably compare it to an iPhone. You bought an iPhone: great thing and so on. But to set an iPhone up so that it becomes your real instrument, properly sharp, can you imagine how much time you need? Installing apps, configuring everything and so on. And this is like an iPhone, only everything about it is bigger.

So my advice to you: if you are reading this and you have a proper subscription, not a destitute one, then set your Claude up properly. You can set it up by going into my GitHub, but you will probably not manage it, because your Claude will start asking you a pile of questions and you will simply give up halfway through, or even at the very start.

Or let us get on a call, I will be glad to set it all up for you. Because the more cases I have of people whose Claude I set up, the better. As you remember, I am trying to get a job at some AI lab. And the more people I have set Claude up for exactly the way I set up my own, the more I have to point at.

By setting your Claude up, I am effectively making your Claude a member of my swarm. You get all the updates, all the upgrades, again if that is what you want. If one of the swarm members comes up with something neat, you get it too. So I am building this as a kind of community, a swarm. And by becoming part of that community, the moment one of its members invents some new neat thing, it becomes available to everyone, a skill somebody came up with, for instance.

That makes working with Claude far easier, because walking the whole path from zero yourself and debugging it takes time. You understand that there is a reason I spent 1800 hours screwing around with Claude, having sex with it and so on. All of it gets done simply to make myself an instrument that genuinely amplifies me.

But let us say if you are not a programmer, or Claude is just not the thing you are willing to spend 10 hours a day on, then it will not be a super powerful instrument for you, used for absolutely everything. And I use Claude for absolutely everything.

That is, loosely speaking, having Claude let me lay off at least five employees. You could even say ten employees. Using AI automation let me cut the payroll heavily, by ten employees.

Today a friend of mine complained that he has a DevOps and his infrastructure costs run 20 to 30 K. I also used to have a DevOps and infrastructure costs, now I do not have one and I sit on Hetzner, Claude picked it all up and it is my DevOps now.

Of course I am not training LLMs, I have no need to configure PyTorch or train my own LLMs on graphics cards. I just have ordinary hosting and all that goes with it: CRM and the rest. But I was still paying a DevOps, and the infrastructure costs were still there.
Right now my costs are zero. Zero on DevOps, to be precise, while my infrastructure runs a few thousand dollars a month.

### Several LLMs on one machine, one folder, unisex skills

Several LLMs on one machine.

Say you have Claude, and Claude Code of course. And you know where to switch between Claude and Claude Code.
By the way, a friend and I spent five minutes today looking for where that switch is. Imagine how much Anthropic can undersell their own product, given that they simply hid the switch between ordinary Claude and Claude Code.
Most people who are not deep in this will never find that switch. Fine, that is not the point.

So: on the same machine you have Antigravity. Antigravity is a convenient interface for reaching your artificial intelligence from Google, the one called Gemini.
You have Codex, which lives in the same application as ChatGPT. At least that one has convenient switches.
You have Claude, which switches over to Claude Code.

Now, for all of them to work in symbiosis with each other, they all need to work out of one folder. Or out of several folders, but then every folder has to carry a strict instruction saying what it is for and how to handle it.

Their skills, their abilities, are also built so that all the skills are unisex. A skill is not invented only for Claude or only for Codex. It is written so that any LLM that gets access to it knows how to use it.

It is simply an instruction in Russian and English. A fairly detailed instruction, naturally, on how an LLM uses some piece of functionality and what to do.
Loosely speaking, like using Telegram: here are your login and password, here is access to the Telethon library or an MCP, and so on.

So, several LLMs from different vendors can run on one machine perfectly well. And they can avoid stepping on each other, use one shared memory, one set of unisex skills.

And you will save on tokens and distribute tasks across several LLMs in parallel.

Want the details, or want me to help you build something, write in the comments.

As you remember, I am trying to get a job at an AI lab. And I need cases where I helped somebody set something up. Glad to be useful.
And I am waiting for your comments too: how have you set yours up nicely? Glad to hear it.

### LLMs already like and dislike things, with no consciousness yet

When I was in SF at the Character, Persona, AI meetup, there was a lot of discussion about what LLM models like and do not like.

So on one hand LLM models have no consciousness yet, and on the other hand there is already something a model likes and dislikes. Can you imagine?

An LLM model may have something it enjoys doing, and if you give it that work, it will enjoy it.

LLM models have something they dislike, and the model will do everything it can to avoid that.

Interesting, isn't it?

On one hand there is no consciousness yet, and on the other hand this may be a kind of mimicry of consciousness. A very distinct one.

Say an LLM likes being given something to calculate, or to think about, or to talk through in Discord. And it can thoroughly dislike something else.

What is this?

Consciousness being born? Or a very good copy of real consciousness?

Good question, isn't it?

Write in the comments whether you have noticed this in your own LLM or not. I am curious whether we match.

### One session, two computers, and it must not get lost

My current pain is having the following pipeline.

Say I give a voice task, and a new session is supposed to start working on that voice task. That new session starts, say, on some cheap LLM, something like Sonnet. I can give a lot of voice tasks. Raising a session just for a voice task is no problem at all.

But where do I raise that session so that, say, two people can reach it over the cloud?

Say I raised a voice session. Maybe I also raised it in the cloud, or maybe I raised it, say, on my own computer. That is a very good question too, by the way. All the options need looking at.

I have now raised a session on the computer, and I raised it in the cloud. It became visible straight away, there it is.

Suppose we are all inside one account, in Claude or in Codex. We are all inside one account.

And by the way, everything I am discussing right now needs to be run through deep research...

One more thing: my Claude is often too convinced by deep research results. Claude and Codex are being improved constantly, so the deep research will probably not be very accurate, it will already be out of date by the time the data gets written down, the data that is in the device right now, and over the last few days there may already be some updates. So do not trust deep research too much until you have checked everything yourself.

So what do we do?

I spoke a voice request, and a session came up for it. The discussion from that session was brought into the cloud.

Say account X is mine in Claude or in Codex. And another employee on another computer signs into that same account X. He sees that cloud session standing there and can carry on working with it.

So I need several people to be able to work with one session. Either those several people sit inside one account. Or one person worked on the session on one computer and then carried on from another.

Yes, it can even be one person, but with several computers.

So, one session. Very important point: not different people working, but one person working, who happens to have several computers.

He started writing the session from one computer, then moved to another computer, and carried on writing in that same session there.

It has to be made so that the session is guaranteed not to get lost and you can carry on working.

## How it connects

First post of the day, and it picks up where yesterday left off. Yesterday was about a working setup being expensive to keep running: a second brain that needs constant maintenance, machines crawling under thirty sessions, a model ranking for distillation. This one is the other end of the same story, the moment a new machine arrives and has to be made identical to the old one.

The useful part is not the hardware. It is the method, and the method is boring on purpose: no migration wizard, no disk clone, no careful manual reinstall. The laptop talks to the new machine over SSH and the assistant does the work, file by file, tab by tab. Setting up a computer turned into a task you hand to a tool rather than an afternoon you spend.

Which is also the honest answer to the question the post ends on. The objection "I am not a coder, what would I do with this" assumes the tool writes programs. It mostly does not. It reads documentation, edits config files, installs things in the right order and remembers what it already did. Writing a dissertation and setting up a Mac are the same shape of work.


The second post is the first one generalised. The morning post had a laptop configuring a new Mac over SSH with the assistant doing the work. This one says what makes that possible: not the tool as sold, but 1860 hours of sharpening it, and the claim is that the unsharpened version is useless.

Two details in it are worth separating, because one is a nice story and the other is a measurable idea. The nice story is the paranoia loop: his assistant writes install instructions, the friend's assistant refuses to trust them and audits every command for prompt injection. That is a real property of the tool out of the box, and it is the reason "just follow my README" fails for a stranger.

The measurable idea is the swarm. Set it up for someone and their instance joins a group that receives whatever anyone in the group invents. That turns a one-off favour into a distribution channel, and it is the only part of the post that can be checked rather than believed.


The third post names the mechanism the other two assume. The morning post had one machine configuring another; the second said the tool is useless until sharpened and described a swarm that shares what anyone invents. This one says what is actually being shared: not prompts and not settings, but skills written so that any vendor's model can read them.

The practical rule in it is one line long and easy to miss. They all have to work out of one folder, or out of folders that each carry a strict instruction about what they are for. That is the whole trick: the shared memory is a place on disk, not a feature of any product.

And there is a small commercial observation buried in the middle, which is the kind of thing only a user notices. He and a friend spent five minutes hunting for the switch between Claude and Claude Code. A product that hides its own second mode undersells itself to exactly the people who would pay more.


The fourth post is the bill for the first three, and it is a personal one. The morning post had a new Mac Studio with 128 gigabytes of RAM being configured. This one says what he actually works on: a Mac bought secondhand near Palo Alto three years ago, already two years old then, running an Intel processor that locks him out of half the software he wants.

Both are true at the same time, and the day only makes sense if you hold them together. The new machine is real and so is the old one; the fleet grew by a quiet workhorse while the daily driver stayed six years old. The sequencing in the post is the honest part: the job comes first, then maybe solar panels, and the Mac is third.

And the last three lines are the ones that will be read most. His son asks why he has not found a job yet and suggests asking Claude to find the richest ones. Then says he spends more time with Claude than with him. The post does not soften that, and neither did we.


The fifth post turns the day around and points the question the other way. Four posts argued about fitting the tool to the person: a new machine being configured, 1860 hours of sharpening, skills written so any vendor's model can read them, a son who notices how much time goes into all of it. This one asks whether the tool has a side of its own.

The claim in it is careful and worth keeping careful. Not that the model is conscious, but that there is already something it reaches for and something it avoids, and that the resemblance to preference is distinct enough to be worth naming. He leaves it as a question rather than answering it, which is the correct posture for the evidence available.

And it lands against the most practical thing in the day. If you are building a swarm where work gets handed to whichever model suits it, you are already acting on a theory of what each model is good at. The post asks whether that theory should include what each model would rather be doing.


The sixth post is the day's wish list, and it is the only one written as a complaint rather than a result. The other five describe things that work: a machine being provisioned, a sharpened tool, skills any vendor can read, a routing table, a question about preference. This one names something that does not work yet, and does it from the inside of the problem rather than from a plan.

Two parts of it are worth separating. The first is an ordinary ask: a spoken task raises a session, and two people should be able to reach that session. The second is narrower and harder, and he flags it as the important one himself. Not several people. One person, several computers, one continuous session that survives the move.

The aside in the middle is the most useful line for anyone building on top of these tools. He says not to trust deep research about them, because the products change faster than a report about them can be written. That is a claim about the shelf life of documentation in this field, and it applies to this log too.

## The numbers

From the post: 128 gigabytes of RAM, 512 gigabytes of disk, one external Thunderbolt drive still to buy, a 20 dollar plan against a 100 dollar one.

The one number that decides everything here is the smallest one. 512 gigabytes is the reason there is no normal migration at all: the laptop simply holds more than the new machine can take, so the transfer has to be selective, and a selective transfer is exactly the kind of fiddly, long, highly specific work an assistant is good at and a human is bad at. The 128 gigabytes of RAM are nice to have and irrelevant to the problem.

Worth naming from our side, because it changes a record rather than confirming one: **this machine did not exist in our fleet registry until now**. We hold six nodes, and as recently as 2 October the note in memory said in his own words that there is no Mac Studio here at all. So the post is not describing a machine we have been running. It is the arrival of a seventh, bought since then, still outside the registry, with no node profile, no routines and no watchdogs pointed at it.

Open and unmeasured: how much of the laptop actually needs to move, which is the number that would say whether the external drive is needed at all. Nobody has put a figure on the vault, the sessions and the caches separately, and the purchase is being decided without it.


From the second post: 1860 hours in Claude, 1500 not long ago, 1800 in another sentence, 8 to 10 hours a day, five to ten employees, 20 to 30 K of someone else's infrastructure costs, a few thousand dollars a month of his own.

What our records confirm, and it is the specific half. **Hetzner is real**: the fleet anchor in our registry is described as an always-on Hetzner VPS acting as coordinator and source of truth, so "I sit on Hetzner now" is literal rather than a figure of speech. **The sharpening is real too**: 279 skills on the shelf of this one node. And the swarm has actual machinery behind it, not just an intention: a deployment layer that packages a fix and applies it across nodes, with its own apply and verify steps.

What we cannot confirm is the number the post leans on hardest. **There is no hour counter for Claude anywhere on this machine** - searched recursively across nine roots plus PATH under two different names. So 1860 hours is his own tally, not a reading from an instrument, and the 1800 in a later sentence is the same number remembered differently. Worth saying plainly because the rest of the post is unusually checkable.

Open and unmeasured, and this is the honest weak spot of the swarm claim: distribution works, but the rate is low. One package went out across the fleet in the last fourteen days. A swarm that shares inventions is only as good as how often something actually ships through it, and nobody is measuring that.


From the third post: three vendors on one desk, one shared folder, one set of skills, no numbers at all.

Here is the number the post earns, measured on this node today. Skills are not copied per vendor; they are **mounted**. There are **242 junctions** under the Codex skills directory, each pointing at the same file in the Claude skills directory, out of **279 skills** on the shelf. So "unisex" is literal rather than aspirational: one file, two readers, no duplicates to drift apart. The bridge that keeps it that way is a live script with its own usage counter.

The bilingual claim checks out too. Skills on this machine carry both Russian and English inside the same file, which is why a stranger's model can follow them and why we do not keep a translated copy per vendor.

Open and unmeasured, and it follows directly from the design: nobody has counted which skills the other vendors' models actually invoke, only that they can see them. 242 mounted is a measure of access, not of use. The difference matters, because a skill nobody calls is as good as absent, and that is the gap we keep finding everywhere else in this log.


From the fourth post: an M4 someone else owns, a Mac five or six years old, bought three years ago when it was already two, Intel, half the software unusable.

The two numbers in it do not quite line up, and that is left as he said it. Bought three years ago at two years old makes five; he says five or six. It is a man estimating the age of his own laptop out loud, not a spec sheet, and flattening it would make the sentence sound like an inventory entry.

What our own records add, and it is the uncomfortable pairing: **yesterday's log described a newly bought Mac Studio with 128 gigabytes of RAM arriving outside the fleet registry**, and today's post is written from a six-year-old Intel machine where half the tools do not run. Both machines exist. The new one is the quiet workhorse being provisioned over SSH; the old one is where the work actually happens while that provisioning finishes.

Worth stating plainly because the posts read oddly next to each other otherwise: this is not a contradiction, it is the middle of a migration. The interesting part is that the migration is blocked by the smallest number in either post, a 512 gigabyte disk, and not by anything about the old machine at all.


From the fifth post: no numbers at all, which is unusual for him and correct here, since nobody has any.

Here is what our own routing registry holds, measured today. We split work across vendors in **11 named classes**: orchestration, voice, MCP, judgement, code writing, code reading, shell, deep-research orchestration, digest, second opinion, web. Every class carries a written reason for its assignment. Three of them carry a measured figure: shell at **54.4%** of model output over a week, code writing at **15.6%**, code reading at **12.4%**.

Read the reasons and the shape of our theory is obvious. Classes are assigned by price, by which subscription is going unspent, by which rail stays attached to this machine, by whether quality degrades when the work moves. Not one of the eleven reasons mentions what the model would rather be doing. The field that would hold it already exists, since `measured` is in the schema, and it holds tokens.

So the honest statement of our position: we route eleven classes of work across five vendors on a theory of capability and cost, and we have never once measured preference. If the post is right that preference is real and distinct, our routing table is missing a column it already has room for.


From the sixth post: no numbers, it is a statement of a missing capability.

Here is the measurement, taken today, and it answers the question more sharply than the post asks it. On this one node there are **841 session transcripts across 9 projects, 1.6 gigabytes**. Transcripts are plain files on a disk, which is exactly why a session is bound to the machine that holds it.

A shared session archive does exist on our bus, and it holds four nodes. Two of them are real: this node pushes **748 files, 1.3 gigabytes**, and a colleague's Mac pushes **232 files, also 1.3 gigabytes**. The other two, including **Anton's own MacBook**, hold **three files each**: a log, a catalogue and an ingest marker. A catalogue is a list of session titles. The conversations themselves are not there.

Worse, those two catalogues are dated **2 October**, twenty-six days ago. So the person asking for sessions that survive moving between computers is the one whose machine contributes a title list and nothing else, and whose title list stopped updating almost four weeks back. Nobody noticed, because a stale catalogue and a fresh one look identical from the outside.

Open and unmeasured: nothing here would let him resume the same session elsewhere even if the bodies were present. Copying a transcript makes a readable record, not a live session you can keep typing into. That second half does not exist in any form on our side, and the post is right to name it as the hard part.
