# 25 August 2026: Thirty Sessions, Half of Them Dead

*One post today, and it is about the cost of opening things. Measured on our own hub, not theorised.*

## What went out today

**Fail of the day.** I counted the sessions open on my hub. Thirty at once, against a sane ceiling of twenty, each one holding its own connectors and eating memory. A patrol robot closed fourteen of them: idle more than five hours, some more than a day. Half of what was "running" was not running at all.

Two more sessions were standing still, waiting on a single click from me. Forty minutes each. They cannot unblock themselves, and one of the two was going through job openings for me personally.

Then at just past eight in the morning the desktop application on the hub died and did not come back on the first revive attempt. Every scheduled robot went down with it, and the hub is the centre of everything I run.

## The three things worth taking from that

**A session with no expiry date lives forever and eats resources quietly.** Fourteen dead sessions were not an accident of today, they were the accumulated result of never setting a lifetime at creation. Sweeping them up with a patrol afterwards is a bandage. The fix belongs at birth: every session declares how long it may live, and the sweeper becomes a safety net rather than the mechanism.

**A step that requires a human must name, in advance, the channel where that human actually lives** — and the default that applies if nobody shows up. Two sessions burning forty minutes each on a pending click is not a human problem, it is a design omission. The alert existed. It went to a place I do not check often enough. That is the same thing as no alert.

**A watchdog cannot live inside the thing it watches.** The desktop watchdog tried honestly to revive the app and failed with it, because the crons it would have used were themselves down. This is the rule we keep re-learning at increasing cost: the monitor must run on an independent rail, and what it asserts on is the age of the output at the consumer, not the exit code of a job.

## Honest boundary

This is one day on one machine, and the numbers are small: thirty, fourteen, two, forty minutes. Nothing here proves a general law about agent fleets. What it does show is the shape of the failure — capacity spent on things that were not doing work, and a human standing in the middle of a pipeline that was supposed to run without one.
