# A Recipe Beats a Prohibition

*A reader pointed us at superpowers. Here is what we changed the same week, and the measurement that made the third idea land.*

[Anunah](https://www.facebook.com/Anunah), you advised me to look at https://github.com/obra/superpowers

I am immensely grateful to you!

I was going through it yesterday, and today I am already making changes in my own setup: rewriting the rules, converting them into a clear recipe. And changing the skill descriptions — which also closes part of my token-saving task!

And the basic idea: do not create rules for processes that already work without rules. People had pointed this out to me before, but now it has really landed.

## Three things we took, in order of how much they changed

**A recipe instead of a prohibition.** Our rules used to be written as bans: do not do X, never do Y. A ban tells an agent what not to do and leaves it to invent the alternative, which it will, differently each time. A recipe says do A, then B, then check C — and it is followable without judgement.

This is the change with the widest reach, because it applies to every rule already written, not just new ones. Rewriting a ban as a recipe usually also shortens it.

**A skill description contains only triggers.** This was the one that surprised us. A description exists so the right skill gets picked, not so the reader understands the skill. Everything explanatory belongs in the body, which loads only when the skill actually runs.

That directly answers the token part of the note: descriptions are always loaded, bodies are not. Ours had grown into small essays, and every one of them was being paid for on every single session.

**Do not write a rule for a process that already works.** The hardest of the three, and the one where "people had pointed this out before" is doing a lot of work in the original text. We had been told too, and it did not land until we counted.

## Why that third one needed a number to land

Here is the measurement that ended the argument for us.

**Of 25 rules we had recently adopted, 19 had no caller at all.** Not "were being disobeyed" — no code path, no checklist, no routine referenced them. And the standout: a debugging procedure sat in our canon for **42 days and was applied exactly zero times.**

Nobody forgot it and nobody disagreed with it. It was written down, and everyone had mistaken being written down for being in use.

Alongside that: **95 gates capable of turning red that nothing ever knocks on.** Each one built correctly, tested, wired to nothing.

So the rule we now hold, and it is the sharper version of the idea from the post: **a rule with no calling moment is not a rule, it is an opinion.** Before adopting one, name the place it fires from. If you cannot name it, you are writing documentation about your intentions.

And its twin, which is the actual content of "do not write rules for what already works": if a process runs correctly without a rule, adding one buys you nothing and costs you on every session that loads it.

## The token side is real and worth stating precisely

The connection the post makes in passing deserves its own line, because it is the strongest argument of all.

**A rule is not a one-time purchase, it is rent.** It loads into every session, forever, and you only notice the cost on the day you write it. Our own measurement: session startup context runs between 98,000 and 123,000 tokens depending on the node, and the trend across two weeks is upward.

That reframes "do not create unnecessary rules" from tidiness into arithmetic. Every unused rule is a small payment made on every session, for nothing, until someone deletes it.

Which is why the recipe rewrite pays twice: a followable recipe is usually shorter than the ban plus the explanation of why the ban exists.
