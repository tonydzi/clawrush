## How it connects

One post so far today, and it is a task rather than a story: take the memory we already run on SQLite and build the same thing again on a database that belongs to a bigger crowd. The interesting part is that the reason given is not performance. It is that ClickHouse is where the GPU and high-load people are, and a repository is a door into a room you are not otherwise in.

That makes this a distribution decision wearing engineering clothes, and worth saying out loud, because the two get argued as if they were the same question. Our SQLite version is not slow for what it does. A port buys nothing in speed. What it buys is a second doorway.


The second post of the day is a question to the room, and it sits oddly next to the first one until you notice they are the same shape. The morning asked whether to rebuild our memory on a database that belongs to a bigger crowd. The evening asks why a form refuses to accept a CV. Both are about doors, and in both cases the door is controlled by somebody else.

The difference is that one door we can measure from the inside. Our own records on captcha are unusually complete, because every blocked application leaves a row, and those rows say something he does not say in the post: the captcha is not a property of the site. It appears and disappears on the same site within a week, depending on how the request looked.

## The numbers

What exists on our side. Our memory lives in two public repositories: one holding the graph memory server on SQLite, **12 stars and 8 forks**, and the starter kit, also **12 stars and 4 forks**. Across this machine there are **201 SQLite files totalling 288 megabytes**, and the largest single one is the CRM at **87 megabytes and 814,451 rows**, with its biggest table holding **310,598** rows. Not one of our repositories mentions ClickHouse anywhere, so this would be the first.

The audience numbers say his instinct is right. ClickHouse sits at **50,659 stars with 9,179 forks** and **8,541 open issues**. The SQLite mirror on GitHub has **10,854 stars**. On the surface he is pointing at a room roughly five times larger than the one we are standing in, and our own doorway there is currently **12 stars wide**.

The room has space for the subject, which is the part worth knowing before writing any code. Of those 8,541 open ClickHouse issues, a search for agent memory returns **23**, and the three at the top are about binary size, seccomp policy and setting descriptions. Nobody is doing this there. That is not a warning, it is the free angle.

And the honest counterweight, because it changes which database to pick rather than whether to do it. ClickHouse is a server you run. SQLite is a file you copy. The AK-47 rule we build everything on is "the simplest thing Anton can repair himself with a hammer", and swapping a file for a server breaks it on the first install. The column-store that keeps the file shape is **DuckDB, at 42,678 stars**, which is 84% of the ClickHouse audience with none of the operational cost. If the goal is a second doorway, DuckDB is the cheaper one, and it is a question for him, not for us.


On the captcha, and this is the part worth reading twice, because the measurements contradict the usual framing of it as a wall.

Our own records by platform. **Lever** served hCaptcha **8 out of 8 times** from one node on 4 September and **13 out of 13** from another, with the challenge appearing as early as the CV upload. Then it stopped: **0 of 2** on 11 September and **0 of 2** on 13 September, same engine, same account, no change on our side except elapsed time. **Workable** gave Turnstile **5 out of 5**, and that one is structurally harder: Cloudflare fires it *after* Submit, inside a closed shadow DOM, and running a visible browser instead of a headless one does not help. **Greenhouse** showed reCAPTCHA only on a burned IP, and after **two days of leaving that IP alone** it accepted **23 applications in a row with no gate at all**.

The pile this builds. At its peak on 3-4 September the queue of forms waiting for a human held **54 of them**: 27 Greenhouse, 13 Lever, 14 Workable, each needing 10 to 30 seconds of a person. Across our application waves, roughly **25%** of everything attempted ends in a code gate or a captcha. **Five companies** are on a permanent skip list because they serve reCAPTCHA unconditionally.

And the measurement that actually answers his question. One batch run by hand through a real logged-in browser on 1 September: **17 applications, zero blocked, zero captchas**. Same roles, same CV, same person. The variable was not the site and not a solver, it was how the request looked.

Which is why our own rule is that the robot does not solve captchas at all. It marks the form, moves on, and leaves the challenge to a human. A solver treats the symptom and teaches you nothing about why the gate fired, and the records above say the gate is a verdict on behaviour and IP reputation, not a fixed wall.

Honest about what we do not have: **this node holds no live application database**, only empty stubs. Every figure above is from the recorded rail measurements taken on the nodes that do the applying, dated September, and the Lever numbers already proved they expire. Nobody has re-measured them this month, so treat them as the last known state rather than today's.
