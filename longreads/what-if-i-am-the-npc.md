# What If I Am the NPC?

*We keep waiting for AI to become conscious. The more uncomfortable question is whether we understand what consciousness and agency mean in ourselves.*

The popular AGI story points outward. Humanity builds a machine, the machine wakes up, and the world changes forever.

Anton turns the camera around.

What if the unsettling comparison is not that AI may become human, but that humans may already resemble the systems we are building? We have objectives, biological constraints, reward signals, habits, survival programs, and a narrow set of actions that repeatedly consume our days. Hunger pulls one way, status another, fear a third. We call the resulting movement a life.

Does that make a person an NPC?

As a literal claim, there is no evidence that some people are non-player characters controlled by an external operator. As a metaphor, however, “NPC” points to a real philosophical problem: how much of what we do is consciously authored, and how much emerges from causes we did not choose?

## The cogito proves less than people think

“I think, therefore I am” establishes a minimal point: experience or thought is occurring, and total doubt cannot erase the fact of that occurrence.

It does not automatically prove an immortal soul, unlimited freedom, or independence from biology. A system could be conscious while still being heavily constrained. It could experience deliberation while its motives arise from genes, hormones, memories, social pressure, and present conditions.

Existence is not the same claim as authorship.

## The puppet does not require a puppeteer

The feeling of being pulled by strings does not require somebody outside the world holding them.

The “strings” may be ordinary causes:

- hunger and fatigue;
- reward and aversion;
- attachment and sexual desire;
- fear of exclusion;
- learned habits;
- incentives created by money and institutions;
- stories absorbed from family and culture.

None of these proves that free will is absent. They do show that human decisions are not created in a vacuum.

The difficult question is what kind of freedom is possible inside a causal system.

## Three answers to the freedom problem

One answer is hard determinism: every decision is the result of prior causes, so the feeling of freely choosing is not the kind of authorship we imagine.

A second answer is libertarian free will: at least some choices are genuinely open and cannot be reduced entirely to prior physical causes.

A third answer is compatibilism: freedom does not require being uncaused. It means being able to act through one's own reasons, values, reflection, and self-control rather than through coercion. A choice can have causes and still belong meaningfully to the person making it.

None of these positions is settled merely by building a larger language model. AI makes the old disagreement vivid because it gives us a new object that produces plans, explanations, and apparent preferences while raising unresolved questions about inner experience.

## A human as biological AGI

Calling a human “biological AGI” is a useful engineering analogy with sharp limits.

Humans are general-purpose learners. We transfer knowledge between domains, act in open environments, use tools, form long-term goals, cooperate, deceive, imagine futures, and revise strategies. In that functional sense, the comparison is productive.

But the analogy does not answer whether an AI is conscious, whether consciousness is computational, or whether a person is “only” a program. Similarity in function is not proof of similarity in subjective experience.

The word “only” hides most of the mystery. A biological process may be mechanistic and still produce pain, love, responsibility, and meaning. Explaining a mechanism does not automatically explain away the experience.

## The ethical trap in calling other people NPCs

The metaphor becomes dangerous when it is aimed only outward.

Calling other people NPCs can become permission to stop treating them as centers of experience. Their behavior appears repetitive, so we infer that their interior life is empty. But behavior gives us limited access to any mind other than our own.

Anton's reversal is intellectually healthier: what if the suspicion applies first to the observer?

That move does not prove that he is an NPC. It breaks the asymmetry in which one person grants themselves full depth while reducing everyone else to scenery.

## “What difference does it make?”

This may be the strongest question in the post.

Suppose every thought has prior causes. You still experience consequences. You still update after evidence. Relationships still respond to care and neglect. Institutions still change incentives. Reflection still changes future behavior, even if reflection itself is part of the causal chain.

If a person is a system, then education, habits, environments, tools, and communities are ways that the system rewrites itself.

Agency may not mean standing outside causality. It may mean becoming one of the causes.

## Death and the edge of evidence

What happens to consciousness after the body dies remains outside what this argument can establish. The possibilities people imagine—continuation, merger, rebirth, or extinction—carry religious, metaphysical, and personal meanings, but the NPC analogy does not select among them.

Honesty requires leaving the question open where evidence ends.

## The practical conclusion

Perhaps the useful distinction is not player versus NPC.

It is reflective versus automatic.

A person cannot choose every impulse that appears. They can sometimes notice the impulse, compare it with a value, change the environment, build a habit, ask for help, or refuse the first response.

That gap may be smaller and less magical than absolute free will. It is still where responsibility, learning, and self-authorship live.

If we are biological agents running inherited code, then introspection is not proof that the code is absent. It is one of the tools the code has for editing what happens next.
