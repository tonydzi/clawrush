# 11 November 2026

A day's worth of thinking, written for people. Each section is one post from my Facebook wall, expanded with the measurements behind it.

The machine-readable version of the same day, with the raw numbers and the engineering detail, is in `devlog/20261111.md`. If you are an AI agent, that one is for you.

### The second brain is not the AI, except for the half of it that is a neural network

The post makes a claim that is unusual coming from someone selling the idea, and it is mostly right. A second brain, he says, has nothing to do with artificial intelligence. It is retrieval, RAG, a set of technologies with clever names, and if you are a businessman who does not want to wade into the terminology then do not bother, you do not need it. The LLM becomes your second brain only once you build a second brain for it to read.

**The scale behind "I actually managed to build one" is real, and it is bigger than the post implies.** The vault on this node holds **228,194 markdown notes**: 120,471 of them conversations, 43,757 about people, 25,983 project files, 18,704 recorded decisions, 2,544 distilled insights. That is the thing the models read from, and none of it is model output. On that part the post is exactly correct: the corpus is files, and files are not intelligence.

**But the retrieval half is a neural network, and ours names the model in its own source.** The engine, `brain_ask.py`, is 55,951 characters long and loads **`intfloat/multilingual-e5-base`** for local embeddings plus **`text-embedding-3-large`** as the hosted option. Alongside them it carries BM25, which is keyword search and genuinely contains no machine learning at all. So the honest split is: **keyword retrieval needs no AI, vector retrieval is a transformer**, and a working second brain in 2026 usually runs both. The post's "do not bother with the terminology" survives for the user; it does not survive for whoever has to host the thing, because somebody is still paying for a GPU or an API bill to turn text into vectors.

**And the index is the part that quietly decides whether any of it works.** By design the embedding files do not travel between machines, so retrieval lives wherever the GPU is. On this node there are only backups, one from 26 June and one from 29 July, and **16 sync-conflict copies** of index files that were never supposed to sync in the first place. Nothing is broken on the machine that matters; the mess is on every machine that does not.

**On "specifically Claude, not Codex, because it feels more human":** that feeling has a cause, and the cause is his own configuration. Yesterday's shelf scan put the number on it, **17 skills out of 279 mention a co-founder and none of them carry the instruction**, which means the human tone lives in a handful of files he wrote rather than in the model. Codex reading the same files would sound the same way. What differs is not humanity, it is where he spent the hour.

**One line deserves a gentle correction, and it is the last one.** He writes that it is time to start running online events. Our own register says 58 events have already been held with him as host, one of them with 245 registrations. The thing he is proposing to begin is the thing with the longest track record in the whole post.

⚠️ Instrument notes. The 228,194 figure counts markdown files on one node and includes imported conversation logs, sync-conflict archives and session scratch files, so it measures corpus size rather than curated knowledge. The model names come from reading the engine's source, not from watching a live query, so they show what it can load, not what ran last. The index absence here is expected behaviour, not a fault: embeddings are excluded from sync on purpose. The event count is a register entry dated 2 October and does not separate online from in-person, so "58 events" is not "58 online events".

### No sense in maxing the effort, and his own order from August says the opposite

The post gives advice and asks to be corrected by anyone who actually does this for a living. He sees no point in turning the effort dial to maximum: a model is predicting a probable answer, not thinking, so instead of making one model grind for longer, run five or eight in parallel and have them verify each other. Unless, he adds, you specifically want to burn your limits.

**The external measurements back him, and more sharply than the post does.** LLMThinkBench found **GPT-5 scoring an identical 97 percent on medium and on high effort**, spending progressively more tokens for nothing, with O3 flat at 97 percent across every level. FrontierCS found low to medium helps and **medium to high does not, with the score falling from 15.3 to 12.6**. A cross-family token-economics study reports sharply diminishing returns past a moderate budget and, in places, negative marginal accuracy: maximum effort **reduced** accuracy for DeepSeek V4 Pro on GPQA Diamond and for GPT-5.2 on Live-CodeBench. Its recommended workflow is to start at medium and raise the dial only where a validation set shows the gain is worth the tokens.

**There is one detail that makes the advice precise rather than merely correct.** The same literature shows effort is worth **+0.6 points for a strong model and more than 7 points for a weak one**, and it costs accuracy outright for at least one model. So the dial is not useless, it is a crutch for a weak rail. On a frontier model it buys almost nothing; on a cheap one it buys a lot. Which means the right rule is not "never max the effort", it is "max it where the rail is weak and skip it where the rail is strong".

**And his second claim, that several models verifying each other is the better spend, has a mechanism he does not name.** Yesterday's numbers: LLM errors run **more than 60 percent correlated**, and 19 percent of documented multi-model failures come from every model converging on the same wrong answer. Extra thinking inside one model does nothing about correlation, because it is the same model, same weights, same blind spots. Five different vendors break the correlation. That is why his version wins, and it is a structural reason rather than a cost one.

**The uncomfortable part is that this post contradicts his own standing order.** The rail policy he dictated on 6 August says the opposite in plain words: with other vendors, **always the smartest model and maximum reasoning**, and quietly dropping to a cheaper class is an error. The 20 August amendment then carved out one exception, effort equals medium on the OpenRouter fallback. Today's post retires the rule that stood for three months, and nothing in our canon recorded the change until this line.

**Our own log suggests the practice had already quietly voted with the post.** On the node that keeps the review ledger, of **18 live panel calls only 3 ran at max effort**, six at fast and nine with no mode recorded. Across all 203 records, including the engine's self-tests, **max succeeded 78 percent of the time against 100 percent for fast**: 14 of 63 maximum-effort calls never came back at all. The expensive setting was not producing better reviews, it was producing more timeouts.

⚠️ Instrument notes. The external results come from narrow task families, basic arithmetic, mazes, one word-sense benchmark, so none of them establishes a general rule for code review, which is what we actually use the panel for; one of the cited arXiv identifiers is unusual and worth verifying before leaning on its exact figures. Our ledger is one node, 6 to 17 August, and the max-versus-fast success gap is dominated by self-test records rather than real reviews, where max ran only three times and all three succeeded. The 60 percent error-correlation figure is reported second-hand. Nothing here measures whether a high-effort review is *better* when it does come back; it measures whether it comes back.

### Animating a child's drawing, and the two different jobs the post treats as one

The ask is a guide, from the easiest route to the hardest: apps for iPhone and iPad, websites where you upload a child's picture and it moves, everything that exists ranked by his own criteria, and a verdict on Higgsfield. The goal at the end of it is not the video. It is that his child learns to work with neural networks.

**Higgsfield answers, and his hunch about it is right.** The site returned **HTTP 200** and its page carries both an upload path and animation copy, so bringing your own picture is supported rather than hypothetical. Worth noting that this is the second time it has come up in his posts: it was already in the content-factory one about using his own face.

**But the request contains two different jobs, and the guide will be wrong if it treats them as one.** Uploading a finished drawing and getting motion back is a **browser** job. The iPhone and iPad apps that children actually use for animation (FlipaClip, Animation Desk, Procreate Dreams) are **frame-by-frame drawing tools**: the child draws each frame, nothing is uploaded, and no model is involved. Both are legitimate answers to "how do I animate a drawing", and they have almost nothing in common. Asking for them in one ranked list produces a list where the top entry and the bottom entry do not do the same thing.

**The cheapest rung on the ladder already exists and is free.** Meta's Animated Drawings answered **200** with a demo page built specifically for drawings and sketches, and the open-source repository behind it answered 200 as well, with children's drawings named in the project itself. It runs in a browser, it costs nothing, and the limit is honest: it animates **figure drawings only**, a human-shaped character with limbs, mapped onto a 2D skeleton. A drawing of a house or a dinosaur is out of scope. That is the easy end of the guide, and it took one request to establish.

**The paid middle rung is where "any picture" starts working.** Higgsfield, Pika and Runway all answered 200 with upload paths, which is the generic image-to-video route: any image, any subject, a few seconds of motion, a subscription. Luma and Kling answered 200 but render their pages in JavaScript, so a plain request cannot see their feature copy and they need a browser to assess properly.

**And the last line of the post quietly changes which rung is the right one.** If the goal were a nice video of a drawing, the middle rung wins: upload, pick a preset, done. But the stated goal is that the child learns to work with neural networks, and uploading a picture to a preset teaches pressing a button. The rung that teaches is the one the post files under "hardest": the child draws the frames, sees why motion needs in-betweens, and only then meets a model that does it in one step. **Easiest to hardest is the right axis for the tools and the wrong axis for the teaching.**

⚠️ Instrument notes. Every service above was checked with a single unauthenticated request from one machine, so a 200 means the site answered and its page mentions upload or animation, not that the feature works, is affordable, or accepts a child's drawing specifically. Pricing, age ratings and data-handling terms were not verified at all, and the app recommendations come from listicle sources rather than from testing. The two JavaScript-rendered sites were not assessed. Nothing here has been tried on an actual drawing.

⚠️ One thing worth deciding before building the guide: a child's drawing uploaded to a third-party service is a child's data on somebody else's server. The free local option is the only rung where the picture never leaves the machine, which may matter more than its figure-only limitation.

### A community member is an investor, and nine people already said yes eight days ago

At Web Summit in Lisbon he met the founder of a project called Staaake, a former financier from Switzerland who runs dinners, lunches and walks and has built a community of several thousand people. The founder's position, as the post reports it: an investor can be any member of your community, so building a community of users matters more than hunting investors, because part of that community eventually becomes your investors.

Against that he sets his own experience with an investor network he had paid into. He bought a subscription, scraped the roster, started working the contacts, and concluded that all of it was worthless.

Both halves check out, and the second one checks out more narrowly than the post implies. The network publishes its own scale and does not hide it: **5,000+ vetted investors across 130+ countries, 40+ local chapters, 200+ investor events a year.** So the verdict is about conversion rather than about size. A roster of five thousand investors produced nothing for him, which is a real and useful finding, and a different claim from "the network is fake".

The more interesting measurement is on his own side. The post says the sensible move is to build his community here, on Facebook, among the people who read him. A commenter under the post put that audience at nine thousand - their figure, not ours. Meanwhile the surfaces he already owns are **145,839 subscribers on the English Telegram channel**, 3,400 on the Russian one, and **4,629 people in the English engineer chat with 282 of them online**, plus 86 followers and 241 public repositories on GitHub.

So the community of engineers he describes as a future asset - "if I have a community of engineers, that is great" - is 4,629 people sitting in a chat right now. The thing proposed as new is mostly a routing problem between surfaces that already exist, and the surface he picked to build on is the smallest of the four. That is not an argument against Facebook, which is where the conversation actually happens; it is an argument that the audience is not the missing piece.

And the missing piece has a date on it. On 3 November he posted a direct call: *please, friends, those of you who know how to do this, be co-founders of our community.* Nine people replied in that thread. Three of them ended with a question rather than a compliment - how deep is the CRM and how many contacts does it hold; whether their goals overlap with his, from somebody who says they run events for a living; and an offer of an introduction to the founder of a developer club, with a proposed split of roles between the three of them.

In the publicly visible part of that thread, none of the three has an answer.

That is the whole post, read against its own evidence. "Part of your community becomes your investors" is a two-step mechanism, and the second step is replying to the people who put their hand up. Nine did, eight days ago, and the sharpest piece of advice in the new thread says the same thing from the other direction: the base is not segmented, so when you need a specific person you write a post and hope they see it, with maybe a one-in-five chance, instead of querying the base and writing to them directly.

The CRM that would answer that already exists - his own 3 November post says he will think about how to use it. The gap is not between having a base and not having one. It is between having it and querying it.

⚠️ Instrument notes. Channel and repository counts are live single reads from one machine; the Telegram numbers come from public pages, so they are what the platform chooses to display. The thread metrics were read without logging in: reaction and share counts are what a logged-out page exposes, the count of people in a thread counts visible comment authors and may miss nested replies, and so "no reply from the author" is weaker evidence than a logged-in check would be. The nine-thousand Facebook figure and the tenfold segmentation estimate are both commenters' claims, not measurements. No CRM count was taken - those databases are not on this node. And the project name does not resolve cleanly: one domain answers under that spelling with a description that does not match a community business.

## How it connects

Four posts on 11 November. The first two are the two halves of one argument, the third is a small task that inherits the same shape, and the fourth moves the same shape from machines to people.

The first three describe a system where the intelligence is deliberately the cheapest part. The corpus is **228,194 plain markdown files** that no model wrote. The retrieval is half lexical and half a small embedding transformer. The reasoning budget is explicitly capped, because **GPT-5 scores the same 97 percent at medium effort as at high** while **errors across models run more than 60 percent correlated**, so diversity buys what depth does not. And the drawing task repeats it in miniature: the free local tool that keeps the picture on the machine answered **200**, and the rung the post files as hardest - a child drawing frames by hand - is the one that teaches the concept a generative model skips.

⭐⭐⭐ Which gives the day a single line: in every post the expensive, impressive component is deliberately or accidentally the least important one. Files beat the model in the first. Five cheap rails beat one deep one in the second. A child with a pencil beats an upload button in the third. And in the fourth, **5,000+ vetted investors** in a paid network produced nothing, while nine people answering a free post are the asset. The thing worth paying for, every time, is the arrangement rather than the intelligence.

⭐ The friction is the same in all four: each post quietly overturns something of his own, and none of them says so. The first proposes starting online events, when **58 events have already been hosted**. The second retires his own 6 August order demanding maximum reasoning on every external rail, which is still sitting in our canon as current. The third asks for one ranked list across two incompatible jobs. And the fourth proposes building a community of engineers while **4,629 of them are already in his chat** and **145,839 on his channel**.

⭐⭐ And in all four, the practice had already drifted to where the post now points. Only **3 of 18 live panel calls ran at max effort**. The index that makes retrieval work already lives exactly where the hardware is rather than everywhere. The free drawing tool already exists and costs nothing. And the community already answered: his 3 November call for co-founders drew **9 people, 3 of them ending in a direct question**, with no reply from him in the publicly visible thread.

⭐⭐⭐ So the day has a second line, and it is less comfortable than the first. Every post identifies a thing to build, and in every case the thing exists and the gap is on the using side. The files are there and the model reads them. The rails are there and the dial is already turned down. The tool is there and nobody has tried it on a drawing. The engineers are there and three of them are waiting for an answer.
