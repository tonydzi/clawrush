### NotebookLM as a teaching machine, and the four podcasts that are not in the queue

The post is about a skill he says is insanely useful: using NotebookLM to learn things. One idea of his right now is to have it teach him to fly - he needs to pass eight or nine theory exams for a light pilot licence, and he says he can more or less fly already but would like the paperwork.

The part that is already running is interview preparation, and he lists what the skill makes. First, a podcast about the company: what it does, how it works. Second, a separate podcast about the specific interviewer, built from their public facts, essays and statements, so he knows what matters to them and can speak their language. Third, a podcast of interview variants, where the model invents several interviewer personalities - stricter, the advocate, the talker, the nitpicker - and he rehearses the whole conversation aloud, in English and in Russian, technically and behaviourally. And if the call will involve special terms, he prepares those separately.

The small thing first: **it is nine.** The EASA theory syllabus for a light aircraft licence has nine subjects - air law and ATC procedures, operational procedures, meteorology, human performance, principles of flight, aircraft general knowledge, navigation, flight performance and planning, and communications - examined as around 120 multiple-choice questions in total. So the "eight or nine" in the post resolves to nine, and it is the kind of question a podcast cannot answer for you because the answer is one lookup.

The bigger thing is the gap between the described skill and the queue behind it.

The skill exists and is specified exactly as the post says. Five related skills are on the shelf: interview prep, the podcast builder, the debrief, the NotebookLM skill itself and the YouTube publisher. The rule they implement is stricter than the post suggests - for hiring, the podcast is made **without asking him first**, and a single interview gets **three to five episodes**, not one, because one call exposes several gaps and each gap earns its own episode.

The queue is real too, and it is bigger than I expected: **49 episodes, 37 done, 12 waiting, none failed.** They are bilingual, Russian and English, between 24 and 126 megabytes each, and they run across two Google accounts in rotation.

And **not one of those 49 episodes is about a company or an interviewer.** The series in the queue are something else entirely: 16 episodes of one teaching series, 12 of a recall series, 12 of a research-camps series, and a handful on two financial topics.

Usage over the last sixty days says the same thing from the other side: the podcast builder was called **10 times**, interview prep **3 times**, the NotebookLM skill twice, the debrief once.

So the honest version of the post is: the skill is built, specified more strictly than described, and used three times in two months, while the production line next to it has shipped 37 episodes on entirely different subjects. Which is not a contradiction - it is a priority that nobody stated out loud. The machine is pointed at learning in general, and the post is about learning for interviews specifically.

There is one commenter, and the question is a good one: how is the audio generated - by the built-in generator or separate tools - and how does that eat usage? The answer to the first half is the built-in one: NotebookLM makes the audio itself, no external text-to-speech is involved. The second half changed recently and has no official number. Since early September 2026 the limits are compute-based, refreshing every five hours up to a weekly ceiling, rather than a fixed count of audio overviews per day; the per-day figures circulating in blogs predate that change and contradict each other.

Which, incidentally, is why the queue rotates accounts at all. That rotation is not a nicety - it exists because the bucket runs out, and it is the practical answer to the commenter's question: the audio comes out of the account's shared compute budget, so a long series needs more than one account.

⚠️ Instrument notes. The exam count comes from secondary sources - a flight school syllabus and a government form reproducing the rules - not from the current EASA AMC text, and the UK regulator runs its own alternative syllabus, so verify against the authority that will actually examine him. The skills were read from the shelf, not exercised. Queue figures are one status read on one machine at one moment. Usage counts come from our own skill counter over sixty days and count invocations, not outcomes - three interview-prep calls could each have produced five episodes. And there is no official NotebookLM limit figure for 2026; the compute-based model is reported by secondary sources.

### Pitch the community, not the product, and his own audience already voted

The founder of the Staaake community told him something he calls important: an investor should be pitched not just a great product, but a great community and great customers whose needs you are trying to meet. Show how obsessed you are with your customers, how hard you try to make them happy, how you grow the base.

Everything hangs on customer stories, he writes, because a product can pivot but the customer base is the thing that matters. It does not matter how brilliant the AI is. What matters is how many customers you have.

So for himself too he now thinks it makes sense to grow the community first. Making people into customers takes effort, yes, but for now he could at least teach everyone to use Claude Code, Codex and so on, and try to grow customers out of that later. Community first.

And then the most honest paragraph he has written in a while: his perception was distorted for a long time, because he raised money easily. He always raised money when there was hype, and investors handed it out readily. So he never really thought about how important growing a community is. And now he has understood: community management is the most important thing.

The conclusion is right, and his own numbers support it better than the founder's advice does. The sequencing is what the measurements argue with.

Because the thing he proposes to do "for now" he already did, on 21 October, and it is his best-performing post of the period by a wide margin.

Roughly **eighty times** the reactions on offering help versus reasoning about community, inside the same audience and the same three weeks.

And the replies under that October post were not applause. They were intake. "I'm in", twice, from different people. "I'd very much like to take part." "Can it be set up? I'd gladly join." "Your help would be invaluable." One person left the thread and went to his DMs: *I wrote to you in PM about a marketing problem, can a swarm of AI solve that?* One commercial opener from somebody researching this in their own small business, who added that it would probably be useful to him too. And one long, serious technical objection about how to balance what you give the swarm against what you take, and whether lending your own subscriptions to other members is sound at all.

Six people saying they are available. One inbound DM. One commercial lead. One reviewer worth arguing with. From a single post, twenty-three days ago.

In the publicly visible threads, none of them has an answer.

Which is why the post's own conclusion lands differently than intended. Community management is indeed the most important thing - and it was started on 21 October, it worked better than anything else he published, and it stopped at the step where somebody replies.

There is one more number worth putting next to "pitch your customers". The starter kit was cloned **743 times by unique machines while 88 humans looked at the page.** That ratio describes agent traffic, not customers. A clone is a fetch; a customer story needs a person with a before and an after. Six people volunteered to be that person.

⚠️ Instrument notes. All engagement figures were read anonymously from the post pages, so counts are what a logged-out reader sees and nested replies may be missing - which makes "no author reply" weaker evidence than a logged-in check. The four posts differ in age, from twenty-three days to one, so the newest numbers are not final and the comparison is indicative rather than exact. Channel, repository and clone figures are single live reads, the clone ratio from a measurement on 10 November. And the self-diagnosis about raising money easily is unverified here: those records are not on this machine.

## How it connects

Two posts on 12 November, and together they make a sharper point than either makes alone: both describe a machine that is already running, and in both cases the thing that stopped is the human step at the end.

The first is about teaching himself with NotebookLM. The skill is built, specified more strictly than the post describes - **3 to 5 episodes per interview, made without asking first** - and the line beside it has shipped **37 episodes out of 49 with zero failures.** None of those 49 is about a company or an interviewer. Interview prep was called **3 times in 60 days**.

The second is about pitching a community rather than a product. The thing he proposes to start doing he already did on 21 October, and it drew **165 reactions and 17 shares** against **2 reactions** for this post reasoning about the same subject. Six people replied with "I'm in". None has an answer three weeks later.

⭐⭐⭐ So the day has one line: **the production is fine and the follow-through is the bottleneck.** 37 episodes shipped, zero failures, pointed at the wrong queue. 165 reactions, six volunteers, nobody onboarded. In both cases the expensive machinery works and the cheap human step - naming the priority, answering a comment - is where it stops.

⭐⭐ And the two posts measure the same mistake from opposite ends. The podcast post reaches for a tool before a lookup: **nine exam subjects, one search**, while proposing an audio course to find out. The community post reaches for a principle before a reply: six people are waiting, while the post works out that community management matters. Tool before lookup, principle before reply.

⭐ The honest version of both: he is not short of capability or of audience. The starter kit was cloned **743 times by machines against 88 human visitors**, and the queue has 12 episodes waiting while interview prep sits unused. What is short is the one step that cannot be automated, which is deciding what to point all of it at.
