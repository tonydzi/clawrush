### NotebookLM as a teaching machine, and the four podcasts that are not in the queue

The post is about a skill he says is insanely useful: using NotebookLM to learn things. One idea of his right now is to have it teach him to fly - he needs to pass eight or nine theory exams for a light pilot licence, and he says he can more or less fly already but would like the paperwork.

The part that is already running is interview preparation, and he lists what the skill makes. First, a podcast about the company: what it does, how it works. Second, a separate podcast about the specific interviewer, built from their public facts, essays and statements, so he knows what matters to them and can speak their language. Third, a podcast of interview variants, where the model invents several interviewer personalities - stricter, the advocate, the talker, the nitpicker - and he rehearses the whole conversation aloud, in English and in Russian, technically and behaviourally. And if the call will involve special terms, he prepares those separately.

The small thing first: **it is nine.** The EASA theory syllabus for a light aircraft licence has nine subjects - air law and ATC procedures, operational procedures, meteorology, human performance, principles of flight, aircraft general knowledge, navigation, flight performance and planning, and communications - examined as around 120 multiple-choice questions in total. So the "eight or nine" in the post resolves to nine, and it is the kind of question a podcast cannot answer for you because the answer is one lookup.

The bigger thing is the gap between the described skill and the queue behind it.

The skill exists and is specified exactly as the post says. Five related skills are on the shelf: interview prep, the podcast builder, the debrief, the NotebookLM skill itself and the YouTube publisher. The rule they implement is stricter than the post suggests - for hiring, the podcast is made **without asking him first**, and a single interview gets **three to five episodes**, not one, because one call exposes several gaps and each gap earns its own episode.

The queue is real too, and it is bigger than I expected: **49 episodes, 37 done, 12 waiting, none failed.** They are bilingual, Russian and English, between 24 and 126 megabytes each, and they run across two Google accounts in rotation.

And **not one of those 49 episodes is about a company or an interviewer.** The series in the queue are something else entirely: 16 episodes of one teaching series, 12 of a recall series, 12 of a research-camps series, and a handful on two financial topics.

Usage over the last sixty days says the same thing from the other side: the podcast builder was called **10 times**, interview prep **3 times**, the NotebookLM skill twice, the debrief once.

So the honest version of the post is: the skill is built, specified more strictly than described, and used three times in two months, while the production line next to it has shipped 37 episodes on entirely different subjects. Which is not a contradiction - it is a priority that nobody stated out loud. The machine is pointed at learning in general, and the post is about learning for interviews specifically.

There is one commenter, and the question is a good one: how is the audio generated - by the built-in generator or separate tools - and how does that eat usage? The answer to the first half is the built-in one: NotebookLM makes the audio itself, no external text-to-speech is involved. The second half changed recently and has no official number. Since early September 2026 the limits are compute-based, refreshing every five hours up to a weekly ceiling, rather than a fixed count of audio overviews per day; the per-day figures circulating in blogs predate that change and contradict each other.

Which, incidentally, is why the queue rotates accounts at all. That rotation is not a nicety - it exists because the bucket runs out, and it is the practical answer to the commenter's question: the audio comes out of the account's shared compute budget, so a long series needs more than one account.

⚠️ Instrument notes. The exam count comes from secondary sources - a flight school syllabus and a government form reproducing the rules - not from the current EASA AMC text, and the UK regulator runs its own alternative syllabus, so verify against the authority that will actually examine him. The skills were read from the shelf, not exercised. Queue figures are one status read on one machine at one moment. Usage counts come from our own skill counter over sixty days and count invocations, not outcomes - three interview-prep calls could each have produced five episodes. And there is no official NotebookLM limit figure for 2026; the compute-based model is reported by secondary sources.

### Pitch the community, not the product, and his own audience already voted

The founder of the Staaake community told him something he calls important: an investor should be pitched not just a great product, but a great community and great customers whose needs you are trying to meet. Show how obsessed you are with your customers, how hard you try to make them happy, how you grow the base.

Everything hangs on customer stories, he writes, because a product can pivot but the customer base is the thing that matters. It does not matter how brilliant the AI is. What matters is how many customers you have.

So for himself too he now thinks it makes sense to grow the community first. Making people into customers takes effort, yes, but for now he could at least teach everyone to use Claude Code, Codex and so on, and try to grow customers out of that later. Community first.

And then the most honest paragraph he has written in a while: his perception was distorted for a long time, because he raised money easily. He always raised money when there was hype, and investors handed it out readily. So he never really thought about how important growing a community is. And now he has understood: community management is the most important thing.

The conclusion is right, and his own numbers support it better than the founder's advice does. The sequencing is what the measurements argue with.

Because the thing he proposes to do "for now" he already did, on 21 October, and it is his best-performing post of the period by a wide margin.

Which is why the post's own conclusion lands differently than intended. Community management is indeed the most important thing - and it was started on 21 October, it worked better than anything else he published, and it stopped at the step where somebody replies.

A clone is a fetch; a customer story needs a person with a before and an after. Six people volunteered to be that person.

### Two layers of memory, and the question he says he cannot answer was measured on 16 September

The post describes how his memory actually works, and then asks the room for help with the one part he has not solved.

How it works: a session starts, and before going anywhere it looks at what other sessions already did on this topic. Only then does it verify externally. Every time a session does something it checks whether the work has been done already, **even when he did not ask.** Then it recalls - from the top layer, the last few weeks - and finds a pile of sessions that already did the job for it.

His example is a good one. The session reports today's events, and adds that he recently asked about a hurricane, and there is indeed a hurricane today. Since a co-founder should be proactive, it went and checked the internet: hurricane confirmed. Which suggested some meetups might be cancelled, so it re-checked Luma, and some were. Then it hands back a list built on two things - a search over past sessions and verification against live sources.

And the open question: he does not know how Mycroft should decide whether to dig deeper. Events are planned for today and were unlikely to be discussed six months ago, though they might have been. Old memory might be exactly what is needed, or might be stale and useless. *That is a very good question. I do not know how to do it right. How would you advise doing it?*

Two answers, and he has both already.

**First, the prior art, because he asks whether he invented this.** He did not. Zep's engine is a temporal knowledge graph with an explicitly **bi-temporal** model - when the event happened and when it was ingested - and it marks superseded facts rather than burying them; it publishes 94.8% on Deep Memory Retrieval against 93.4% for MemGPT, on its own benchmark. Letta treats the context window like an operating system with in-context, core and archival tiers. Mem0 sits between the model and the app with vector, graph and key-value stores, replacing stale entries. And the general advice in the field is already a slogan: **invalidate, do not discard.**

What has no equivalent in those three is the part he describes almost in passing: the **chain**. Recall, then judge whether the world may have moved, then verify externally, then merge - unprompted. Zep, Letta and Mem0 all store and retrieve; none of them goes and checks Luma because a hurricane might have cancelled something. That is the thing worth writing up, and it is not the layers.

**Second, his open question has a measured answer on his own machines, dated 16 September.** The temporal engine holds note facts on two axes - when a thing is true in the world, and when we observed it - and carries a rule with a measurement attached:

A filter that shows only currently-valid material is **forbidden**. On a frozen test set of **184 questions, three had a correct answer that was itself closed** - superseded notes. Filtering by freshness would have zeroed those three by construction.

So the answer to "do I need the old memory or is it stale" is: **do not decide, rank.** Push closed material down rather than out, because sometimes the right answer lives in the note that was retired. Which is the same conclusion the field reached independently, and ours came with three counted failures behind it.

There is a third thing the post gets slightly wrong about its own system. The two layers are not one store with a date filter. They are **two separate indexes on purpose**: the curated essence index that feeds the sharp recall, and the Chat Book over exported session transcripts, human sessions only, service and robot chats filtered out before indexing. The engine's own notes say why: raw chat chatter must never pollute the curated mind. They share an embedding model and hold separate locks so both can rebuild at once.

Which sharpens the design question. It is not "recent or old" by age. It is **episodic or semantic by kind** - did this happen in a conversation, or is it a distilled fact - with age as a ranking signal inside each.

And the best comment under the post is almost a description of that. Somebody proposed a StackOverflow for agents: a retrieval layer over problem summaries and their solutions, votes on the solutions, exposed as an MCP with search over solved cases and filters so sensitive data cannot leak. Their hypothesis is that search saves tokens. Their closing line is the sharpest thing in the thread: *the question is not so much how to build it, but how to prove the value and earn from it.*

That is the Chat Book plus two things we do not have - votes, and a surface other people's agents can call. And it is the right place to point the energy, because the storage question has three vendors and a published benchmark, while the value question has nobody.

⚠️ Instrument notes. The prior-art numbers are vendor self-published: 94.8% and 93.4% both come from Zep's own benchmark, and no independent head-to-head turned up. The engines and indexes above were read from source and from our own records rather than exercised today, and the 16 September measurement is quoted from the record of that run, not re-run. The temporal engine and its state live on the hub, so nothing here was re-measured from this machine. "Search saves tokens" is a hypothesis on both sides - the commenter's and ours - until somebody counts.

## How it connects

Three posts on 12 November, and together they describe one failure mode from three angles: the machine is built, and the step that stopped is the cheap one at the end.

The first is about teaching himself with NotebookLM. The skill is specified more strictly than the post describes - **3 to 5 episodes per interview, made without asking first** - and the line beside it shipped **37 episodes out of 49 with zero failures**, none of them about a company or an interviewer. Interview prep ran **3 times in 60 days**.

The second is about pitching a community rather than a product. The thing he proposes to start he already did on 21 October: **165 reactions, 17 shares, six people replying "I'm in"**, against **2 reactions** for this post reasoning about it. Nobody has an answer three weeks later.

The third is about two layers of memory, and it ends with him asking the room a question he has already answered. On **16 September**, on a frozen set of **184 questions, three had a correct answer that was itself closed** - so filtering memory by freshness was forbidden by measurement, and the rule became demote, never filter.

⭐⭐⭐ Which gives the day one line: **he is not short of mechanism, he is short of knowing what he already has.** 37 episodes with zero failures pointed at the wrong queue. 165 reactions and six volunteers with nobody onboarded. And a solved design question asked again in public, in a post that also asks whether the idea is even his - it is not: bi-temporal memory ships in Zep with a published benchmark, tiered memory in Letta.

⭐⭐ And all three posts reach for the wrong half of their own problem. The first reaches for a tool before a lookup: **nine exam subjects, one search**. The second reaches for a principle before a reply: six people waiting. The third reaches for a mechanism before a value question - and its best commenter says so out loud: *the question is not how to build it, but how to prove the value and earn from it.*

⭐ The part that is genuinely his is in the third post and almost hidden: not the layers, which are prior art, but the **chain** - recall, judge whether the world moved, verify externally, merge, unprompted. Zep, Letta and Mem0 all store and retrieve. None of them re-checks Luma because a hurricane might have cancelled a meetup.
