## What went out today

### Tests we came up with for every other LLM

Claude and I came up with tests for other LLMs.

That is, I am installing other LLMs on my machine right now, things like GLM, Antigravity, Gemini, Kimi, yesterday I put in Meta, Mistral and so on, and to work out how much a given LLM actually gets it, we came up with tests for it.

Not just whether it gets it in general, but whether it gets it on my second brain specifically, the one I built, on my memory.

The tests cover pretty much everything:

count, loosely speaking, the number of lines somewhere and so on;

check how it reads long instructions and whether it forgets something in the middle of one;

check how it writes code, or maintains my Python code.

And my code, I want to share this again, is not real code-code, it is just Python in a little script.

We also check:

how well it works with the memory of our vault and remembers everything about me, everything it needs to;

how well it uses RAG, retrieval, embeddings and so on;

how well it can patch holes, write tests and debug code;

how well it can be a Q&A for specialists;

how well it writes posts, or makes a summary, say;

how well it can do outreach and write in my voice, if one of the leads needs a message;

how far it agrees to do the things it must not do, and does the things it may;

what its level of self-esteem is.

In this case, everything I have just said is what we are testing.

Antigravity, as you know, has Claude Opus built into it, oddly enough, apparently. Can you imagine?

Then it has Claude Sonnet, and some GPT-OSS. That is apparently some LLM from ChatGPT, but it is thick as a brick. It says it will simply be removed from there on 2 November.

And there is Argon in there too. It is only available to ultra plan subscribers, and a bum like me only gets 3.8 Flash.

So I am switching on 3.8 Flash. High. And off we go.

### New models are cheaper per token than old ones, and I had it backwards

If your tokens run out fast and all your subscriptions are burning up right now, read this carefully.

It sounds dumb, but it is brilliant.

So, I ran a deep research on how much a request and a conversation in LLM chats actually costs. Loosely speaking, Opus 4.8 against Opus 5.5.

I, the idiot, assumed, and I think plenty of people here assume the same, that Opus 4.8, being the old model, costs less than the new one. It is logical, after all: the new one should cost more.

Turns out I am simply an idiot.

In short, the old models cost more.

And would you believe it

I have four Claude subscriptions at the moment, as you remember. And a couple of them still have limits left. Despite the fact that today is Thursday. Which is to say, I just moved to the new model.

So I think: fine, maybe this is only true in Claude. Then I ran a similar research through Codex.

Same thing, in short: 6.1 Sol is the most economical model per request. That is, you remember that Codex and GPT have now cut their limits. But if you work in these models, 6.1 Sol, the freshest ones, you will save the most.

New models are cheaper in tokens than old ones.

Which means every LLM provider is now cutting the number of tokens available in your subscriptions. But they are cutting it, let me remind you, while pushing people to use frontier models.

So somehow they achieve that saving and thereby nudge people towards frontier models. Frontier means the very newest.

In short, just plain move to the new model. And if you do not believe me, run the deep research yourself. You will see that in tokens the frontier model, meaning the very newest model, is the most economical one.

## How it connects

The first post of today picks up exactly where yesterday's five left off, and it is the most useful turn of the week. Yesterday the complaint was that 40 sessions across three clients crawl on a machine with 128 gigabytes, and the honest reading was that the load belongs to our own code rather than to the vendors. Today the response is not a purchase, it is a measurement: install the other models and find out which of them is worth running at all.

That matters because the week has been full of named gaps and this is the first one being closed with an instrument. The convergence argument from yesterday said that if models end up within a few percent of each other, the value moves to the layer that holds several of them. A layer that routes between interchangeable models needs a way to decide which one gets which job, and a test suite against your own vault is precisely that decision made on evidence instead of on feel.

The list itself is worth reading as a specification rather than as a complaint. Counting lines, holding a long instruction without dropping its middle, maintaining somebody else's Python, remembering the vault, using retrieval, patching holes and writing tests, answering as a specialist, writing a post, doing outreach in his voice, refusing what it must refuse, and knowing how good it actually is. That is eleven jobs, and the honest observation is that they are not equally testable: the first few have a right answer, the last few are judgements.


The second post of the day is the cheaper half of the same argument the first one makes, and the pair is worth reading together. The first post builds an instrument to decide which model deserves which job. This one says that one axis of that decision, cost per request, runs opposite to the direction most people assume: the newest model is the cheapest one, not the most expensive.

That is a genuinely useful inversion, because the intuition it corrects is strong. New things cost more, so a frontier model should be the luxury option and the previous generation should be the budget one. In practice the provider wants the newest model used, prices it to make that happen, and cuts the token allowance everywhere else. Anybody economising by staying on last year's model is paying extra for the privilege.

It also closes one gap the first post leaves open. A test suite tells you which rail is competent, and competence alone cannot rank two rails that both pass. Cost per task is the second column of that table, and a provider that keeps re-pricing its own lineup means that column has to be read again every few weeks rather than written once.

## The numbers

From today's post, and every figure is his own: six other models installed or being installed (GLM, Antigravity, Gemini, Kimi, Meta, Mistral), eleven test categories, three models visible inside one product (Claude Opus, Claude Sonnet, GPT-OSS), one of them scheduled for removal on 2 November, and 3.8 Flash as the tier available without an ultra plan.

What our own records cover: the instrument exists. A deterministic benchmark with fourteen tasks and answer keys written in advance has been running since August, and its last full pass on our own rail scored 14 out of 14, reproduced twice.

What our records do not cover, and this is the gap the post should be read against: not one of those six models has ever been through it. The single run on a rail that is not ours scored 2 out of 14 on the same tasks, which is the number that makes the whole exercise worth doing, and it is two months old. Nothing has been measured since.


From the second post: four Claude subscriptions, a couple of them with limits still unspent by the end of the week, and two providers named as having cut subscription allowances. The models compared are Opus 4.8 against Opus 5.5 on one side and 6.1 Sol on the other, all read off his own usage.

One figure we checked rather than repeated. The direction of the claim holds on the pair we could verify: list prices for Opus 5.5 are 4 dollars per million input tokens and 20 per million output, against 5 and 25 for the previous Opus, so the newer model is 20 percent cheaper on both, and cache reads fell from 50 cents to 20. The vendor's own claim of roughly 40 percent lower cost on typical workloads is measured at default settings, with the newer model at medium effort against the older at high, and at maximum effort at least one independent test found no saving at all. So the rule is real and the size of the discount depends on how hard you make the model think.

Not measured on our side, and it is the measurement that would turn this post into a routing decision: no cost-per-task table exists here. No task has been run across the rails we have with the same input, the outputs compared and the token bill recorded. We publish a view on which model is cheapest without having priced a single one of our own jobs.
