## Reading comprehension and listening comprehension are separate instruments, and only one of them gets read

**What happened:** several closed AI-engineer gatherings were attended. Technical content delivered as live speech, native pace, practitioner slang. Measured comprehension: about one word in three.

**The measurement, stated plainly:** ~33% on spoken domain English. The same person reads papers, docs, release notes and repo threads in that language daily without friction, so written comprehension is high. Nobody had ever measured the two separately, and the aggregate self-assessment ("my English is fine") was quietly carried from the written channel to the spoken one.

**Why the two diverge, mechanically.** Written technical English arrives pre-segmented: punctuation marks clause boundaries, terms are spelled in their indexed form, and the reader sets the clock. Spoken technical English in a room of practitioners has none of those: no boundary markers, heavy phonetic compression of exactly the high-information tokens (a framework name, a model name, a flag), and shared context that substitutes for half the sentence. The hardest words to hear are the ones carrying the most meaning, which is the opposite of how reading degrades.

**Generalises past language.** Any capability measured through one channel and consumed through another will drift the same way: a runbook that reads clearly and cannot be followed under load, a dashboard that is legible at rest and unreadable during an incident, a test suite green on recorded fixtures and blind on live traffic. The rule: **measure the channel you will actually be consuming through, not the channel that is convenient to measure.**

**The repair that matches the diagnosis:** hours of spoken domain audio (AI podcasts, conference talks, recorded engineering discussions) rather than more reading. Reading more does not train segmentation of continuous speech, and the written channel is already strong, so additional reading improves the metric that was never the problem.

**Not measured:** the actual delta. Written comprehension on comparable material has not been scored, so the gap is known to exist and not known in size. Also not instrumented: whether listening hours move the number, and how fast.

**Open item:** record a baseline on one spoken source (a single podcast episode, note the fraction of sentences fully understood), do a fixed number of listening hours, re-score the same source. Without a before-and-after on the same input there is no way to tell improvement from familiarity with one speaker.
