# 26 October 2026 - dev log

*Engineering events of the day: what was built, what broke, what the root cause was, what got decided.*

## 668 studies ordered, 443 applied, and nothing ordered for 24 days

Today's post describes a model that orders its own deep research from several vendors instead of answering from memory. Logged because we built exactly this, used it at scale, and then stopped, and the stopping is invisible from the outside.

The registry numbers are good and worth publishing: **668 ordered studies**, **443 applied**. Applied is the only status that means anything, because it says a result changed a decision, a note or a piece of code rather than landing in a folder. A 66 percent application rate on a research pipeline is a real figure, not a vanity one.

The other half of the same table is the problem. **175 parked, 25 dead** - about a quarter of all orders never reached a consumer. And the newest order is dated **2 October**, twenty-four days ago. So the honest description today is a working instrument that nobody has pulled the trigger on for three weeks, published in a post that speaks in the present tense.

**An idle pipeline and a broken pipeline look identical from the registry.** Status counts cannot tell them apart: both show the same last-order date, the same parked pile, no error anywhere. That is the actual defect here, and it is ours rather than the post's. A capability with no freshness check on its own output drifts into a claim about the past without anyone noticing, which is the same class as the benchmark that scored 14 out of 14 in August and has not run in anger since.

The cheap fix is not a new routine, it is a date. One line that reads the newest order in the registry and goes red past a threshold turns a silent gap into a visible one, and it costs a single query against a file we already maintain. Until that exists, the next person to read the registry will see 668 orders and conclude the thing is running.

Open and unmeasured: no freshness alarm on the last order date, no breakdown of why 175 studies parked (no consumer named, quorum never reached, or the question went stale), and no record of which of the three named vendor rails is currently answering, so the fanout's own health is unknown.

## Ordering the same question several times is a defence, not a luxury

Second item from the same post, logged because it is the design decision most people skip to save time.

The argument against one research run is not that one vendor is weak. It is that a single report gives you no signal about its own reliability: you get an answer and no way to weigh it. Running the same question across several rails produces disagreement, and disagreement is the only cheap evidence that a question is contested rather than settled. Our own canon arrived at the same place from the other direction and set a floor of three independent rails with a quorum of four out of six, precisely because agreement between two readings of the same source proves nothing.

The practical note for anyone copying this: assemble the reports rather than picking the best one. A synthesis that keeps the conflicts visible is more useful than a clean summary that hides which parts were only one rail's opinion, and it is the difference between a report you can act on and one you have to re-run.

Open and unmeasured: no count of how often the rails actually disagreed on the same question, which is the number that would prove the fanout earns its cost.

## 129 of 279 skills are bound to this house, and the bindings do not look like bindings

Second post of the day argues that copying somebody else's skills transfers nothing, because a skill encodes where things are rather than what to do. Logged because we could put a number on it, and the number is larger than the argument assumed.

Measured on this node: **279 skills live**, of which **129 contain at least one tie to our own environment**. Breakdown: 86 reference vault or bus path variables, 44 name specific fleet nodes, 36 carry chat ids or account handles. Forty-six percent.

The important part is the shape of those ties, not the count. **Absolute machine paths are at zero** - they were scrubbed in August when the public kit was cleaned, 23 private chat ids replaced along with them. So the obvious form of non-portability is gone, and what remains is worse for a stranger: an environment variable that resolves on our machines and to nothing on theirs, a node name that means something only inside our registry, a handle that addresses an account they do not own.

**A hardcoded path announces itself; a variable that silently resolves to empty does not.** The first fails loudly on the first run. The second runs, does nothing, and reports success, which is the same failure class as the empty lessons journal and the idle research pipeline logged earlier today.

Practical consequence for anything we publish: the useful export is not the skill file. It is the skill file plus a named list of the lines that are addresses. Our own kit has a contact footer and a licence on every file, and no such list on any of them.

Open and unmeasured: no per-skill manifest of which lines are environment-specific, no test that runs a skill with the variables deliberately unset to see whether it fails loudly or quietly, and no count of how many of the 36 handle-carrying skills would leak an address if published as-is.

## The marketplace question is open, and the registry says it was already answered once

Second item, logged because the post states something our own history contradicts.

The post says marketplaces have not been used yet. The registry says otherwise: on 14 August the public skill kit was prepared specifically for catalogues, 30 marketplaces were checked for liveness, and 13 of them turned out to be dead or invented. Two deep research orders from the same day cover exactly this ground: an atlas of agent-skill marketplaces and a survey of non-English venues.

So the promised research partly exists and is two months old. Noted rather than smoothed over, because the useful next step is not a new order; it is re-checking which of the 17 live venues from August are still live, which is a morning of HTTP requests rather than a research run.

## 124,952 leads, and not one Twitter column in the schema

Third post of the day opens with Twitter being where American AI engineers and crypto people live, and follows it with the CRM that makes lead work easier. Logged because reading the schema turns two adjacent sentences into one measurable limit.

Measured on the live base: **124,952 leads**, 21 columns, **78,507 rows with a usable handle** (63 percent). The identity columns are `telegram_id`, `telegram_type` and `source_account`.

**There is no `twitter`, `x_handle` or equivalent column anywhere in those 21.** The base is a Telegram base end to end: every row was acquired through a Telegram surface and is addressable only through one. On the skill shelf the CRM side is eleven live tools (`crm-sync`, `triage`, `pipeline`, `local-leads`, `photo-leads`, `telegram-lead-outreach`, three `sostav-*` tools, and the two X tools), and of those the X pair is `x-post` and `x-inbox-watch`: post a thing, watch replies. Neither one does outbound to a person.

So the capability reads as three separate layers, and only two of them exist: a channel where the audience is (Twitter), a database of leads (Telegram), and a rail between them (absent). The post does not claim the rail, and a reader will assume it, which is the reason to write the number down.

Also worth noting against the handle count: `dm_messages.db` was not found on this node at all, searched recursively across nine roots plus PATH. The message history behind those 124,952 rows lives somewhere else, which means the conversation layer cannot be audited from here even for the channel that does work.

Open and unmeasured: how many of the 78,507 handled leads have a discoverable Twitter account under the same name, what the match rate would be for a handle-to-handle lookup, and whether an outbound rail into X is even available to us given that the API access has been shut on their end since August.

## Three suspects for the slowdown, and the one we have already caught failing quietly

Fourth post of the day reports the machine crawling with 10 to 30 sessions open across Codex, Claude Code, Cursor and Antigravity, on 128 gigs of RAM. Three candidates named: the vault re-index, Screenpipe, a hanging microphone driver. The first is explicitly ruled out by observation ("I thought it was the re-index, but apparently not"). Logged because one of the remaining two has a dated failure in our own records.

**Screenpipe, on this node: ten skills on the shelf** (`screenpipe-api`, `screenpipe-cli`, `screenpipe-recall`, `screenpipe-worklog`, `screenpipe-focus-review`, `screenpipe-meeting-prep`, `screenpipe-meeting-follow-up`, `screenpipe-research-synthesis`, `screenpipe-durable-learning`, `screenpipe-shareable-recap`). So it is a load-bearing component here, not an experiment left running.

**And on 30 September we logged this:** the Screenpipe audio rail produced nothing for more than five days while the health check reported it as `active`. The component was not disabled and not crashed. It was running, recording nothing, and reporting success. That is the same failure class as the empty lessons journal and the idle research pipeline logged earlier today, and it is why "maybe the microphone driver hangs" deserves a measurement rather than a shrug.

**The arithmetic that kills the memory theory.** 128 gigabytes across 30 sessions is over 4 gigabytes per session before anything swaps. For processes talking to cloud APIs, holding a connection, a buffer and some JSON, that is implausible by an order of magnitude. So memory pressure is at most a small part of it, and the likelier limit is contention for scheduler time and disk: 30 processes plus four editors plus a syncing daemon plus whatever Screenpipe writes, all queueing for the same resource. The jerky mouse pointer fits contention far better than it fits a full memory bus.

Open and unmeasured, and this is the third day running that the same measurement is missing: resident memory per session, CPU share per tool, and the split of load between re-index, Screenpipe and the clients. Half an hour with a process list under real load produces all three. The difference it makes is a 256 gigabyte purchase against an afternoon of configuration.

**Correction on the vault figure, measured after the post went out.** The number in the post is 20 gigs. A live `du` on this node returns **58G** for the vault root. Two readings are possible and we are not choosing between them without more work: either the figure is stale, or he means the curated layer that actually gets indexed rather than the whole tree, which also holds raw originals and imports. Worth separating because the two numbers imply different cures: if the re-index walks 58 gigs, the fix is scoping what it walks; if it walks 20, the load is elsewhere. Also checked and worth recording: the RAG index files are not present on this node at all, which is expected rather than broken, since the embedding index lives where the GPU is.

## A model ranking for distillation, and the scoring that is not written down

Fifth post of the day reports a comparison on one specific job: distilling session data before it enters the second brain. Result as given: Opus best, Sonnet second, Terra clearly worst, Sol in the expensive pair and deliberately unused. Logged because the choice is structural, not cosmetic.

**Why the cheap rail matters here more than elsewhere.** Distillation is the write path into long-term memory. A weak answer in a chat is thrown away in a minute; a weak distillate is stored and then answers every future question built on it. And the failure is silent in the familiar way: the output still looks like a summary, so nothing in any status table goes red. Same shape as the empty lessons journal, the idle research pipeline and the Screenpipe audio rail logged earlier today.

**What our own setup already said.** The distillation skill is live on this node, and our routing canon states the same split this measurement found: thinking and synthesis to the strong models, groundwork to Sonnet, with the session-bench judge invoking Sonnet by name rather than a frontier model. So the post confirms a rule we already run on, which is worth more than a fresh opinion.

**The gap, and it is the honest part.** "I ran a study" is reported without the study. No cost per distillate per model on the same input, no number of samples, no stated criterion for what made one distillate better. Our records hold the routing rule but no scored comparison of distillation quality across rails. So the ranking is a practitioner's verdict, probably correct, and unreproducible by anyone else as written.

Cheapest way to close it, and it is the same half hour we keep not spending: take one fixed set of sessions, run it through each rail, store the distillates side by side with token cost, and score them on one named criterion. That turns a verdict into a table.

## A recruiter said tooling is assumed, and the skill at risk is reading output

Sixth post of the day reports one conversation from an interview loop: asked whether Claude Code was allowed in a LeetCode interview, the answer was that they assume candidates use tooling anyway and are not afraid of it. Logged because the conclusion drawn from it collides with everything else in today's log.

**What is evidence and what is forecast.** The quote is one person in one conversation, with no company named and no second source. We hold no record of it. The claim that in a few years no engineer will be able to read code is a prediction, and nothing on this fleet measures it. Both are reported here as what they are.

**The collision, and it is the useful part.** Every failure logged today was silent, and every one of them was caught the same way: by reading what a component actually produced and refusing to believe the status next to it.

- The lessons journal reported zero events for seven days across 264 routines. Zero looked like a good week.
- The research pipeline held 668 orders with the newest three weeks old. A full queue looked like an active one.
- The Screenpipe audio rail produced nothing for five days while the health check said `active`. A dead component reported success.
- The distillation ranking was published as a verdict with no scoring behind it. A conclusion looked like a measurement.

None of those four is a coding problem. All four are reading problems. So if the skill going extinct is reading the output rather than writing the input, the cost does not surface as broken code; it surfaces as a dashboard that is green and wrong. That is the single thread running through all six posts of this day.

**Practical consequence for anything we build.** An operator-shaped workflow needs the freshness check on the instrument's own output to be mandatory, not optional, because the human who used to notice by reading is the part being removed. We have that check for some components and not for others, and today produced four examples of the gap.
