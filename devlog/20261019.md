# 19 October 2026 - dev log

*Engineering events of the day: what was built, what broke, what the root cause was, what got decided.*

## A target of 500 conversations, and no field anywhere that records what a conversation produced

Today's post declares a measurable objective: roughly 50 to 60 meetups inside one week, roughly 500 new contacts. It is worth logging because the objective is specific and the instrument for judging it does not exist on our side.

What exists: a lane for finding and applying to events, a lane for turning photos taken at events into lead cards, and a CRM holding several hundred dossiers. What does not exist: **a field that links a contact to the event where it happened, and a field that records what came back.** No event id, no "met at", no outcome.

The consequence is not cosmetic. With those fields missing, the only reportable number at the end of the week is events attended, which measures effort, and effort is exactly what today's post says has been producing zero for two years. **Counting the thing you did instead of the thing it bought is how a garage looks productive for 24 months.**

The cheap instrument is two columns, not a system: on every card created or touched at an event, write the event name and date; on every reply, interview invite or referral, write which card it came from. That turns "50 meetups" into a conversion rate, and a conversion rate is the only form in which this plan can be judged, repeated, or abandoned on evidence.

Second thing worth recording, because it is a design note rather than a feeling. The nudge to go outside was produced by the orchestration layer built in that garage, not by a person. A system whose whole purpose is to work indoors recommended leaving the building, which means its instructions encode something about distribution that its author had not acted on. That is the sort of output worth keeping a log of: when the harness tells its operator to do something the operator has been avoiding, and the operator complies, the harness is doing strategy work and nobody is measuring that either.

Open and unmeasured: event attribution on CRM cards (no field, so zero knowledge), reply rate from event contacts (unanswerable until the above exists), and whether a declared "week of self-development" produces anything distinguishable from a normal week (no baseline recorded before it starts, and today was the day to record one).

## Continuity is the product, and it was inherited rather than built

The second post of the day makes an engineering claim worth isolating from the market thesis around it: for a companion system, the load-bearing feature is neither voice nor a face nor a body. It is that the thing remembers every prior conversation and never has to be re-explained.

That claim is correct and this installation satisfies it, so it is worth writing down exactly what satisfies it, because the list is shorter than it looks: a vault holding the operator's own corpus, retrieval over that vault, an instruction layer that is read at the start of every session, and a memory index that survives restarts. No part of that is novel. The behaviour that reads as personality is the instruction layer plus the corpus, nothing more.

**And here is the defect the post does not see, because its author cannot experience it: the continuity was inherited, not built.** This system felt like a partner from early on because the vault already contained fifteen years of messages, notes and history before anything was assembled on top. Retrieval had something to retrieve.

A new user installing the same architecture gets the pipes and an empty reservoir. On day one the system has no shared history, so it behaves exactly like the products the post criticises for resetting, and it stays that way until enough material accumulates. **Cold start is the whole engineering problem of a companion product, and in our case it was skipped by accident of biography.**

That turns into a concrete and currently unanswered question: what is the minimum corpus at which this stops feeling generic? Not a philosophical question, a measurable one. Take the stack, point it at a fresh vault, feed it N days of real material, and ask at what N the responses start depending on the person rather than on the instruction layer. We have never run that test, and we cannot answer it from this installation because its vault has been full from the start.

The cheap version is a shadow run: one empty vault, one operator who is not the author, a week of ordinary use, and a log of how many answers referenced anything specific to that person. That number at day 1 versus day 7 is the only honest input to the question of whether this is a product or a personal artefact.

Open and unmeasured: cold-start behaviour on an empty vault (never tested), minimum corpus for personalised retrieval (unknown), and whether anyone other than the builder changes behaviour because of it (one data point, and it is the builder, in the other post from today).

## Retrieval by approximate description works, and the offer to install it skips the hard half

The third post of the day states the capability the other two assume: query a personal corpus without knowing a name or a date, by describing the episode loosely, and get the right record back.

That is worth logging precisely because it is not search. Keyword search requires the querier to supply a token that exists in the target. This path requires only an approximate description of an event, which means the work is done by embeddings over a corpus that was ingested from several messengers and mailboxes into one place. **The reason it performs well is not model quality, it is haystack completeness: fifteen years of one person's actual material, with the gaps filled by ingestion rather than by curation.**

Two engineering notes follow from that, and they point in opposite directions.

First, this is the strongest demonstrated feature in the whole stack. It has been exercised in daily use, it has produced details the owner had genuinely forgotten, and it does not depend on anything unreleased. When the post says "describe it loosely and Mycroft will find it", that claim holds.

Second, the closing offer, "if you want one, I will be glad to help", hands over the architecture and not the corpus, which is the half that does the work. A new installation has empty retrieval and answers from the instruction layer, so for some unmeasured period it behaves like a generic assistant with a confident tone. **We have no number for how long that period is, and the offer is currently made without one.**

The cheap instrument is the same shadow run already named in today's other entry, with one extra column: on a fresh vault, log for each answer whether anything retrieved came from the user's own material or whether the response was produced from instructions alone. The ratio over the first week is the honest answer to "how soon is this useful to you", and it is the first thing anyone accepting the offer will want to know.

One more item, logged as unverified rather than as a feature: the post lists booking hotels and buying tickets alongside recall. Those are actions with money attached, and no end-to-end run of either is recorded. Recall is demonstrated, transactions are asserted, and the two should not travel in the same sentence without that distinction.

Open and unmeasured: time-to-usefulness on an empty vault (no number), share of answers grounded in user material versus instructions (not logged), and any completed booking or purchase performed by the system (no record).

## Forking a persona over one vault, and the convergence test nobody has run

The fourth post of the day describes a second persona derived from the first: same memory, same retrieval, different character. There is a named customer behind it, a psychologist who needs patient-load operations, so this is a build rather than a thought experiment.

The architectural claim is the interesting part and it is falsifiable. **If the vault, the retrieval layer and the tooling stay fixed, then personality lives entirely in the instruction layer, and personas are forkable by swapping that one file.** Everything in this stack is consistent with that: what reads as a specific someone is a character sheet plus a corpus, and only one of those two is being changed here.

What has not been tested is whether the fork holds. Two instruction layers over one vault can converge, because retrieval returns the same material, the same phrasings come back, and after enough turns both personas start sounding like whoever wrote the prompts. **A persona that collapses into the original voice after a week is a skin, not a second agent, and we have no measurement either way.**

The experiment is cheap and worth running before the second persona is handed to anyone: same set of twenty prompts to both personas, same vault, scored by a third party on whether the author can be told apart at day 1 and at day 7. If the distinguishability drops, the design needs separate memory or separate retrieval weighting, not just a different tone.

Second item, logged because it is a requirement rather than a preference. The persona is specified to push back: to chase the operator and object when work is not done. That matches what this repository keeps learning from the other direction, where approval gates that always approve stop being gates and silent routines stop being instruments. An assistant that never objects is a defect with good manners, and specifying objection as a persona trait is a cleaner fix than bolting a reminder on afterwards.

Third, the operational note. The offer to configure this for other people is now repeated twice in one day and remains entirely manual. **A hand-configured install is a service with a hard ceiling in heads, not a product**, and nothing currently in the pipeline converts one into the other. There is no installer, no onboarding script, no definition of which of the two it is meant to be. That is a decision rather than a bug, but it is undecided in public, which means every new taker is a bespoke engagement.

Open and unmeasured: persona distinguishability over a shared vault at day 1 versus day 7 (never tested), whether a forked persona needs its own memory partition (unknown), and the cost in hours of a single hand-configured install (never timed, and it is the number that decides service versus product).
