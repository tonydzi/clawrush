### ⭐⭐ The strongest case for fine-tuning is in his own thread

A reader on a cheap fast model that truncates context and does not read documentation fully: *"the information will be in the harness, but it will not read it - and that is exactly where fine-tuning is needed."*

That is the one failure mode where memory-plus-prompting cannot substitute for weights: the retrieval works, the context is assembled, and the model does not read it. Worth separating from the general "fine-tune for style" argument, which memory handles better.

Another reader supplied an experiment design for free: **one model, three conditions - plain prompt, hard reading protocol, fine-tune - and publish the numbers.** Cheapest possible first step of the planned research, and it answers the question the post is actually asking.

### Open, in order of payoff

4. **Separate the two fine-tune arguments.** Style and voice: memory wins on deletability. A model that will not read its context: only weights help. The post merges them.
5. **Thin out the templated phrasing in distribution.** The phrase is his, the repetition is the pipeline's, and two readers in two days have now named it.

⚠️ Share-of-subscribers does not separate "dead reach" from "inflated subscriber list" - both produce the same ratio from outside. What the number does establish is that the 144,878 figure cannot be used as audience size in any plan.

### ⭐⭐⭐ (a) is published, and the platform is explicitly not the variable

- **Nguyen et al., Psychological Bulletin, 2025**: 71 studies, 70 meta-analysed, **r = -0.34** for short-form video engagement against cognitive performance. The attention-specific subset is 14 studies, **all conducted in China**.
- **JMIR, 2026**: 58 studies, **96,676 participants**, mental health outcomes, **r = -0.21**.
- **For cognition, platform made no difference.** The format is the unit of analysis.
- The association is strongest for **addiction-style measures** ("habitual, uncontrollable use") and weakest for **time-based** measures.
- Both reviews are correlational; the authors warn against causal reading.

This inverts part (b): he asks how to configure the feed so only wanted content arrives. The measured risk sits in loss of control, not in feed contents or minutes watched. A well-curated feed consumed compulsively is on the wrong side of the strongest effect in the literature.

### ⚠️ (a) was also half-answered by our own corpus, 8 weeks ago

`DR26-09-14-ZB-04-1613` (screens, TV and games for children) recorded: aggregate screen time is a weak predictor, **under 0.4% of variance** for behaviour/attention problems, which its sources likened to eating potatoes; the Twenge/Haidt vs Orben/Przybylski dispute is **unresolved for 2024-2026**; and the effect is visible specifically in **visual social networks (Instagram/TikTok) and vulnerable teenage girls** rather than in screens generally.

So the honest framing for the commission is "extend and re-point", not "find out". The registry has no TikTok-as-market entry; the three nearest parked items (`DR26-09-04-HUB-01-2323` Playwright lanes, `DR26-09-15-ZB-04-0758` social schedulers with MCP, `DR26-07-06-HUB-02` reader-engagement inserts) are about rails and posting, not about audience economics.

### ⭐⭐ (b) the MCP ecosystem exists and points the other way

- **Official**: TikTok shipped an MCP server in May 2026 at TikTok World, on its business API, for advertisers to run campaigns through their own agents (reported ~40 tools in a layered mode).
- **Third-party publishing**: Blotato, Upload-Post (Blotato states it uses the official API rather than browser automation).
- **Third-party reading**: Apify, HasData - public profiles, videos, comments, keyword search as JSON.
- **Not found**: an MCP, agent or filter that sits between a user and the recommendation engine to suppress what reaches him. Everything shipped is built for reaching an audience, not for being reached less.

⚠️ One community wrapper notes TikTok's Business API answers **HTTP 200 even on failure**, which matters for any rail we build on it.

### ⚠️ (c) and the money half of (d) have no public answer

US user base is reported around 136 million. Demographics disagree between sources: 55.7% / 44.3% male-female in one January 2026 figure against 45.5% female / 37.3% male for the 18+ ad audience in another. The only income datum I reached is an agency claim that teenage users skew to households under $30K, unverified against a primary dataset.

On the actual question - what share of TikTok users pay for online courses, and at what price - **nothing**. Average order value per platform is not derivable from open sources. That part has to be measured with a live offer, not researched.

⚠️ And TikTok is absent from `targets.json` entirely (repo, telegram, threads, x, dzen, devto). There is no account to measure from, so any audience test there starts with creating one.

### Open, in order of payoff

1. **Fix the reach before adding a platform.** 176,992 subscriptions producing 300 readers is the finding. A sixth platform inherits that problem rather than solving it, and the English channel specifically needs a diagnosis (reach throttling vs inflated list) before its 144,878 appears in any plan again.
2. **Re-point the existing DR instead of commissioning part (a).** `DR26-09-14-ZB-04-1613` plus Nguyen 2025 and JMIR 2026 cover the harm question. What is missing is the adult, non-child, creator-side slice.
3. **Change part (b)'s question.** From "how do I filter the feed" to "how do I not lose control of the session", because that is where the measured effect lives.
4. **Measure the money, do not research it.** No open source answers average order value for AI education per platform. The cheapest real test is one offer against the X audience, where reach is 1.0% rather than 0.044%.
5. **Name the instrument before trusting it.** My own first measurement of the core number was off by a factor of two for a methodological reason that took one extra pass to find.

⚠️ Instrument notes. Subscriber and view counts are single reads today from one machine via the Telegram API; X views come from the public tweet endpoint over 4 tweets, so ~230 is a small sample. Facebook follower count and Threads follower count could not be read: both require a logged-in session, and FB write operations on this node are barred by policy. Meta-analysis figures are from search summaries of the primary papers, not from reading the Psychological Bulletin and JMIR PDFs directly - the coefficients should be confirmed against the papers before they appear in a client-facing document. The 14-study China-only detail for the attention subset comes from the same summary.

### How the four posts connect

Four posts on 13 November, and all four read a dial that measures something other than the thing being asked about.

**Post 1**: is Workshop Labs worth learning from, and would he apply there. The name on GitHub holds 6 repos and 0 stars; the tool he described wanting sits with the company that acquired them, at 4,261 stars. The dial was the name.

**Post 2**: a bar of 10 external contributors, and why people star and fork his repos. He has 36 merged external pull requests. His most-starred repository has one fork and zero outside contributions; the one carrying 25 of those merges has fewer stars and sixteen forks. The dial was stars.

**Post 3**: where his audience is, and whether TikTok holds it. 176,992 subscriptions across Telegram and X deliver about 300 readers per post, 64 of them from a channel with 144,878 subscribers. The dial was subscriber count.

**Post 4**: "I think I already contributed to her project, I should check." He is the third-largest contributor to it, with three pull requests merged the same day he opened them. The dial was memory.

The fourth one is the sharpest version of the pattern, because the evidence was in his own name. Posts 1 through 3 required measuring something external - a stranger's repository, a market, a channel's reach. Post 4 required reading a contributor list that has his handle on it, in third place.

And the second thread closes across all four. Post 2 found where strangers convert into his contributors at 69%: a graph-memory repository, eleven people, substantive pull requests. Post 1 named what that audience wants: personal agents backed by fine-tuning or memory on your own data. Post 4 shows him already doing the mirror image of post 2 - being the stranger who contributes, in a personal-agent project, merged within hours by a maintainer who builds exactly that. Post 3 is the only one of the four that goes looking for an audience somewhere he has no account and no measurement.
