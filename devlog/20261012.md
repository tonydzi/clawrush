# 12 October 2026 - dev log

*This file collects the engineering events of this date and is appended as more land.*

## A target list from a model is a hypothesis with an attribution, not a market study

**What happened:** the question "what can we make money on" was put to the digital co-founder, and the answer came back as an ordered segment list with pricing, not as a direction.

**The output, recorded as given:** sell first to US accounting and tax firms of 8 to 50 people, margins 50 to 60%, on the grounds that they bring their own SMB clients; then HVAC (plumbers and electricians); then dentists; not pizzerias, margin too thin. Offer ladder: audit at three grand over 5 days, workflow build over 10 to 20 days, 3 to 5 months of support. Reported close time: 2 to 6 weeks for owners of firms with $0 to 100 million annual revenue.

**The selection rule underneath, which is the transferable part:** rank candidate segments by whether a won customer brings further customers, not by margin or ease of sale. A first client that carries a book of its own is a distribution channel; a first client with the same margin and no book is one deal. Only the first kind compounds, which is why segment order matters more than segment quality.

**Why the attribution in the post is load-bearing.** Every number above came out of one model conversation. Written as the owner's own conclusions they would read as market research; written as "here is what my co-founder said" they read as what they are, a hypothesis to test. That is the same discipline as reporting a measured value with its instrument.

**Internal inconsistency, named not smoothed:** "8 to 50 people" and "$0 to 100 million in annual revenue" are not the same population. Both are in the source as given; neither has been reconciled.

**The actual blocker, and it is not strategy:** the CRM holds no leads in any of the three segments. Zero accountants, zero HVAC contractors, zero dentists. Whatever the ordering rule says, the executable next step is lead acquisition in one named segment, not further segment analysis.

**Prior experience that cuts against the timeline:** warming up Chinese and Japanese B2B clients ran on a roughly one-year cycle with different deal sizes. The claim that US SMB closes in 2 to 6 weeks is therefore a claim about a market the owner has not sold into, carried over from a model rather than from experience.

**Not measured:** every figure above. No deal, no quote accepted, no conversion. Nothing here has been priced against a real buyer.

**Open item:** pick one segment, acquire 20 named leads, and test the close-time claim against reality before building any offer material. That is cheap and it falsifies the most expensive assumption in the plan.

## An audit badge is a liability-transfer mechanism, not a quality signal

**What happened:** the term Red Team was identified as the name for work already being done (adversarial analysis of agents in public GitHub repositories), and the business structure around agent auditing was sketched out as a four-party chain.

**The craft distinction, which is the technical core.** A smart contract audit is mathematical: state machines, arithmetic, invariants you can prove. An agent audit contains no maths. The target consumes natural-language instructions and reasons from them, so the attack surface is psychology and logic: contradictory instructions, authority claims, framing, pressure, context injection. The two jobs share the word "audit" and almost no method. Anyone moving from one to the other should expect to relearn technique, not port it.

**The structural finding, stated as a mechanism:** a business afraid to deploy an agent is not primarily worried about quality, it is worried about liability. In the US, a support agent that promises something can expose the company to suit. That reframes the product: **what an audit sells is not confidence, it is a transfer of risk**, and risk transfer requires a counterparty.

**Hence four parties, not two.** (1) The business that wants the agent and fears it. (2) The security company issuing the badge. (3) The insurer, willing to underwrite but requiring a standard built collaboratively rather than issued unilaterally. (4) The integrator reselling agents and carrying the badge. Possibly two insurers: one covering the integrator, one covering the end business.

**Where the load actually sits:** on the insurer. A badge no insurer accepts as a condition of cover changes nothing about the deployment decision; it is a logo. A badge that keeps a policy from lapsing changes the decision entirely. Therefore the audit company's hardest work is recognition agreements, not test design, which is the opposite of where an engineering team's instinct sends it.

**Second-order effect worth recording:** the presence of credible auditors *accelerates* adoption rather than slowing it, because the blocker being removed is legal, not technical. That inverts the usual framing of security as friction.

**Not established:** whether any insurer today accepts any agent-audit badge as a condition of cover. The whole model hinges on that and it is currently a design requirement, not an observation. No named legal case backs the liability claim; it is reported as general knowledge. The glide-path scenario is hypothetical.

**Open item:** the cheap falsification test is one conversation with one underwriter. Ask what evidence about an agent would let them write or keep a policy. If the answer is "none yet, there is no standard", the badge business is premature and the standard work is the business.
