## Post 1: NotebookLM as a teaching machine, four podcasts described, zero in the queue

Claims under test: (a) a skill builds four kinds of interview-prep podcast; (b) NotebookLM is a general-purpose learning tool, and could teach a pilot theory syllabus of "eight or nine" exams; (c) a commenter's question about how the audio is generated and what it costs in usage.

### ⭐⭐⭐ The skill exists and its rule is stricter than the post

Five skills on the shelf on this node: interview prep (16,666 b), the podcast builder (39,056 b), the interview debrief (16,628 b), the NotebookLM skill (9,296 b), the YouTube publisher (28,486 b).

The hiring rule inside interview prep is stronger than the post describes: for hiring, the podcast is made **without asking Anton first**, and one interview takes **3 to 5 episodes** rather than one, because a single call exposes several gaps and each gap earns its own episode. Asking permission is defined as a violation of the rule; delivering one episode for an unfamiliar company is defined as executing it at a quarter.

So the described capability is real and under-described rather than over-described.

Reading those together: the production line works and has shipped 37 episodes without a single failure, and it is aimed at general learning. The interview application the post is about ran three times in two months. That is a stated capability with an unstated priority, not a broken one.

### ⚠️ The exam count resolves to nine, and it is one lookup

The EASA theory syllabus for a light aircraft licence covers **nine subjects**: air law and ATC procedures, operational procedures, meteorology, human performance, principles of flight, aircraft general knowledge, navigation, flight performance and planning, communications. Examined as roughly **120 multiple-choice questions** in total across subjects.

Worth naming because of what it says about tool choice: the post wonders whether it is eight or nine while proposing to build an audio course for it. The count is a single lookup; the syllabus is what needs the course. Those are different problems and only the second one is worth a podcast.

### ⭐⭐ The commenter's question has a two-part answer, and the second part changed in September

Question: is the audio generated by the built-in generator or by separate tools, and how does it eat usage?

First part: built-in. NotebookLM synthesises the audio itself; no external text-to-speech is in the path.

Second part: there is **no official per-day figure for 2026**. Since early September 2026 the quota model is **compute-based, refreshing every five hours up to a weekly ceiling**, rather than a fixed count of audio overviews per day. The per-day numbers still circulating in blog posts predate that change and contradict each other, ranging from three to ten overviews or from fifteen to thirty minutes of audio.

Which explains a design decision already in our own queue: it **rotates between two accounts**. That rotation exists because the bucket runs out, and it is the operational answer to the question - audio comes out of the account's shared compute budget, so a long bilingual series needs more than one account to finish.

### Open, in order of payoff

1. Name the priority out loud. 37 episodes shipped on general learning, 3 interview-prep calls in 60 days. Either the interview line is the priority and the queue should show it, or it is not and the post oversells it.
2. Look up the exam count rather than building a course to discover it. It is nine, the subjects are listed above, and the course is worth building for the syllabus rather than for the number.
3. Verify the syllabus against the authority that will examine him. The nine-subject list is from secondary sources, and the UK regulator runs its own alternative syllabus.
4. Record outcomes per invocation, not just invocations. Three interview-prep calls may have produced three episodes or fifteen; the counter cannot tell, which makes "used three times" weaker evidence than it looks.
5. Watch the compute ceiling rather than a daily count. The quota model changed in September; a queue sized against per-day limits is sized against a rule that no longer exists.

## Post 3: two layers of memory, and the question he says he cannot answer is already measured

Claims under test: (a) sessions should search previous sessions first, before verifying anything externally, even when not asked; (b) two layers of memory, recent on top, dig deeper only if the top layer misses; (c) his open question - how should the system decide whether to dig into old memory, and whether old memory is even wanted once it has gone stale; (d) his own prior-art question: is he the inventor of this idea or not.

### ⭐⭐⭐ Answer to (d) first: no, and the closest prior art is bi-temporal by name

| system | memory model | how it treats stale facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Zep / Graphiti** | temporal knowledge graph, **bi-temporal**: when the event happened and when it was ingested | old facts marked **superseded**, not buried |
| **Letta** | context window as an OS: in-context, core, archival tiers | agent self-edits its own memory |
| **Mem0** | vector + graph + key-value layer between LLM and app | add / update / delete, replaces stale entries |

Zep publishes 94.8% on Deep Memory Retrieval against 93.4% for MemGPT - their own benchmark, not independent. And the general advice in the field is stated as a rule: **invalidate, do not discard**, so past states stay reconstructible.

So the two-layer idea is not his, the bi-temporal axis is not his, and the tiering is not his. What is his, and is worth describing separately, is the **chain**: recall first, then decide whether the world may have moved, then verify externally, then hand back a merged answer - unprompted. The hurricane example in the post is that chain, and none of the three systems above does the verification leg.

### ⭐⭐⭐ Answer to (c): already measured here, and the answer is "demote, never filter"

The engine exists on the hub: `brain_temporal.py`, state in `turnstate.db`, holding wikilink edges and note facts on **two axes** - `valid_from`/`valid_to` for when a thing is true in the world, `observed_at` for when we saw it - plus `source_kind` machine or human. Deliberately the same columns as `memory_epistemic.py`, so two implementations of one time model cannot drift apart.

And the rule, with the measurement behind it:

> A filter showing only `valid_to IS NULL` is **forbidden**. Measured 16 September on a frozen gold set: of **184 questions, three had a correct answer that is itself closed** (superseded notes). Filtering by freshness would have zeroed those three by construction.

Knob `BRAIN_TEMPORAL`, default off.

So his open question - "is old memory needed, or is it stale and useless?" - has a measured answer on his own machines: **do not decide. Rank.** Push closed material down instead of out, because the right answer sometimes lives in the superseded note. That is also what the field recommends, independently.

### ⭐⭐ Answer to (a) and (b): both are built, and the two layers are two separate indexes

"The session searches sessions first, even when I did not ask" has been live since **31 August**: the recall entry point mixes in the top three past sessions on its own.

And the two layers are not one index with a date filter - they are **two indexes by design**:

| index | contents | purpose |
|---|---|---|
| curated essence (`_brain_e5`) | distilled notes, decisions, insights | the sharp `/ask` mind |
| **Chat Book** (`brain_sessions_index.py`) | exported session transcripts, **human sessions only** | find which past chat discussed a topic |

The separation is explicit in the engine's own docstring: the raw chat chatter must **never pollute** the curated essence index. Service and robot chats are filtered out before indexing; the two reindexers share the embedding model but hold separate locks so they can run side by side.

Which answers the post's design worry more precisely than the post states it. The question is not "top layer or deep layer" by age. It is **episodic or semantic by kind** - did this happen in a conversation, or is it a distilled fact - and age is a ranking signal inside each.

### ⭐⭐ The best comment is almost a description of the Chat Book

A commenter proposed a "StackOverflow for agents": a RAG over problem summaries and their solutions, votes on solutions, exposed as an MCP with search over solved cases and filters so sensitive data cannot leak. Their hypothesis: search saves tokens. Their framing of the real problem: *the question is not so much how to build it, but how to prove the value and earn from it.*

That is the Chat Book with two additions we do not have - **votes on solutions** and **an MCP surface for other people's agents**. The filter concern is already handled on our side by excluding service and robot sessions and keeping secrets out of the index, but "so sensitive data cannot leak" is the right thing to worry about if the surface is ever opened to anybody else.

Their last sentence is the sharpest line in the thread, and it applies to the whole post: the mechanism is not the hard part.

### Open, in order of payoff

1. Stop treating the dig-deeper question as open. It was answered on 16 September with a measurement: demote, never filter, because 3 of 184 gold answers are themselves closed.
2. Describe the verification leg, not the layers. The layers and the bi-temporal axis are prior art with published benchmarks; the recall-then-verify-then-merge chain, run unprompted, is what has no equivalent in Zep, Letta or Mem0.
3. Answer the commenter in public. A RAG over solved cases with votes, exposed as MCP, is a concrete design proposal on top of something we already run, and the "how to prove value" question is the one the whole series keeps avoiding.
4. Decide whether the Chat Book ever gets an outside surface. If it does, the leak filter is a prerequisite, not a later feature.
5. Do not order a deep research on "am I the inventor". The prior art is three named systems and one published benchmark, and it took one search.

⚠️ Instrument notes. The prior-art figures are vendor self-published - Zep's 94.8% and MemGPT's 93.4% come from Zep's own benchmark, and no independent head-to-head was found. The engine and index descriptions were read from source and from our own memory records, not exercised: the 16 September measurement is quoted from the record of that run, not re-run today. The temporal engine and its state live on the hub, so none of it was re-measured from this node. The commenter's "search saves tokens" is a hypothesis, not a measurement, and so is ours until someone counts.
